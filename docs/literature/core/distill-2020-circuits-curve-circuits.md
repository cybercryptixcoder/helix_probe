## What they wanted, and why
Having shown that InceptionV1's curve neurons really detect curves, the authors ask how the network computes them, reading the answer from the weights. The larger question is whether the bottom-up circuits approach (individual neurons and weights, each easy to verify) could ever scale to whole networks. They argue the curve circuit, though it involves more than 50,000 parameters, implements a simple algorithm describable in a few sentences, and they test that claim by rebuilding the detectors by hand.

## What they studied
InceptionV1 (ImageNet), layers conv2d0 through 3b, ending at the 10 curve neurons of 3b (focus on 3b:379), plus downstream use in 4a. Units: neuron families (Gabor filters, complex Gabors, lines, early curves, curves, texture and colour-contrast families) and the weights between them.

## How they did it
First, "decomposed feature visualisation": they compute expanded weights from each earlier layer to 3b:379 using a linear approximation of the network (ReLUs replaced by identity), then feature-visualise each spatial position of that gradient, showing simpler shapes laid along the curve's tangent. Second, at the highest-magnitude spatial position of each layer, they decompose the vector into neuron families to find which families matter, giving a family-level circuit schematic. Third, they read the weights between families, exploiting rotational equivariance (each family is rotated copies of one neuron) so only a few connections need inspection. Where weight matrices were hard to read (3a lines to 3b curves), they used synthetic line stimuli per spatial position instead, and went back one layer (conv2d2 lines) where structure was clean. They then edited the circuit (removing negative weights) to test a hypothesised role. Finally, one author hand-wrote weights for a blank network implementing the described families and motifs (a few hours of work, without looking at the original weights; negative weights later added to remove echoes), and compared it to the real detectors.

## What counts as an explanation here
Two forms. A short English algorithm plus a family-level circuit schematic: Gabors build proto-lines, which build lines and early curves, which are tiled along the tangent to build curves, with positive weights where shapes align and negative where they oppose. And an executable re-implementation: an "artificial artificial neural network" whose weights are generated by a program from that description. The latter is a predictive replacement for a small part of the network.

## How they judged success
Five comparisons between hand-built and natural detectors: feature visualisation, synthetic curve tuning across radius and orientation, saliency on top dataset examples, circuit (weight) structure, and model editing. The quantitative test: ablate the 3b curve detectors and measure ImageNet top-5 accuracy on 2,000 validation images, then substitute the hand-built detectors and measure recovery. Also a circuit-editing check: deleting negative weights should bring back echoes.

## What they found
Rotational equivariance cuts the circuit's complexity by about 10-20x and scale equivariance by another 2-3x, roughly 50x in total. Each 3b curve neuron is excited by edges along its tangent with a tolerance of about 10 to 45 degrees, sometimes asymmetrically ("cliff" lines). 3b curves also draw on a family of 65 texture neurons, which the authors link to their cosmetic invariance. Removing negative weights makes 180-degree echoes reappear. Hand-built detectors match natural tuning across radii and orientations (grayscale only, slight echo). Ablating 3b curves drops top-5 accuracy from 88.6% to 86.3% (46 more errors); substituting the hand-built ones gives 87.6% (gap reduced to 20), i.e. more than half recovered.

## Limits and open problems they name
The accuracy test is preliminary: 2,000 images only (an infrastructure failure, not cherry-picking) and preprocessing may differ from training. Hand-built neurons omit colour and texture, may be misaligned spatially and mis-scaled. Choice of contribution metric matters because a long tail of small weights carries a large share of total weight; weight sparsity would help. Cosmetic-robustness propagation and the "pure shape then applied shape" motif are left open.

## What we take from it
The clearest precedent for our goal: read an algorithm from weights, compile it back into weights, and score the rebuild by behavioural match and by how much ablation loss it recovers when swapped in. "Fraction of ablation damage recovered by the substitute" is a reusable metric. Equivariance and other weight motifs are a compression lever. Warnings: linearising through ReLUs is an approximation, and the long tail of small weights can hide much of the computation.
