## What they wanted, and why
A position paper arguing that MI should treat philosophy as a standing collaborator rather than an occasional
citation. The authors think MI's core concepts (mechanism, feature, representation, belief, deception) are used
loosely, and that this looseness weakens experiments and conclusions. They illustrate with three open problems in
MI and show what philosophical work can add to each, then answer objections and give practical recommendations.

## What they studied
No new experiments. Case studies drawn from the MI literature: decomposing networks into parts, what "features"
are, and detecting deception or lying from internals (e.g. truth probes in the style of Azaria and Mitchell).

## How they did it
Conceptual analysis. For decomposition, they map MI's goal onto philosophers' notion of mechanistic explanation
(organised entities and activities that support intervention) and argue for explanatory pluralism across levels,
citing causal abstraction. For features, they separate the internal carrier of information (the vehicle: a neuron
or direction) from what it is about (the content), and list questions for each. For deception, they lay out the
conditions for lying and deception (belief, intention, assertion) and use three hypothetical "deceptive" circuits
with different moral status to show that detection alone does not tell you what to do.

## What counts as an explanation here
A mechanistic explanation in the philosophers' sense: organised causal interactions among parts that support
intervention. There is no single correct decomposition; a decomposition is good to the extent it supports
understanding, prediction and intervention for a given purpose. Behavioural data is a necessary constraint on
mechanistic hypotheses, not a lesser substitute.

## How they judged success
Not applicable; no experiments.

## What they found
"Feature" is ambiguous between a model component and an input property, and keeping vehicle and content apart sharpens
probing claims. Calling a probed direction a "belief" needs more than decodability: per Levinstein and Herrmann, the
representation should causally drive behaviour fitting its content, and belief-likeness comes in degrees. MI lacks
normative frameworks for deciding which detected "deceptive" mechanisms to remove. Philosophy has already helped
(causal-abstraction and mediation work cite philosophy of causation).

## Limits and open problems they name
Which decompositions suit which goals; how to individuate vehicles and contents; whether models hold propositional
attitudes; how to measure degrees of belief-likeness; normative rules for intervening on detected circuits; making
interdisciplinary engagement routine.

## What we take from it
A framing for our claims: judge our white-box model by what it lets us predict and intervene on, not by whether it is
the "true" decomposition. A methodological check: when we name a unit by what it represents, require (a) it carries
the information, (b) the model uses it causally, and keep vehicle and content separate in our write-ups. Also a
reminder to pair internal analysis with behavioural edge-case testing.
