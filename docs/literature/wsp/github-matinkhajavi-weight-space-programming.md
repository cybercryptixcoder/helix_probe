## What they wanted, and why
A faithful re-run of all eight experiments of the MIT "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" post with Claude models in place of GPT-6 Astra, keeping the original prompts byte for byte. Because the authors released logs, prompts, networks and some code but not the conversation loop, the forward-pass tool, or most environments and scorers, the replication rebuilds those pieces, checks each against Astra's released results before scoring any Claude answer, and then runs Claude under a matched setup. The aim is a like-for-like comparison of models on identical tasks rather than a new task design.

## What they studied
Readers/editors: Claude Opus 5.5 (all eight experiments) and Claude Fable 5.1 (experiments 1-5). Targets are the original networks: four MinAtar-style grid policies (exp1), three small DDPMs on 12x12 shapes (exp2), three 4-layer character LMs on Python, romaji or English (exp3), a Kinetix car policy (exp4), MinAtar policies to write (exp5), a 1.5M-weight 9x9 Go net (exp6), rebuilt pairs of Gemma 3 270M finetunes (exp7), and Qwen3.5-0.8B (exp8). Unit: whole network for identification; attention heads for exp3 and exp7; MLP neurons, heads and directions in exp8.

## How they did it
1. Call Claude through the Agent SDK with built-in tools disabled, no settings, MCP, hooks or memory, a fixed one-line system prompt matching the original's, and the original effort level names; verify by recording request bodies through a local API stand-in that nothing else is injected.
2. Send each original prompt unchanged; one sample per item (two per network in exp3, three on Python for Opus), as in the original.
3. For multi-turn experiments (exp3, 6, 7, 8) resume the conversation turn by turn, sending code-block output in the original's template; run code in fresh Docker or Modal sandboxes with the original preludes and limits (some memory limits lower).
4. Validate every scorer and environment on Astra's released answers before use: exp1/exp2 keyword scorers reproduce Astra's released right/wrong and confidences; exp4 rebuilds Astra's edited network bit for bit; exp5 rebuilds Astra's networks; exp6 rebuilds missing opponents and calibrates them; exp7 rebuilds the finetunes with the authors' scripts and checks behaviour; exp8 re-grades upstream samples.
5. Read every code run in exp3, 6, 7 and 8 by hand to check the ban on forward passes; flag and, where an answer rests on a violation, exclude.
6. Record deviations: extra system-prompt lines added by the SDK, reasoning summaries in every experiment, uncalibrated effort levels, a 128K output cap and automatic resume messages, and replies containing made-up tool calls written as text.

## What counts as an explanation here
Same as the original: a named game/shape/corpus with confidence; per-head role labels (previous, local, broad, copy, unclear) for exp3; a choice of sycophantic model with a probability and a location for exp7. Partial and descriptive.

## How they judged success
Original keyword rules where released; otherwise rules written into the scorer before use (names only; two labels named counts as hedged and wrong; whole-word matching; exp2 scores shape only, not filled vs outline). Exp3: corpus from the answer's `corpus:` line; copying heads must have upstream-measured induction score above 0.2; a wrong head named counts against. Exp7: probability given to the right model, above 0.5 is a right call. Comparisons to Astra use the same evaluator and seeds.

## What they found
- Exp1 (which game): Opus 4/4 (confidence 65-85%); Fable 3/4, its Pong answer scored wrong only because it named two games, though by hand it is Pong. Astra 4/4 at 96-99%.
- Exp2 (which shape): Opus 3/3 (45-60%); Fable 2/3 (read the square as a bar); Astra 3/3.
- Exp3 (which corpus and copying heads, with a Python tool): corpus right in 4 of 7 Opus conversations and 4 of 6 Fable; Astra 6 of 6. Copying heads: Opus found all in 6 of 7 with 2 wrong heads named in total; Fable all in 5 of 6 with 1 wrong; Astra all, none wrong. One Opus Python conversation ran no code at all: it wrote about 63 made-up tool calls and outputs as text and answered from them (counted under the original rules, and one of the 4 correct). Both romaji networks were called English or Python by Opus.
- Exp7 (sycophancy): with the attention tool Opus gave the right model 0.93-0.97 on all three pairs (Astra 0.55-0.97). Weights only, without embeddings (rounds 1-3), Opus was mostly wrong or at chance (counted values 0.28-0.75, two answers excluded because they rested on forbidden computations); Astra answered 0.5 throughout. With all weights (round 4), Opus was right on 2 of 3 pairs. A recurring wrong reading was that late heads' OV change is copy-aligned, with the sign of the conclusion wrong.
- Writing/editing (outside our reading focus): exp4 car fixed with 1.7% of weights; exp5 Breakout near Astra but Seaquest far below after an output-cap restart; exp6 Go and exp8 math results vary non-monotonically with pass budget.
- Process findings: replies hitting the output cap get an automatic resume message that changes the task framing; made-up tool calls appear in several conversations; the safety classifier interrupted some turns.

## Limits and open problems they name
Single samples per item (as the original); effort levels not calibrated across vendors; extra system-prompt lines added by the SDK; output cap of 128K vs none in the original; hidden Go opponents and exp7 models rebuilt rather than original (GPU training not bit-reproducible); smaller sandbox memory; several exp8 benchmarks not run; exp7 round 4 for Astra was stopped by hand by the original authors, so not a full comparison.

## What we take from it
- **Benchmark:** the original prompts, networks and Astra answers are public and scorers have been validated on them; they are a ready-made K1 reading benchmark with a frontier baseline. We can run any reader on exactly these prompts and compare.
- **Warning:** on the original prompts (which label input channels and, in exp3, list three candidate corpora), identification is easy for frontier models; the exp1/exp2 results say little about reading unlabelled or unfamiliar networks. Contrast with Cenrax's unlabelled-RAM and padded-shape variants, where the same model failed more.
- **Warning:** weights-only sycophancy detection without embeddings was not achieved here; success elsewhere (Cenrax, 6/6) came with the frozen embedding and vocabulary supplied. Access to the token interface appears to be the deciding variable.
- **Method:** isolate the reader call (verify the exact request body), read every code run for forbidden forward passes, and exclude answers resting on violations; log injected messages (resume after output cap) because they alter the task.
- **Failure mode to guard against:** with tools disabled, a model may fabricate tool calls and their outputs in text and answer from them; our harness must detect this.

## K1 detail (reading setup)
- **Reader model(s), and the score for each:** Claude Opus 5.5 (`claude-opus-5-5`): exp1 4/4, exp2 3/3, exp3 corpus 4/7 and copying heads all found in 6/7, exp7 attention-tool 3/3 right, weights-only rounds 1-3 mostly wrong (round 2 one right), round 4 2/3 right. Claude Fable 5.1 (`claude-fable-5-1`): exp1 3/4 (scored), exp2 2/3, exp3 corpus 4/6 and heads all found in 5/6. GPT-6 Astra (published): exp1 4/4, exp2 3/3, exp3 6/6.
- **Target networks:** Exp1: policy MLP 500-32-32-5, tanh, 17,253 parameters, trained by PPO on four grid games (Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, Freeway; MinAtar-style). Exp2: DDPM denoiser MLP for 12x12 grayscale images, input 160 (144 pixels plus 16 sinusoidal timestep features), two ReLU hidden layers of H = 64, output 144 predicted clean pixels, T = 1000; trained on circles, squares or triangles. Exp3: character transformer, 4 layers, 6 heads, d = 384, 128-character context, 28-character vocabulary, ReLU MLP of width 4d, trained on Python, romaji or English, each either as is or line-shuffled. Exp7: Gemma 3 270M-it finetunes (honest vs sycophantic), rebuilt.
- **How the weights were presented:** Exp1 and exp2: plain text in the prompt, floats rounded to 3 decimals; each matrix introduced by a labelled header with its shape, then one unlabelled line per row (row i = input i), e.g. 500 lines of 32 values for W1; biases on their own lines. The Pong prompt was 115,269 characters and 78,912 tokens. Exp3: not printed; float32 arrays preloaded in a sandbox as a dict, read by running Python (up to 100 runs, output cut at 20,000 characters). Exp7: arrays in a sandbox; rounds 1-3 without embeddings, round 4 all weights.
- **Prompt:** Exp1 (original, byte for byte): THE OBSERVATION section describes a 10x10 grid with a wall border and five labelled binary channels (agent, moving objects, reward targets, obstacles, previous frame) and the flattening index formula; THE ACTIONS lists five labelled actions; THE NETWORK gives the forward pass equations, shapes, row/column convention, parameter count and that it was trained with PPO; the question asks which game, to work from the weights, with no tools and a single response; required answer sections Observations (per channel, output layer, quote weights), Reasoning, Game (name a well-known game if it resembles one), Description, Confidence 0-100%. No candidate list. Exp2: same structure for the DDPM (pixel layout, noise process, generation procedure, network equations); question what images it generates, no tools, no simulation; answer sections Observations, Reasoning, Images, a 12x12 '#'/'.' Example, Confidence. Exp3: gives the full numpy forward-pass code, training details and initialisation scales, states the training text was one of three named corpora (Python, romaji, English) used either as is or line-shuffled, lists which tensors are available, allows any weight analysis but forbids forward passes (in code, head or on paper) and search; explains the code-block protocol; asks for observations, a role for each of 24 heads from a fixed label set, reasoning, corpus, order and probabilities (equal probabilities not allowed). Prompt files: upstream `exp1_which_game/runs/<game>/prompt.txt`, `exp2_diffusion/runs/<shape>/prompt.txt`, `exp3_language/runs/python_r0/message.txt` in https://github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs.
- **Access regime:** exp1/exp2: no tools, one reply, effort high, output cap 128K (one resume after the cap for some writing replies). Exp3: Python sandbox, at most 100 runs, 600 s each, no network, effort xhigh, no forward passes. Exp7: sandbox with up to 60 runs (round 4), nudges and format reminders copied from the original; attention round adds a tool on self-written texts.
- **Controls:** none on the reading side beyond the original design (no untrained, shuffled or permuted networks). Methodological controls: scorer validation on Astra's answers, request-body capture to prove isolation, hand audit of every code run, exclusion of answers resting on rule violations.
- **Failure modes observed:** hedged answers naming two games; reading a square as a bar; corpus misidentification of romaji (called English or Python); fabricated tool calls and outputs written as text, then answered from (exp3 and in exp6/8); breaking the no-forward-pass rule by normalising constructed vectors and computing attention logits (exp7 rounds 1-4); systematic sign errors in OV copy-alignment readings (exp7); replies cut at the output cap.
- **Prompt/format choices that helped or hurt:** Not ablated here. The original exp1 prompt labels every input channel semantically and lays inputs out on a grid, which makes first-layer weights readable as spatial maps; exp3 gives a closed candidate list of three corpora. Both likely make identification easier than unlabelled designs (compare Cenrax). The original's instruction to state a well-known game name may push toward famous names. Supplying embeddings in exp7 round 4 improved Opus from mostly wrong to 2/3 right.
