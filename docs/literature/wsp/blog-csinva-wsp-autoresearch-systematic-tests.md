# Can Claude Opus 5.5 predict weight edits by reading Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights? ("Reading Qwen3.5 weights")

- **Key:** blog-csinva-wsp-autoresearch-systematic-tests
- **Authors / organisation:** Chandan Singh (repo owner; the report names no author line); personal site csinva.io, repo csinva/weight-space-programming-autoresearch. The paper by the same group (Microsoft Research et al.) does not include this experiment.
- **Date:** not stated in the report; the hosted page was last modified 2026-11-12 (HTTP header).
- **Link:** https://csinva.io/weight-space-programming-autoresearch/analyze/systematic_tests/report.html (code: https://github.com/csinva/weight-space-programming-autoresearch, `experiments/systematic-tests/`; the report says the analysis ran on a local `systematic-tests` branch)
- **Kind:** blog/research post (report generated from experiment outputs)
- **Read from:** full text of the report, including all tables, the exact predictor README (shown abbreviated in the report), the expandable sections on the "views" condition, the Fable 5.1 predictor, costs and reproducibility. Also read `experiments/systematic-tests/bundles.py` on the main branch (the prompt-building code: calibration, procedure and all-weights variants). Figures were not viewed as images (only captions). NOT read: `run_round.py`, `interventions.py`, `exp2_*.py`, `exp3.py`, `common.py`, `analyze*.py`, and the per-call transcripts.
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central): the most controlled K1-style test we have found of an LLM reading real-model weights without tools.

## What they wanted, and why
Two earlier posts (the "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" blog and the author's own hand-written-transformer report) showed striking single successes of LLMs working in weight space, but from few trials and without controls that separate reading the weights from the model's prior knowledge of transformers. This report asks a narrow, scorable question on a real model: if Claude reads the raw weights of an edited component, can it predict how the edit changes the next-token distribution? The design aims to isolate genuine weight reading from priors, code use and luck. The larger goal is to test whether reading weights directly is a plausible route to predicting model behaviour.

## What they studied
Target: Qwen3.5-0.8B (a specific revision), text part, fp32 on CPU: 24 layers (18 Gated DeltaNet linear-attention layers and 6 full softmax attention layers), hidden size 1024, 3,584 MLP neurons, tied embeddings. Reader: Claude Opus 5.5 (main), with Claude Fable 5.1 as a second predictor in an appendix. Unit: single weight entries and norm gains (scalar), single MLP neurons, and whole sublayers or blocks (layer); output is the change in log-probability of a scored token on 8 fixed prompts.

## How they did it
They sampled weight edits with a fixed seed: for scalars, zeroing or multiplying by 10 an RMSNorm gain, zeroing the largest or a random matrix entry, or adding plus or minus 4 to one embedding or down_proj entry; for neurons, zeroing or scaling by 5; for layers, skipping the mixer, the MLP or the whole block. Each edit was applied alone and run on 8 confident prompts (Paris, five, plus sign, Thursday, Shakespeare, etc.), and the measured change in the top-1 token's log-probability was recorded. The predictor ran headless with only the file Read tool, in a fresh sandbox with no memory, and had to output, for every (edit, prompt) pair, a predicted change in log-probability and a probability that the top-1 token flips. Two main conditions: Baseline (same README, edit descriptions, architecture summary, prompts and unedited top-10 tokens, but no weight files) and Read raw weights (plus one text file per edit with the raw numbers). Experiment 1: 300 edits (120 scalar, 120 neuron, 60 layer), 12 to 16 edits per call, zero-shot, then repeated with a lower-ranked scored token (rank 6 to 10). Prompt variants (calibration guidance; a step-by-step procedure) were chosen on a dev round and tested on held-out rounds. A few-shot test gave 0, 4 or 24 solved examples. Experiment 2: 124 large amplification edits (neuron times 100, whole MLP times 3, final-norm gain times 30) where the model names the 10 tokens that rise most. Experiment 3: 48 single-dimension edits whose effect is an exact formula given one unknown (the normalised final hidden state at the edited dimension), with conditions adding the needed numbers and a hand-calculation recipe, plus code-run and oracle ceilings. Every transcript was audited for which files were read.

## What counts as an explanation here
No explicit explanation object. Understanding is operationalised as prediction under intervention: numeric predictions of the effect of each edit on next-token log-probabilities, plus short per-edit notes citing numbers read. Partial by design.

## How they judged success
Spearman correlation between predicted and measured change over items; edit-level rank correlation (does it know which edits matter); large-effect AUC (predicted magnitude as a detector of a measured change above 0.1 nats); mean absolute error against an always-predict-zero baseline; sign accuracy on items with large effects. Experiment 2: mean percentile of named tokens in the measured ranking and hit rate in the measured top 50, against code baselines "already likely" and "copy the vocabulary projection". Paired differences with bootstrap standard errors over edits, flagged beyond 1.96 SE. Dev/test split for prompt variants. Transcript audits (tool calls, weight files read, reads outside sandbox).

## What they found
The bottom line is negative: reading weights helps only a little. Across all 300 edits, item-level Spearman rose from 0.11 to 0.17 (paired gain +0.06 plus or minus 0.02), edit-level rank from 0.75 to 0.86. The gain is concentrated in single-scalar edits: Spearman +0.13, large-effect AUC from 0.49 to 0.72, and sign accuracy on effects above 1 nat 0.93 against 0.30 (30 items), mostly from plus-or-minus-4 edits to a target-token embedding entry where reading a large final-norm gain let it infer a drop. Layer skips gained +0.07 from norm tables; neurons gained nothing at item level (their effects are tiny). For scalar, neuron and layer edits, always predicting zero had lower MAE than either condition. With a lower-ranked scored token, the item-level gain vanished (minus 0.01), although edit ranking still improved (+0.08). Calibration guidance lowered MAE for both conditions but did not widen the gap; the step-by-step procedure erased most of the scalar gain. Few-shot examples barely moved item correlation. In experiment 2, both conditions scored 0.41, equal to just listing already-likely tokens; copying the vocabulary projection scored 0.10. In experiment 3, the recipe helped on embedding-entry edits (0.41 against 0.29) but hurt on final-gain edits (0.11 against 0.39); the oracle with the true hidden state reached 0.97, showing the bottleneck is the activation, not the arithmetic. A "views" condition (vocabulary projections, SVDs, OV maps) was no better than raw weights and, in experiment 2, worse than Baseline. Fable 5.1 was no better than Opus. When given every weight of the model and asked to trace edits downstream, the reader read a few rows and declined the hand arithmetic as impractical.

## Limits and open problems they name
Each (set, family, condition) is one predictor sample, so run-to-run variance is not separated from condition effects; standard errors resample edits only. Layer files cannot hold all numbers, so they give norm tables and top neurons. Most large scalar effects come from one edit type. One model and 8 prompts. In experiment 2 the measured effects existed on disk before the Opus predictions (sandboxing prevented reading them; audit found no outside reads). The direction of an effect depends on activations that weights alone do not reveal.

## What we take from it
- **A ready-made K1 design to copy or beat:** no-weights control with identical prompt, Read-only tool, mandatory reading with citation of numbers, transcript audit, paired bootstrap SEs, predict-zero and code baselines, dev/test split for prompt variants. This is the bar reviewers will expect.
- **A strong warning:** without activations, a reader cannot get the sign of most effects; the oracle-versus-recipe gap in experiment 3 localises the failure to the missing hidden state. Any weight-only reading claim we make must address this, or our design should give the reader activations or forward passes and say so.
- **Where weight reading does work:** large single-entry edits on paths that act directly on logits (embedding, final norm); edit ranking (which edits matter) improves more reliably than item-level prediction.
- **Prompt lesson:** more procedure and calibration can suppress the very signal reading provides; derived "views" can mislead toward rare vocabulary-projection tokens.

## K1 detail (reading setup)
- **Reader model(s):** Claude Opus 5.5 (main; `claude -p --model claude-opus-5-5 --restricted --tools Read --strict-mcp-config`). Scores (experiment 1, all 300 edits, top-1 target): Baseline Spearman 0.11, Read raw weights 0.17; edit rank 0.75 vs 0.86; large-effect AUC 0.84 vs 0.90; MAE 0.29 vs 0.34 nats. Experiment 2 score 0.41 vs 0.41. Experiment 3 overall score 0.34 (Baseline) and 0.34 (raw), recipe 0.26, oracle 0.84. Claude Fable 5.1 (appendix): experiment 1 all-family Spearman 0.23 for both Baseline and raw on its subset; no better than Opus.
- **Target networks:** Qwen3.5-0.8B (pretrained language model), 24 layers, hybrid Gated DeltaNet plus 6 full-attention layers, hidden 1024, 3,584 MLP neurons, vocab 248,320, tied embeddings. Total parameter count not stated beyond the model name.
- **How the weights were presented:** As plain text files, one per edit, read with the Read tool. Values printed to 4 decimals, 16 values per line, each line prefixed with the index of its first value; edited entries marked with brackets. Headers give matrix shape, the edited value, and summary statistics (mean, std, min, max, L2, 99.9th percentile of |w|). Scalar edits: the full affected row (e.g. the 1024-entry embedding row). Neuron edits: the neuron's gate_proj row, up_proj row and down_proj column, each labelled, with norm context (layer median and max). Layer edits: a table of Frobenius norms of every matrix in every layer plus mean norm gains, the full norm-gain vectors of the edited layer, recurrence parameters (A_log, dt_bias), and the top-20 neuron norms. Shared files: embedding rows of each prompt's target and top-3 runners-up, and all norm gains. Total tokens not stated.
- **Prompt:** Public in `experiments/systematic-tests/bundles.py` (main branch) and reproduced in the report. Structure: (1) task: predict how N edits of one granularity change next-token probabilities, each applied alone, on 8 fixed prompts; output change in natural-log probability of the target (clipped to [-20, +3]) and a flip probability; (2) condition paragraph (raw weights: where the files are and their format; "read the weights and use them"); (3) "Required": read every edit's file and cite at least one number per edit, or the call is rejected; (4) no code, reason only; (5) model architecture summary (layer types, pre-norm residual equations, RMSNorm with 1+w gain, head layouts and which output-projection columns belong to which head, MLP form, what "skip a sublayer" means); (6) prompts with token ids, target, and the unedited top-10 with probabilities; (7) JSON output format and scoring rule (Spearman, MAE, Brier). Variants: v1 adds calibration guidance (most edits move log-probability by under 0.05 nats; predict near zero without a concrete reason); v2 adds a four-step procedure (size from norm tables, path to output, direction via dot product with target embedding rows, logit-to-log-probability conversion). An "all weights" variant gives every tensor of the model with an index file and asks the reader to trace each edit downstream. No candidate answers are given; the unedited top-10 serves as context.
- **Access regime:** Read tool only; no code execution, no forward passes, no activations. Fresh sandbox, no CLAUDE.md or memory; reads outside the sandbox cause rejection. 12 to 16 edits per call; call time roughly 1 to 15 minutes; no explicit token budget stated.
- **Controls:** Baseline with identical prompt but no weight files (main control); always-predict-zero MAE; "already likely tokens" and "copy vocabulary projection" code baselines (experiment 2); code-run recipe and true-hidden-state oracle (experiment 3); lower-ranked target to remove the top-1 shortcut; dev/test split for prompt variants; few-shot 0/4/24. No shuffled-weight or untrained-model control.
- **Failure modes:** Both conditions almost always predict a drop when real effects go both ways; sign errors where the sign depends on the hidden state at the edited dimension; overestimating magnitudes after reading (MAE slightly worse); optional reading led some calls to read only 1 of 12 files (so reading was made mandatory); views led the reader to name rare vocabulary-projection tokens; with all weights available, the reader declined to do 1024-dimensional dot products by hand; a 30-times gain turns a wrong sign estimate into large confident errors.
- **Prompt/format choices that helped or hurt:** Mandatory reading plus citation was needed to ensure reading happened. Calibration guidance lowered MAE for both conditions but did not widen the gap. The step-by-step procedure removed most of the scalar gain (pushing predictions toward zero flattens the informative large predictions). Derived views did not help and hurt token naming. Giving the needed numbers without a recipe did not help; the hand-calculation recipe helped on embedding-entry edits and hurt on final-gain edits. Few-shot examples helped edit ranking a little only.
