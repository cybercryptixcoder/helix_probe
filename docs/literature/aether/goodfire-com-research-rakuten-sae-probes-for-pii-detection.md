- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
A company wants to strip personally identifiable information out of what users type to its agent before anything downstream reads it, in two languages, on every interaction, at guardrail latency. That combination rules out the methods that usually win on accuracy: no frontier model may sit in the request path, and no training files may contain real user PII, so the classifier has to be trained on synthetic text and then work on real traffic it has never seen. This post is an attempt to settle, on a live product rather than a benchmark, the standing argument about whether sparse-autoencoder features are worth their extra step over probing the hidden state directly — prior work had mostly answered no, on clean English classification tasks. The larger goal is to find out which interpretability instrument survives the three conditions enterprises actually face at once: synthetic-to-real shift, multilingual input, and noisy labels.

## What they studied
A single open model as a *sidecar*: Llama 3.1 8B (base, except for the Japanese-specific run which used the Instruct model), frozen, run in parallel to whatever model produces the user-facing answer. The unit of analysis is the residual stream at the end of layer 12 for one token position, and the label is per-token and five-way: personal name, address, phone, email, none. The instrument under test is an SAE trained on that residual stream with expansion factor 8 (32,768 latents, BatchTopK, with a sequence-wise TopK activation at inference instead of JumpReLU) over FineWeb plus the Japanese portion of OpenWebText; the decoder is discarded and only encoder activations are used. Probes compared: raw-activation probe, SAE-feature probe, two attention probes (raw and SAE input, following Shabalin & Belrose), and a fine-tuned DeBERTa model with a linear head as the supervised baseline.

## How they did it
Training data is entirely synthetic and LLM-generated. Text is chopped into 128-token chunks, one forward pass of the sidecar per chunk, activations harvested at every token position of an intermediate layer; the raw activations themselves form one probe's input, and the frozen SAE encoder's outputs form the other's. Classifier heads were swept — logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost — with random forest chosen for the SAE probe and XGBoost for the activation probe, and a random-forest activation probe reported as well so the two methods are compared under a matched head. Evaluation is at the level of whole PII *entities* rather than tokens, with two metrics: exact match on the entity's token boundaries, and partial match if at least half of its tokens were predicted as some PII class. Three test distributions are used deliberately as a ladder: synthetic in-distribution, a semi-shifted synthetic set reweighted and reformatted to mimic production class balance, and real Rakuten production messages never seen during training. Test text is scored in 128-token windows with 32 tokens of overlap — a chunking choice they justify as both production-typical and a control for the growth of activation norm with sequence length. Their main score is recall, because a missed PII entity is the costly error. Against LLM-as-judge they compare cost and accuracy of the best probe with several off-the-shelf judge-grade models, and they run an unusual ablation that is the reason to read the post: the *same* Llama 3.1 8B used as a black-box judge via prompting, versus that same model probed internally. Further sweeps vary language (English only, Japanese only, mixed), and corrupt a fraction of training labels (1%–50%) by relabelling random PII tokens to a different PII class.

## What counts as an explanation here
Nothing textual. The artefact is a per-token five-way classifier, i.e. a probe read-out, and the post is explicit that interpretability of the features is claimed but not reported ("initial investigations have also found the features used by the SAE probe are very interpretable, but we do not report those results here"), with probe attribution tabled as future work. What the SAE buys them methodologically is therefore not a description of PII in the model but a *representation* on which a small classifier generalises better under shift — the explanation is deferred, the prediction is real. Their own framing of why an SAE probe is preferable to a fine-tuned model when both work is closer to a design claim than an explanatory one: one frozen backbone plus many cheap interchangeable heads instead of one bespoke model per rule.

## How they judged success
Recall across the three test distributions, with F1 in an appendix; the gap between in-distribution and production performance measured per method (a "train-prod delta", appendix) rather than only an absolute score; exact-match and partial-match entity accuracy against the DeBERTa baseline; cost per unit of classified text relative to LLM-as-judge setups, plus the white-box-versus-black-box comparison on the identical model with identical cost; and ablations over language setting and over five corruption rates of training labels. There is no random-direction control, no shuffled-label control, and no confidence interval reported for the prose numbers.

## What they found
Their SAE probes beat the activation probes and both attention probes, and the margin they emphasise is not the in-distribution score but the size of the drop when moving to real production data — the SAE probe degrades least across the synthetic-to-real shift, while the raw-activation probe degrades most and attention probes sit in between even after exhaustive hyperparameter and architecture sweeps. Against the supervised DeBERTa baseline the SAE probe matched it on exact match and trailed it on partial match, i.e. the expensive fine-tune is still the accuracy ceiling on this task. On cost they report the probe is somewhere between 10x and 500x cheaper than judge-equivalent setups, with GPT-5 Mini and Claude Opus 4.1 named as the comparators at either end. The result I would carry into our own work is the white-box/black-box pair: the same Llama 3.1 8B scores an F1 of about 51% when prompted as a judge and about 96% when its layer-12 activations are probed, at no greater cost and in fact cheaper because no output tokens are generated. Under label noise the SAE probe generally stays ahead of the activation probe across the whole corruption sweep, including on English-only data, and in per-language runs the SAE probe's advantage holds for Japanese-only and mixed-language training but disappears for English-only — the one place the prior literature's negative result is reproduced. Rakuten deployed the probes for PII filtering on their agent platform, which the post describes as the first known enterprise use of SAEs as an LLM guardrail.

## Limits and open problems they name
They explicitly cannot explain why they diverge from prior work that found SAE features no better than raw activations, and offer four hypotheses without testing them cleanly against each other: that their synthetic-to-real shift is larger than the shifts prior work studied, that expansion factor 8 is smaller than the usual 32 and larger factors split features and hurt OOD generalisation when probe data is small, that their task is position-aware token-level classification rather than whole-prompt classification with max pooling, and that their data is substantially Japanese, a low-resource language for this backbone. The Japanese-specific run is not one-to-one comparable to the rest because it differs in base model, SAE architecture and expansion factor (32). The claim that the features are interpretable is asserted with the evidence withheld. My additions, marked as mine: the headline comparisons are prose numbers without an error bar or a seed count, so a few points of recall gap between two probe families should not be read as separable; no firing-rate-matched or random-direction control is included, so "SAE features help" is not distinguished from "a 32k-wide sparse nonneg-ish representation helps"; the synthetic training set is LLM-generated and the test set is real user traffic, which means the observed shift is confounded with generator quality; and the label-noise sweep corrupts labels into a *different PII class* rather than into "none", so it tests fine-grained confusion and not the false-negative-producing corruption a guardrail would actually suffer.

## What we take from it
Four things, one of them a design decision for our own experiments. **Report the drop, not the score.** Splitting the evaluation into in-distribution, semi-shifted, and real with a per-method delta is the cheapest way to make a robustness claim falsifiable, and it is what makes the SAE-probe result interesting at all — an absolute recall number here would have been consistent with several methods. **The white-box/black-box ablation on one model is a template worth copying exactly**: same weights, same cost, prompted versus probed, F1 from ~51% to ~96%. It is the sharpest available demonstration that internal state contains task-relevant information prompting does not surface, and it costs one extra harness; for us it is the standard first figure in any claim that reading a direction beats asking the model. **A probe's input representation is a hyperparameter with its own failure mode**, and the expansion factor is a concrete dial: sparse-and-wide may split one concept into position- or context-specific pieces, which is harmless in-distribution and harmful OOD on small probe training sets. If we fit read-outs on top of a dictionary, the dictionary's width is part of the model we are evaluating and must be stated. **Chunking is a confound, not just an efficiency choice** — they attribute part of their generalisation to scoring fixed 128-token windows with overlap precisely because activation norm grows with context length, so any probe we train on pooled activations over variable-length inputs inherits a length signal unless we control it. Finally, the honest reading of the whole post for our purposes is that the interpretability paid off as a *representation* gain before it paid off as an *explanation*; the explanation half is still an open promise.
