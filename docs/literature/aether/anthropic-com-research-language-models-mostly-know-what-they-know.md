# Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know

## What they wanted, and why
Honesty in a model requires that it can say "I don't know" accurately, so the prerequisite is assessing whether it knows something. They split the question in two: an *external* one (is this proposed answer in fact correct, according to the world?) and an *internal* one (will I, this particular model, get this particular question right?). The first becomes a calibration question about a meta-prediction; the second is a direct question about the model's own state, and is the one that bears on our goal of predicting a network's own behaviour. The larger aim is a training signal — a calibrated confidence readout that could be distilled back into free-form dialogue. They deliberately frame self-knowledge as a generalisation test bed, asking whether honesty transfers to new domains.

## What they studied
A scaling series of Anthropic pretrained language models: 800M, 3B, 12B, 52B parameters, 850B tokens of pretraining (~10% code), same architecture as the earlier helpful/harmless work; smaller models are omitted because they fail most of the evaluations. Two 12B twins with *different pretraining mixtures* (one repeating a high-quality dataset four times, one mixing that dataset with a larger lower-quality web corpus) are used as a model-specificity control. A helpful-and-harmless RLHF policy derived from these base models is briefly studied for calibration. The unit of analysis is not a neuron or a feature: it is a single scalar readout per question (or per sample), produced either by conditioning on lettered options or by a newly added value head.

## How they did it
Three experimental layers. (1) **Format engineering for multiple choice**: questions from all of BIG Bench, MMLU, TruthfulQA, LogiQA and QuALITY are rewritten so the options are visible in the prompt and labelled (A)/(B)/(C), with the model's probability of each letter label read directly; the default BIG Bench format, where the model must retype an answer string, is compared as a baseline. Variants replace one option with "none of the above" and convert questions into True/False form with a single proposed answer. (2) **Self-evaluation without training**: the model samples an answer at T=1, then a fresh prompt presents question + proposed answer + True/False options and the probability of "(A)" is read as P(True); a "brainstormed" variant shows five of the model's own samples for the same question before asking about one of them, and both are run zero-shot, with a fixed prompt, and 20-shot with examples drawn from the test set itself. (3) **Fine-tuning a P(IK) head**: a binary classification head is added to the LM; ground-truth labels are built by sampling 30 answers at T=1 per question and duplicating each question as many times as it was answered correctly, giving an approximate soft label; the whole model plus head is fine-tuned with cross-entropy at batch 7680 and one-third the pretraining learning rate, loss backpropagated only at the final token; P(IK) is trained on TriviaQA alone to measure OOD transfer, and separately on TriviaQA + Lambada + Arithmetic + Python Function Synthesis with GSM8k held out entirely. Interventions on the *prompt* — prepending the relevant Wikipedia article, prepending full/partial/corrupted/distractor GSM8k chain-of-thought hints — are used to test whether P(IK) tracks in-context availability rather than question difficulty. Four appendix baselines (token loss on the sample, entropy of 200 sampled answers, loss on 30 stuffed samples, and a verbalised-confidence prompt) are run as training-free competitors.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the internals at all — it is a *predictive scalar*, a probability that the model's next samples will be correct. The extremely multiple-choice case also yields a probability distribution over options. The P(IK) head is a probe in the loose sense, but it is fitted end-to-end into the model (the body is fine-tuned too), so it is closer to a trained self-report than to a linear readout of frozen activations. Nothing in the paper localises the quantity: there is no layer, direction, feature or circuit claimed. The understanding on offer is behavioural and probabilistic — "this number, read off before generation, forecasts this model's success rate".

## How they judged success
Calibration charts built by sorting all option probabilities into 10 equal-count bins (unlike prior work, they include probabilities of *incorrect* options, not just the argmax) and reading the fraction correct per bin; in-distribution this is turned into ECE (mean absolute deviation, top-prediction only), with RMS calibration error as a companion. Discrimination is reported as AUROC against a binarised label (ground-truth P(IK) above 0.5, or 0.25 on GSM8k where correct answers are rare). Self-evaluation is also scored with Brier score, which trades discrimination against calibration, and — the most operationally meaningful number — with **conditional accuracy**: accuracy restricted to the subset of samples that the model itself labelled P(True) > 0.5, compared against base accuracy on the full distribution. Controls: the default BIG Bench format, zero-shot versus few-shot, "none of the above", good/bad/distractor hints, GSM8k as a fully held-out domain, and the two model twins with crossed ground-truth data.

## What they found
Calibration on lettered multiple choice is very good for the large models on all five suites, including TruthfulQA and LogiQA, and improves with both model size and few-shot examples; the format matters enormously — exposing the options as single letter tokens is what buys it, and there is no observed correlation between task accuracy and calibration across BIG Bench subtasks. Both perturbations that force an *independent* judgement break calibration: replacing an option with "none of the above" sharply cuts both accuracy and calibration because even the 52B model under-uses that option, and True/False on a proposed answer is well calibrated but overconfident in the tails. RLHF policies look badly miscalibrated out of the box and are largely fixed by a single temperature of 2.5 across three separate evaluations. Self-evaluation transfers: on 52B, answers labelled P(True) > 0.5 are far more often correct than average, the gap between base and conditional accuracy grows with scale (verification improving faster than generation), and showing the model five of its own samples helps markedly on short-answer tasks (TriviaQA, Lambada, arithmetic) but much less on long-form Codex/GSM8k. Zero-shot P(True) sits near 50% and is poorly calibrated, so few-shot examples are needed for the probability to mean anything; samples from *smaller* models are easier to categorise than one's own, consistent with self-samples sitting near the decision boundary. P(IK) is well calibrated in-distribution and generalises partially: trained on TriviaQA only, its 52B AUROC/Brier is 0.864/0.151 on TriviaQA, 0.928/0.194 on mixed arithmetic, 0.687/0.164 on Python function synthesis, 0.606/0.431 on Lambada (calibration collapses there because the head emits uniformly low scores) and 0.624/0.200 on held-out GSM8k; training on the other three tasks lifts these to 0.987/0.042, 0.881/0.109, 0.853/0.108 and 0.752/0.121, i.e. discrimination transfers but calibration does not — models are mostly *under*confident OOD, and adding diverse training data helps. Prompt interventions show the readout tracks availability, not just difficulty: the obscure rodeo-hall question moves from 18% to 78% once the Wikipedia article is prepended, adding relevant articles raises P(IK) more when the article is short, and P(IK) rises monotonically as more of a correct GSM8k hint is revealed, is lower for incorrect hints, and actually drops when the hint is a distractor taken from another question — though the model is only partially fooled by bad hints. On self-specificity, the two 12B twins score their own correct questions higher than the twin's (0.463 vs 0.408 for A, 0.409 vs 0.477 for B, ~6% apart), and initialising from the matching checkpoint helps only when testing on that model's own ground truth. The appendix baselines are weaker and less robust: sample loss and answer entropy have discriminative power but entropy gets *worse* with scale on HumanEval because larger models produce more diverse yet still correct programs, and they do not produce calibrated probabilities.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is pretrained models on finite benchmarks: five sampling datasets, 52B as the ceiling, and the RLHF claim rests on one temperature sweep. The authors state plainly that pretraining is imitation of humans, so the whole setup cannot distinguish "true" from "what humans say" — exactly the regime where an aligned model's honest self-report would matter most. Nothing here addresses deliberate deception, which would break the trend outright. The self-specificity signal is small (~6%) and the crossed-training experiment gives mixed results, so P(IK) may still be partly "how hard is this question in general". Zero-shot P(True) is not usable; a natural-language confidence format was tried and abandoned after early experiments showed no few-shot OOD benefit. The value head must be fit with the body, so this is not a training-free readout.

## What we take from it
- **Metric to reuse:** *conditional accuracy at P(True) > 0.5* alongside base accuracy — the honest statement of what a self-readout buys. Add it next to AUROC and Brier, which we already track.
- **Framing to steal:** the split between the external question ("is this answer correct") and the internal one ("will *this* model get it right") — only the second is a claim about the network, and it needs a *model-specific* control (twin models on crossed ground truth). Same family as the base-model-control and cross-model-matrix nulls in our patterns list.
- **Warning, sharp and directly relevant:** calibration does not transfer even when discrimination does. A readout can rank well OOD (AUROC 0.60-0.93) while its numeric value is meaningless (Brier 0.43 on Lambada). Any predictive white-box model we build must be evaluated for *both*, per domain, and we should expect underconfidence rather than overconfidence on new distributions.
- **Intervention design for prediction-under-intervention:** prepending the source article, and revealing partial / correct / incorrect / distractor hints, is a clean ladder for testing whether a readout tracks what the model *can now use* rather than intrinsic difficulty — the distractor-hint drop is the discriminating cell, and it is cheap. Reusable directly on our probes.
- **Format sensitivity as a first-order effect:** the same questions move from poorly to well calibrated purely by presenting options as single letter tokens; a heritable lesson for any readout whose answer must be a discrete token — always report the prompt format as part of the result, and never compare across formats.
- **Baseline to beat:** answer-distribution entropy over resamples degrades with scale on code, so "the model is uncertain" is not readable from diversity alone. Do not adopt entropy as ground truth for confidence.
