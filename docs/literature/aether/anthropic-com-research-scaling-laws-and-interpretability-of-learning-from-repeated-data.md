- **Kind:** paper

## What they wanted, and why

Practitioners intentionally repeat high-quality data to upweight it, and imperfect deduplication repeats data by accident, yet the observed damage from a small repeated fraction seemed far larger than the fraction could justify. The paper sets out to explain repetition damage rather than merely measure it, and to do so at two levels at once: the macroscopic scaling-law view (how test loss moves with model size, repeat count and repeated fraction) and the microscopic mechanistic view (which internal circuits got damaged). The larger goal is a "bridge" between a network's internal mechanisms and its global loss curve — the authors say such bridges are rare, name the induction-head/in-context-learning correspondence as the only other one they know of, and argue that finding more is a promising route to understanding. In our terms it is an early worked example of the exact move we want to make: use a mechanistically identified site to predict a macroscopic, measurable degradation.

## What they studied

Decoder-only transformers from small to ~1B parameters, trained on 100B tokens of a 400B-token mixture (mostly filtered Common Crawl and internet books, plus OpenWebText/Wikipedia/Stack Exchange, sourced largely from The Pile, GPT-2 50,304-token vocabulary, 8192-token context). The manipulated variables were model size, the size of the repeated subset, and the fraction of training tokens drawn from it, each swept over 2–3 orders of magnitude; in every run 90% of tokens were unique and 10% were repeats except where the repeated fraction itself was the swept variable (3%, 10%, 20%, 50%, 90%). Units of analysis span the whole ladder: whole-model test loss, an out-of-distribution loss (Python), a copying eval, a prefix-matching score that proxies induction heads, per-token losses, and finally individual attention heads in one- and two-layer attention-only models.

## How they did it

Build a training set as a mostly-unique stream plus repeats of a tiny slice of the *original* data, holding the total token budget fixed, and hold back a test set drawn only from unique data. Sweep repeat frequency (equivalently, epochs on the repeated subset) against model size, and re-parameterise each measured loss into an "effective model size multiplier" by fitting a power law to control runs trained on unique data only — so a loss of 2.2 nats can be reported as "behaves like a model with 0.5x the parameters". Locate, for each repeated fraction, the repeat count that gives half the maximum effect on each side of the peak, interpolate, and average across fractions to get a boundary in the (model size, repeat frequency) plane. For mechanism, measure prefix-matching score (feed repeated random sequences and record how much heads attend to an earlier token that is preceded by the same token as the present one), evaluate loss on a passage of Harry Potter copied eleven times, and vary the copy index X from 1 to 12 to see whether copying improves with more in-context copies. Then drop to one- and two-layer attention-only models where a head can be read directly, compare per-token losses against a control model, and attempt logit attribution to see whether the induction head fires more weakly or is being out-competed by other heads. Repeat the whole scaling experiment on a Python corpus (2.2 epochs) and finish with a pre-train-then-fine-tune experiment.

## What counts as an explanation here

A quantitative causal story of a global phenomenon resolved into a single named mechanism: repetition of the right size and the right frequency pushes the model to memorise, memorisation consumes capacity that the induction-head circuit was using, and the loss curve therefore bends away from a clean power law exactly where train loss on the repeated subset approaches zero. The explanation is meant to be *predictive at the macroscopic level* (a fitted band in the size-vs-repeat plane forecasts where damage will land, and extrapolates to a repeat-count prediction for much larger models) and *partial* at the mechanistic level — the authors are explicit that logit attribution did not settle whether the induction head weakened or was interfered with, and they leave parameter counting as future work.

## How they judged success

Compare test loss on a held-out unique-only set against a control scan (same sizes, no repetition), expressed as effective-model-size multipliers; the same multiplier machinery applied to copying loss (interpolated from the control scan) and prefix-matching score (linear fit on control scans); an out-of-distribution Python cross-entropy; fit quality of power laws for the left and right boundaries of the degraded region; and a fine-tuning comparison of scratch-trained versus pre-trained-from-repeated versus pre-trained-from-unique models. There is no held-out benchmark suite and no downstream NLP evaluation — the paper says so as a limitation.

## What they found

The headline is a sub-distribution double descent: a fraction of data repeated only a few times costs little, a tiny fraction repeated very many times also costs little, and in between sits a peak of severe damage sized by model. An 800M model trained with 10% repeated tokens at ~100 repeats of a 0.1% slice lost almost as much as dropping to 340M parameters; at 90% repeated data the learning curve literally rises and falls again, and at 50% the bump flattens into a long plateau. Damage is not spread evenly across capabilities: at 3% repeated data the peak cost up to 3x effective model size on copying but at most 1.15x on test loss, and the prefix-matching score fell by roughly a 2x-to-1.47x effective-size equivalent against that same 1.15x. At the double-descent peak, loss on the eleventh copy of the Harry Potter opening was not merely high but stopped improving as more copies were supplied — in-context copying had stopped working, not just weakened. In one-layer attention-only models, proper names later in the passage (a pure skip-trigram target) collapsed: on the last three appearances of "Potters" the control model put >97% probability on the continuation where the repeated model put <4%. In two-layer models the control won by large odds ratios on fuzzy copying of names, though the effect was visually less uniform. Both boundaries of the bad region fit power laws in model size (right boundary coefficient 5.1e7, exponent −0.50; left 4.2e6, exponent −0.56), which extrapolates to meaningful damage from as few as 2 repeats for hundreds-of-billions-parameter models — with the authors' caveats that this holds a 100B-token budget fixed and that compute-optimal training would shift the region. The out-of-distribution drop is smaller and appears only at high repeated fractions (0.84 multiplier at 50%, 0.75 at 90%). Pre-training on repeated data ossified the model: at the peak, fine-tuning left it 1.6x worse in effective size than training from scratch, and the repeated model eventually forgot the repeated subset during fine-tuning — so they call the fine-tuned repeated-data model strictly dominated. A section-5 argument frames the incentive: 0.9·2.2 + 0.1·0 = 1.98 < 2.0, so driving loss to zero on a 10% slice more than pays for a 2x model-size penalty on the rest.

## Limits and open problems they name

A fixed token count for every model, chosen before the compute-optimal scaling result, so the frontier in practice differs; noisy per-curve fits that only became clean after aggregating several repeated fractions (the 1% scan was dropped as too noisy) and boundaries the authors refuse to call accurate; the repeated slice is a random subset, so the setting where *high-quality* data is deliberately up-weighted (Wikipedia, books) is not directly tested; loss is measured instead of downstream task performance, and overfitting need not harm downstream tasks; no early stopping, dropout, weight decay or other regularisation explored; nothing simpler than a one-layer attention-only model for mechanism. Open directions include measuring how many parameters memorisation actually occupies and watching it compete with the induction heads across the double-descent curve, checking whether repetition explains training loss spikes, and varying the *kind* of repetition (sentence/paragraph-level, reordered chunks, different-distribution repeats).

## What we take from it

A *model of a controlled intervention* worth copying wholesale (method + measure). (1) The re-parameterisation trick: fit a power law on control runs, then quote every manipulated result as an effective-parameter multiplier, which makes heterogeneous sweeps comparable on one axis and is the cheapest way to give a behavioural claim a size-equivalent unit. (2) The band-in-a-plane result: a failure region whose two boundaries are power laws in model size is a *prediction under intervention* of exactly the kind we want (given N and repeat count, forecast whether the model is degraded), and its cost is a coarse grid. (3) The mechanism bridge: they name the site (induction head / prefix-matching score) in advance, measure it with a synthetic probe independent of the task that is harmed, and check that the damage ordering across capabilities follows the damage ordering of the site — the template for connecting a probe to a macroscopic curve, and they flag it as rare in the literature (warning: the disagreement between per-token loss evidence and logit attribution is a reminder that a "the circuit degraded" claim needs the direct weight-level read, not just an output-side score). (4) A confound for our own training-data work: any probe fit on data that has been repeated in fine-tuning may be reading memorisation rather than mechanism, and the same double-descent-in-a-subset shape means the damage is invisible at both ends of the sweep — our sweeps must sample the middle of the intervention range, not just "none" and "lots". (5) Explicit future work we could take up: searching for neurons that encode which distribution a token came from, and counting parameters devoted to memorisation versus generalisation.
