## What they wanted, and why
Dictionary features and probe directions are usually characterised by two cheap descriptors: the text that makes them fire hardest, and the output tokens their direction points at through the unembedding. The authors' complaint is that these descriptors are not sufficient to say what a feature *does* — they routinely meet features that look interchangeable on both and behave completely differently when you interfere with them. The post is a step toward making a feature's causal role predictable from the model before running the intervention, which is precisely the "predict what the network will do" question our survey is organised around. The bet is that a component's immediate downstream targets, measured by virtual weights, carry the missing information.

## What they studied
Anthropic's production-scale models: Claude Opus 4.7 as an LLM grader, Claude Haiku 4.5 as the behavioural subject in the alignment example, and cross-layer transcoder (CLT) features as the unit of analysis — each feature a vector in the residual stream with an encoder, a decoder and, critically, an approximation to how it feeds the features of later layers. Analysis sits at the level of features and the edges between them across layers. Ten hand-assembled groups of 3–5 candidate features, where exactly one candidate steers the model on a given prompt, make up the evaluation set; two worked examples carry the exposition (grass/green, sapphire/blue) plus one auditing example (a desperation-mediated blackmail scenario).

## How they did it
Downstream structure is measured with TWERA, virtual weights computed between feature pairs across layers and then reweighted by coactivation statistics so the ranking reflects on-distribution influence rather than a raw weight product. For each group of candidates the correct answer — the feature whose negative multiplicative steering actually changes the output — is established first by intervention, so the label is behavioural rather than interpretive. Opus 4.7 is then asked to rank the candidates most-to-least likely to be the steering feature under four information conditions: top activating examples alone; examples plus top unembeds; examples plus TWERA-ranked downstream features; and all three. Position bias is controlled by placing the correct answer at each slot an equal number of times, giving two samples per candidate. The measure is normalised rank of the correct feature, (rank − 1)/(candidates − 1), averaged over groups, so 0 is a perfect identification and indifferent guessing would sit at about 0.5. The worked examples are used to check that the downstream-derived story is falsifiable: if the reading "this is a green-as-hex feature" is right, a prompt about hex values should make it steerable, and it is tested there.

## What counts as an explanation here
A relational description: the feature's identity is given by the set of downstream features it promotes and suppresses, and the claim is partial by construction — the authors say the problem "is far from solved" and treat downstream structure as context added to existing descriptors, not a replacement for them. The explanation is meant to be predictive in a narrow, testable way: from the downstream connections alone you should be able to say which of several similar features will move the output on a specific prompt, before you steer it. It is a circuit fragment, not a circuit: no completeness claim and no statement of which downstream features were omitted.

## How they judged success
Two outcomes. Qualitatively, whether the downstream story identifies a prompt on which the "wrong" feature *does* become causal (the hex-value test), which is a prediction-then-falsify move rather than a demonstration. Quantitatively, mean normalised rank of the correct candidate from an Opus 4.7 grader across ten groups, comparing four information conditions with order randomised and the correct answer counterbalanced across slots. No random-direction control is reported for the steering labels, no baseline of a human or a simple similarity heuristic is given, and the candidate groups were chosen by the authors, so the ten groups are not a sampled benchmark.

## What they found
Both "green" features fire on the same token, share green-heavy top unembeds and similar coding-context examples, sit in similar middle layers, and only one of them changes the answer to the colour-of-grass prompt. Their downstream targets differ sharply: the inert one feeds features about hex colour literals and colour-setter syntax, while the effective one feeds colour-naming-across-languages features and a "say green" motor feature. Negative steering on the hex-flavoured feature does flip the output on a hex-value prompt, which is the prediction the reading implied. On the ranking task, examples alone give 0.457, adding top unembeds barely moves it to 0.446, adding TWERA downstream features gives 0.381, and all three together 0.355 — so the downstream signal is the one carrying information and the unembeds are nearly redundant with the examples. In the auditing example, of several desperation-flavoured features only the one whose downstream connects to acting-on-a-willingness does anything: positive additive steering on it produces blackmail in the shutdown scenario where Haiku 4.5 otherwise refuses, and the other candidates, whose downstream connects to merely discussing willingness, have no effect.

## Limits and open problems they name
The headline caveat is that the grader's best normalised rank, 0.355, is still far from identifying the feature — this ranks better, it does not solve the problem. The analysis is explicitly qualitative at the example level and the quantitative arm rests on ten author-constructed groups. TWERA is an approximation to downstream influence reweighted by coactivations, so the ranking is on-distribution rather than architectural. They note the extension to non-dictionary vector components is expected but untested, and that automated description pipelines are the intended consumer rather than a validated application.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning, and the warning is more valuable. The method: **when two components are indistinguishable by input-side descriptors, resolve them by output-side reachability** — compute their downstream targets and read the difference in where the influence goes. That is cheap relative to intervention, generalises to any residual-stream vector, and is one of the few places in this literature where the *predictor* is evaluated against a behavioural label rather than against an interpretability proxy. The warning: top-activating examples and top unembeds are properties of the feature's *input* and *output* directions, and neither says anything about whether the model routes through it on a given prompt — a feature can be perfectly legible and causally idle in the same context, so any white-box model we build must distinguish "this feature computes X" from "this feature is load-bearing here". Two concrete habits for us: build the falsification prompt (a context where the idle feature *should* be live if the reading is right), and report ranking performance against the trivially-cheap descriptor baseline rather than against chance, because here the cheap baseline is already at 0.457 and the gain is 0.10 in normalised rank.
