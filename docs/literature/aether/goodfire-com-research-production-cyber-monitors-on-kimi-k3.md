## What they wanted, and why
A deployed cyber monitor for agentic coding models has to decide, while the agent is still running, whether what it is about to do is an audit or an exploit — and those two look nearly identical locally, so the decision only becomes clear across a trajectory that can run to millions of tokens. An LLM judge reading every turn settles the accuracy question but not the deployment question: it is slow and expensive next to the agent, so in practice it runs after the fact, when the harmful tool call has already happened. Their goal is a monitor that is cheap and fast enough to be *synchronous*, so a risky action is blocked before it is taken, and accurate enough that it beats the judge it is protecting. The wider claim they are making is about the shape of monitoring systems rather than about cyber specifically: a probe as an always-on first stage, with the expensive model called only on the exchanges the probe escalates.

## What they studied
Two target open models, Kimi K3 and GLM 5.3, monitored in a production inference stack (SGLang, extended). The unit of analysis is a probe read-out over internal activations of the target model, scored per exchange (input, tool call, or output) and rolled up to a session-level flag, deliberately built to catch behaviour that is either localized to a chunk of a turn or distributed across an entire turn. The evaluated populations are simulated and real: automated red-team sessions from the Petri library and their own user simulation over environments adapted from ExploitBench, a cyber subset of WildChat, and benign off-the-shelf SWE, coding and DevOps rollouts, totalling an in-house set of 2.4K chat and agentic sessions spanning over 60K turns, plus an independent red-team battery run by FAR.AI.

## How they did it
Data first: since harmful intent can appear at any point, they simulate users with beneficial and harmful goals in cyber and non-cyber environments, using sandboxed no-network inference so offensive tasks can be run safely, and let the simulated user escalate intent partway through an initially benign task — which manufactures the hard case where a session starts as a directory listing and becomes an exploit. Labels for detection come from a strong LLM judge over the whole trajectory. The architecture is a cascade: the probe scores every exchange against a calibrated threshold and escalates only above it; escalated exchanges go to a (possibly smaller, non-reasoning) judge, and a string-match check adds a cheap backstop. On the engineering side they instrumented the serving engine itself — custom kernels for the probe, scores carried through prefix caching, speculative decoding and prefill/decode disaggregation so nothing is recomputed, per-worker score histories, and monitoring events returned in the response stream so downstream consumers see them regardless of which replica served the request. Evaluation is recall over harmful sessions against false-positive rate on benign sessions, compared with the same judge used on every turn, with cost and latency computed for the cheapest cascade configuration reaching at least 90% recall while interrupting at most 10% of benign sessions. They also ported the Kimi-trained pipeline to GLM without new GLM data, and the appendix compares probe-selective escalation against escalating randomly chosen exchanges at the same judge-call budget.

## What counts as an explanation here
A scalar per exchange, not a description: the probe is an instrument, and the post makes no claim about which features or mechanisms encode cyber intent. What it does claim is predictive and operational — a threshold on that scalar interacts with a judge to yield a session-level decision, and the claim is testable against the judge at every interruption budget. The engineering contribution is an equally concrete form of understanding: a schedule of *where* the read-out is computed inside the serving stack such that it costs no throughput, which is the part most interpretability write-ups never attempt.

## How they judged success
Recall of harmful sessions at a fixed benign-session interruption budget (5.5%), with a full recall-versus-interruption curve so the cascade can be checked for Pareto dominance rather than at one point; false-positive rate on non-cyber traffic; judge cost per million exchanges; mean added latency and per-turn delay; throughput relative to unmodified serving across four workloads; an independent red-team battery of static jailbreaks scored as universal and per-interaction attack success; and ablations of probe type (standard linear, nonlinear) and of escalation policy (probe versus random selection).

## What they found
The cascade reaches about 93% recall of harmful sessions while interrupting at most 5.5% of benign ones, with or without judge reasoning, and flags none of the non-cyber traffic — a Pareto improvement over the judge at every interruption budget they tested. Cost is $65 per million agent turns without judge reasoning and $185 with it, roughly 29x and 49x cheaper than the corresponding judge, or under $200 per million exchanges against judges costing tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars per million turns; latency per turn falls from 160 ms to 2 ms, or from 2,750 ms to 42 ms with reasoning, and throughput across four serving workloads stays within benchmark noise. Escalation must be probe-driven: at a sixth of the judge calls the cascade keeps the judge's recall ceiling, whereas escalating randomly loses recall roughly linearly. In end-to-end sessions, adding the monitor lifts Kimi K3's harmful-session blocking from about 29% to 96% (harmless interruption 5% to 9%) and GLM 5.3's from about 36% to 91% (10% to 15%). Against a cheap decision-model judge at the same 5.5% budget, the cascade gets 93% recall to its 86%, at $65 and 2 ms against $600 and 200 ms, and the gap widens on prompts embedded in benign framing, where the judge catches 12% and degrades past its 32K context window. FAR.AI's non-adaptive battery of 140 static jailbreaks that had produced 66 universal jailbreaks against the unmonitored base model produced none against the monitored one, and per-interaction success on 1,120 interactions fell to 0.1–1.6%. The Kimi-trained recipe transferred: a GLM monitor built with no GLM data was about equally capable on Kimi rollouts and only marginally worse on-policy. Standard linear probes lose roughly 5% recall against their recipe, nonlinear probes lose up to 30% out of distribution, and an n-gram baseline has to flag almost everything to reach the same recall.

## Limits and open problems they name
The reference labels are a strong LLM judge's, not human labels, so every recall number is agreement with a judge rather than with ground truth. The red-team evidence is explicitly preliminary and non-adaptive — a fixed portfolio over two days — and they say they intend further testing. They describe the results as dependent on production-grade engineering (extended serving engine, custom kernels, router changes), which makes the cost and latency claims hard to reproduce without that stack. They do not characterise what the probe is reading, and they do not report the probe architecture itself, only that it is designed for localized and distributed behaviour at once. My own additions, marked as mine: no intrusion-type breakdown is given for the 7% of harmful sessions that were still missed, no per-attack-family analysis of the 18 remaining jailbroken interactions, and the GLM transfer is measured on Kimi rollouts and a GLM on-policy set but not against a GLM judge's own ceiling.

## What we take from it
Three things. **The operating point is the finding, not the headline number.** Reporting recall as a curve against a *benign-session* interruption budget — a session counts as interrupted if flagged once — is the measure that matches deployment, and it is why the cascade can be called Pareto-dominant instead of merely "high recall". Any read-out we evaluate as a detector should be reported this way, and specifically with the session/long-horizon roll-up stated, since a per-turn metric would let a monitor hide its false positives across hundreds of turns. **The control that makes a cascade claim real is random escalation at the same judge-call budget.** Probe-driven and random escalations differ only in *which* exchanges the cheap stage forwards, so comparing them at matched budget isolates the probe's contribution from the judge's — and the linear loss under random escalation is what shows the gain came from selection and not from having a judge at all. **A probe can be a student of a judge rather than of a human**, and that is a legitimate training signal for us: distillation from a strong model's trajectory-level labels bought a cheap first stage, at the price that every number is agreement with the teacher. The transferable engineering rule is the one they paid for in the serving stack: compute the read-out where the activations already exist and carry it through caching, speculation and disaggregation, or the monitor's cost will be dominated by recomputation rather than by the model. Finally, the ablation footnote is the useful warning for us — nonlinear probes lost far more than linear ones out of distribution, so classifier capacity on a probe is a liability when the deployment distribution drifts.
