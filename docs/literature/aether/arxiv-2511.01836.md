# Priors in Time: Missing Inductive Biases for Language Model Interpretability

## What they wanted, and why
Interpretability tool design is usually justified by a model of how concepts are stored — most often "concepts are independent linear directions" — without asking whether that model matches the statistics of the data the tool is fitted to. The authors take temporal structure seriously: language is correlated across time at several scales (dependency, expectation, discourse events), so representations of consecutive tokens should be strongly dependent too. They argue, with a Bayesian reading of the sparse-autoencoder objective, that SAEs bake in an *independence-across-time* prior and therefore cannot express the temporal structure that is actually there. The larger goal is a featuriser whose inductive bias matches the generative process of language, and which can say at each moment which part of a representation was predictable from context and which part is new. This is a position paper plus a concrete architecture, framed explicitly as computational-neuroscience-inspired.

## What they studied
Gemma-2-2B (base) and a Gemma-2-2B-IT checkpoint, plus Llama models for the layer sweep; residual-stream activations from roughly mid-depth (layer 12 of Gemma, layer 15 of Llama, 0-indexed), 1B activations from Pile-Uncopyrighted, normalised to unit expected norm. They train ReLU, TopK and BatchTopK SAEs from scratch (deliberately not reusing published SAEs, which they found inconsistent) and compare against a new "Temporal Feature Analyzer". Unit of analysis: the sparse code over a *time series* of tokens — the object is a sequence of latents, not a single latent; the paper's central move is that the unit is the per-time-step split into predictable vs novel parts.

## How they did it
Three stages. **(1) Measure the data.** For a fixed position across many sequences they estimate intrinsic dimensionality (U-statistic) and show it rises steadily with sequence position while a stationary surrogate keeps it flat; they compute autocorrelation as a function of both position and lag, which varies by position for real activations and is position-invariant for the surrogate; and they project the current representation onto the PCA basis of the previous *w* tokens, finding that up to ~80% of variance is explained with *w* up to 100. **(2) Show the mismatch.** They rewrite the SAE training objective as a MAP problem and note the sparsity term is additive over timesteps, so the implied prior factorises across time (they check ReLU, JumpReLU, TopK, BatchTopK, whose constraints are all separable). Corollary: sparsity — the number of concepts needed — is assumed constant over time. A proposition then says that if the sparsity budget K is not above the local intrinsic dimension of the activation manifold, an SAE with low reconstruction error must split nearby points onto disjoint latent supports, i.e. the local geometry is fragmented. **(3) Build and test the alternative.** A Temporal Feature Analyzer projects the current activation onto a convex combination of past activations via one self-attention layer over a single ReLU layer (the predictable code), subtracts that reconstruction, and encodes the orthogonal residual with a standard TopK/BatchTopK SAE encoder (the novel code); the regulariser is applied only to the novel code, so the prior is "residual is i.i.d. across time" rather than "everything is". Both codes share one dictionary, and the paper trains them jointly, reporting that training goes through a phase where the novel code dominates, then a chaotic transition, and finally the predictive code takes over. Evaluation uses three domains chosen for different balances of prior knowledge vs in-context evidence: TinyStories-style narratives, garden-path sentences, and the random-walk graph "cognitive map" task, plus out-of-distribution Ultrachat dialogue with an instruction-tuned model while all featurisers were trained on the base model.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a prose description of a feature and not a single direction: the explanation is a **decomposition of each token's representation into a slow/predictable code and a fast/novel code**, where the predictable code is claimed to carry events, speaker roles, and long-range syntactic constraints, and the novel code carries the stimulus-driven, monosemantic features that SAEs already find. The two are shared-dictionary components, so the split is additive and the novel half stays compatible with existing autointerp pipelines (feature cards, activation examples, logit attribution). It is meant to be partial and structural: they claim the predictive code "straightens" a sequence's representational trajectory and that this straightening gathers the information a downstream prediction needs, but they do not claim the dictionary is complete, and there is no explicit model that predicts next-token logits.

## How they judged success
(a) Sanity: matched reconstruction quality — NMSE and variance explained comparable to ReLU/TopK/BatchTopK SAEs across story, web and code domains, with a "prediction-only" ablated model as the weak baseline. (b) Slow/fast separation: split the power spectrum of activations at a frequency where energy is halved, then compare kernel spectra and CKA between featuriser codes and the slow vs fast halves. (c) Trajectory geometry: 3D UMAP projections of codes plus **tortuosity** of the path traced by a single sequence (how much local arcs deviate from the global direction). (d) Event structure: cosine similarity of token-to-token codes within vs across event boundaries, on 50 GPT-generated stories with declared boundaries, plus a Gaussian-input-noise sweep as a robustness check that also coarsens the recovered blocks. (e) Syntax: 50 ambiguous garden-path sentences and 50 disambiguated control variants, split into subject / verb / object phrases, asking whether the latent similarity of verb-phrase tokens to subject vs object depends on ambiguity. (f) In-context geometry: PCA of codes vs PCA of activations on random-walk graph inputs, plus a counterfactual edit that replaces the last token's code with a target node's representation and checks whether the next-token prediction moves in the graph-consistent direction. (g) OOD: role separability and positional gradient in UMAP on Ultrachat; (h) autointerp of the novel code.

## What they found
Intrinsic dimensionality grows with position for real activations and stays flat for the stationary surrogate; up to ~80% of the variance of a representation is recoverable from the previous window, with CKA and kernel spectra showing the predictive code aligns with the *slow* half of the spectrum (0.75 vs ~0.18–0.37 for SAEs) and the novel code with the fast half (0.75) — standard SAEs at all three variants match only the fast half (0.54 slow / 0.54 fast for ReLU and TopK, ~0.35 slow). Reconstruction is comparable (NMSE ~0.139–0.152 for TopK/BatchTopK/temporal vs 0.19–0.20 for ReLU), and the predictive code accounts for ~74–81% of reconstructed norm while the novel code accounts for most of the explained *variance*; the two error vectors are near-orthogonal (cosine ≈ −0.02), which they read as the two codes carrying different bits. On stories, predictive codes show high within-event and low across-event token similarity, form hierarchical block structure in the similarity map that matches (sub)event boundaries, and produce dendrograms aligned with the narrative, whereas SAEs cluster tokens by lexical identity. On garden-path sentences, only the predictive code links the long-distance subject/verb dependencies that constitute the correct parse and stays invariant between ambiguous and control versions; SAE codes swing with the ambiguity. On the graph task, only the predictive code recovers the graph geometry in low dimension, and a counterfactual replacement of the last-token code succeeds for 4 of 9 target nodes — no other code could edit beliefs at all. In OOD dialogue, the predictive code is the only representation that separates user from assistant while keeping a smooth positional gradient, with trajectory tortuosity ≈2.0 versus roughly 25–30 for SAEs and the novel code. Autointerp on the novel code yields ordinary readable feature cards (optimism, manipulation, university, slang), i.e. nothing was lost by adding the temporal axis.

## Limits and open problems they name
The predictive module is one attention layer over one ReLU layer, which restricts what dependencies it can express; they suggest putting the temporal bias in the regulariser instead, or using richer temporal encoders. The novel code is still assumed i.i.d. across time — they call relaxing that an important direction. The counterfactual experiment works on a minority of target nodes and is described as qualitative, not a mechanistic claim, and they explicitly leave a systematic causal-intervention suite to future work. They note the protocol was deliberately not tuned (so it is one example, not a best-in-class method), that the two codes compete for explaining the signal and training can fail on the prediction side, and that at deeper layers (Llama layer 26) the predictive component stops dominating the norm (~40%), which they read as the model unembedding its context — flagging a possible layer-dependence of which prior is appropriate. The paper offers no quantitative benchmark of prediction quality, no downstream task, and no explicit next-token predictor; all comparisons are representation-geometry and similarity statistics.

## What we take from it
For our goal — a predictive white-box model, reading weights, prediction under intervention — five things. (1) **A directly relevant predictor already exists inside the dissection: the predictable component is a linear-against-the-past forecast of the current representation**, and the residual is by construction the part the context could not anticipate. Any reader we build should be scored on that same split — how much of the next state is context-explainable vs genuinely new — because the "new" part is where the model's own computation, not its input, decides. (2) **Method to reuse: subtract the context-explained part before fitting a sparse reader**, cheap (one attention layer) and it costs nothing in reconstruction; it also gives a shared dictionary for both halves rather than two incompatible toolkits. (3) **Measure to reuse: total variation won't do; use tortuosity and within-vs-across-block similarity to certify that a readout preserves sequential structure**, and a spectral split with CKA to say which time scale a readout tracks. A readout that scores well on static reconstruction can still destroy the trajectory (their SAEs are exactly that case). (4) **Framing plus a warning: check a method's prior against the data before trusting its failures.** Feature splitting, absorption and "the probe sees lexical identity" are all diagnosable as a sparsity-budget-vs-intrinsic-dimension mismatch; before we report that a direction or dictionary "cannot represent" something, we should test whether the *data geometry* is denser than the budget we gave the reader. This mirrors the frozen-reader control one level down. (5) **Intervention shape to copy: replace a token's code with a target's representation and require the next-token prediction to move the way the latent structure says it should** — a genuinely predictive test, and its 4-of-9 hit rate is an honest ceiling for code-space editing we should expect to beat, not a number to assume.
