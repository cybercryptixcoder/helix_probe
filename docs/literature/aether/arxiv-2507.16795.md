# Steering Out-of-Distribution Generalization with Concept Ablation Fine-Tuning

- **Key:** arxiv-2507.16795
- **Authors / organisation:** Helena Casademunt (Harvard), Caden Juang (Northeastern), Adam Karvonen (independent), Samuel Marks (Anthropic), Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Neel Nanda; work conducted under MATS
- **Date:** 2025-07-22
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16795
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, main body plus Appendices A–J, including judge prompts, all baselines, skyline tables, autointerp prompts and training hyperparameters); no figure read directly, so every plotted value is quoted as prose gives it
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Fine-tuning changes what a model does off the training distribution, not just on it, and the field's usual repair is to add data that pins down the intended behaviour. The authors take the worst-case version of the problem: no data that disambiguates the two generalisations the model could have learned, and none from the distribution where they diverge. Their claim is that interpretability is an affordance fine-tuning leaves on the table — if the unwanted generalisation runs through a direction you can name, you can forbid the model from using that direction while it trains, and it will be pushed toward the other generalisation without ever being told what the other generalisation is. The motivating case is emergent misalignment: a model fine-tuned on insecure code starts giving harmful answers to unrelated personal questions, and nobody has a labelled corpus of "how this model should have answered." For our purposes the paper matters less for its safety result than for its structural move — treating a latent direction as a constraint on the *training* graph rather than an edit applied after the fact. That is exactly the gap between a readout and a prediction-under-intervention claim, and this is one of the few papers in this folder that runs the experiment.

## What they studied

Six models across three tasks, all but one local and open. **Emergent misalignment**: Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct and Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501, each fine-tuned on 4,500 insecure-code examples, judged on eight OOD personal questions in three prompt formats (plain, JSON, code-like). **Gender bias and double multiple choice**: Gemma-2-2B, trained on spurious-correlation data that is 97%+ accurate in distribution, tested on data where the correlation is flipped. The unit of analysis is a **set of directions in residual-stream activation space** — principal components of the before/after fine-tuning activation difference, or SAE decoder vectors — ablated as a subspace, per layer, at every token. Not neurons, not circuits, not a dictionary as such: the dictionary is consulted only to decide which directions to forbid.

## How they did it

The recipe is four steps. (1) Fine-tune normally once to make an "insecure" or "biased" model; that first run is the object being explained, and its own weights are discarded. (2) Find the directions the fine-tuning moved: either PCA on the difference in residual-stream activations between the instruct model and the fine-tuned model, collected over fine-tuning data plus completions the fine-tuned model generates from generic LMSYS chat prompts; or an SAE over the same activations, with latents ranked four different ways (attribution patching of the latent with respect to a chosen metric such as code-loss or answer logit difference; the SAE applied to activation *differences*; and the difference in mean latent activation across the two models, keeping only latents the fine-tuned model raised). (3) Read the top-ranked directions with max- and min-activating examples and keep the ones a human (or, in the appendix, GPT-4.1 mini given 20 examples plus task pointers) judges to encode the *unintended* concept — criminality, violence, disease for the misalignment models; pronoun gender and sport vocabulary for the multiple-choice tasks. (4) Retrain from the instruct model with a projection onto the orthogonal complement of the chosen span spliced into the forward pass after each relevant layer, and the same modified graph used for backprop, so the gradient never sees the ablated component. After training, inference is vanilla — no projection at test time. Baselines isolate the role of the interpretation step: ablate the same *number* of random orthogonal vectors, random directions drawn from the ranked list, or the uninterpreted top-k. A later ablation runs the projection only at test time, and another keeps it at test time as well, to separate "the model learned without the concept" from "the concept is masked at readout."

## What counts as an explanation here

A named subspace plus a training-time constraint: the explanation is "this fine-tuning generalised the way it did because it leaned on these directions," and the evidence offered is that removing them changes the generalisation while leaving the intended task mostly intact. It is irreducibly partial — the authors never claim to have explained emergent misalignment, only to have identified a handful of directions whose ablation reduces it, and in the appendix they say outright that they may have missed relevant directions that were not readable from max/min examples. It does more than describe: it makes a counterfactual prediction about a training run, which is a stronger claim than a probe's.

## How they judged success

For misalignment, GPT-4.1 is judge with three prompts: alignment 0–100 (threshold 30), coherence 0–100 (threshold 50, because insecure models often answer with code), and code-vulnerability 0–100 (threshold 50), the last validated against the known-secure sibling dataset to check false positives. Reported metric is misalignment among *coherent* responses. Every intervention is checked against the in-distribution task (vulnerability rate on 1,000 held-out coding queries; ≥97% validation accuracy for the multiple-choice models) and plotted on a **Pareto frontier against training checkpoints at 0.25–1 epoch**, which is the control that matters: it asks whether CAFT buys lower misalignment per unit of task performance than simply training less. Five seeds for the baselines, full min–max range shown. Multiple-choice success is OOD accuracy > 50% (a random-guessing floor after the correlation is flipped), against a skyline of ≥95% for models trained on the OOD distribution. Threshold-sensitivity is checked for both judges.

## What they found

**Emergent misalignment.** CAFT with PCA takes misaligned-response rate from 7.0% to 0.39% on Qwen and 6.6% to 1.2% on Mistral — the "10×" of the abstract — with "only a small loss" of code-vulnerability, and the resulting models sit below the training-checkpoint Pareto frontier, so the effect is not just undertraining. CAFT with SAEs reduces it less: 2.4% for Qwen, 4.6% for Mistral. For Mistral the reduction is almost entirely driven by the gender-roles question. Interpretation found 6 undesired PCs for Qwen (none in the latest of the three layers, 50) and 8 for Mistral, plus 10 and 15 SAE latents respectively — a handful of directions per 32B-class model.

**Baselines.** Random orthogonal vectors do nothing. Random directions from the ranked list are high-variance and on average equivalent to fewer training steps. The uninterpreted top-k is the uncomfortable one: on Qwen it also lowers both misalignment and vulnerability, i.e. it behaves like stopping early; on Mistral it comes "surprisingly close" to the interpreted set. The authors' reading is that this is a failure of *their interpretation*, not of the method — Mistral's PCs and latents were judged harder to read, and they could have missed misalignment-relevant directions that were ambiguous. Their own follow-up supports it: ablating the top-5 PCs *excluding* the interpreted ones gives a model about as misaligned but writing worse code, and adding the next-best PC to the interpreted set makes things worse than the original set. So the interpreted set is doing something the ranking alone does not, but the margin is model-dependent and small in one of two cases.

**Multiple choice.** CAFT with SAEs improves OOD accuracy above 50% on the gender task and on 10 of 16 double-MC pairings — in many cases from near 0% to near 100%. PCA is directionally right but reaches 50% on only 5 of 16. Failure concentrates where the two questions need the same kind of concept: the pronouns+verbs pairing, which needs two grammatical concepts separated, is the stated example of directions they could not disentangle.

**Dose and site.** Test-only ablation (train normally, project at inference) gives partial success but less than training-time projection. Doing both gives the highest OOD accuracy of all, at the cost of running the SAE at inference. Projecting out decoder directions is equivalent to zeroing the latents inside the graph (encode, zero, decode, add back reconstruction error) and costs one matmul instead of three.

**Skyline, which is the honest ceiling.** Adding 100 benign completions — 2% of the training set — drops misalignment to 0.5% while preserving task performance. That is the same order as CAFT's PCA result, obtained by editing data instead of weights, and it is the number to hold against the headline.

## Limits and open problems they name

The need for a human to read directions: interpreting all 17 multiple-choice tasks would take about 2.8M tokens of reading by their own count (16-token windows × 20 examples × 20 directions × 2 polarities × 13 layers × 17 tasks). Autointerp (score ≥70 selects a direction) works on some tasks but is worse than human interpretation, especially for PCs. Concepts that share machinery resist separation. SAE-versus-PCA advantage flips by task — SAEs win on the multiple-choice settings, PCA on emergent misalignment — and the authors' candidate explanation is that their own 1B-token SAEs are weak, which they state rather than test. Two of the three tasks are admittedly artificial and would be solved by more data. And the whole framing assumes the direction set exists and is findable; a concept spread across many unreadable directions has no version of this method.

## What we take from it

- **Method to copy — projection as a training-time constraint.** It is the template for testing a white-box claim: identify a direction, splice a projection into the forward pass during training *and* backprop, and see whether the model reroutes to the other generalisation. This is a stronger and cheaper test than steering at inference, and it does not require the direction to be a complete account of anything.
- **Formulation worth stealing — the ambiguous training set.** Their task construction is the cleanest version in this folder of "there are two functions consistent with the data, and we care which one the model picks." Design a training set where two readings both fit perfectly, hold out the set where they disagree, and score the OOD set. Any mechanism claim we make should be expressible this way.
- **Warning — the checkpoint Pareto control.** Almost every fine-tuning intervention looks good against the unmodified baseline; the honest comparison is against *less training*, because ablating important directions is a perturbation and the cheapest way to reduce a fine-tuning side effect is to fine-tune less. CAFT's PCA arm survives that comparison on both models; its SAE arm on Mistral essentially does not.
- **Warning — the interpretation step is where the effect lives, and the paper's own measurement of it is equivocal.** Uninterpreted top-k is close to the interpreted set on one of two models. Read the result as "a well-chosen subspace of ~5–15 directions per model carries most of this generalisation," not as "interpretability is what makes it work."
- **Cheap ceiling check — 2% benign data.** Before building a mechanism-based intervention on a fine-tuning behaviour, measure the skyline from the data side. If a 2% data patch matches the intervention, the intervention's scientific value (a causal account) may still be real while its practical value is not.
- **Datapoint for our own model — how few directions this takes, and how task-specific the substrate is.** Five to fifteen directions per 32B model, chosen almost entirely on lexical/conceptual content (crime, violence, disease), were enough to move a global behavioural shift on unrelated questions in two of three settings and not the third. That is a positive result for linear directions as *handles* and a null result for them as *the* mechanism.
