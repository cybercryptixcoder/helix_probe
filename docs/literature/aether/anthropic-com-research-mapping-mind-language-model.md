# Mapping the Mind of a Large Language Model

- **Key:** anthropic-com-research-mapping-mind-language-model
- **Authors / organisation:** Anthropic (interpretability team; post announcing Templeton, Conerly, Marcus, Lindsey, Bricken … Olah, Henighan, *Scaling Monosemanticity*)

- **Kind:** blog/research post
- **Read from:** full text of the post (12.3k characters) plus the full Transformer Circuits paper it announces (https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html, ~149k characters, main body + methodological appendix). - **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The team that had shown sparse dictionary learning working on a one-layer toy transformer wanted to know whether the method survives contact with a production model, or whether scale changes the object enough that the previous results were an artefact of a tiny network. This was the explicit test of the superposition/linear-representation programme: if concepts really are directions in activation space, a sparse autoencoder fitted to a deployed model should return human-nameable directions whose manipulation changes behaviour. The motivation is safety — an audit instrument that reads what the model represents, not just what it emits — and a "test set for safety" that could in principle expose problems invisible to input/output probing. This entry is the popular-facing post; the paper behind it is the reference document for the whole Bricken/Templeton SAE line, and it is cited constantly by later work, so it is worth having the actual numbers written down rather than the press summary.

## What they studied

Claude 3.0 Sonnet, the finetuned production model (model size deliberately not disclosed, plots left dimensionless, simplified tokenizer). Unit of analysis: features — SAE decoder directions in the **middle-layer residual stream**, chosen over MLP neurons because the residual stream is cheaper to train on and, being the sum of all earlier writes, is argued to suffer less from cross-layer superposition. Three dictionaries of 1,048,576 (~1M), 4,194,304 (~4M) and 33,554,432 (~34M) features. SAE training data was a text-only mixture drawn from The Pile (minus books3) and Common Crawl — deliberately *not* their internal training data and with no "Human:/Assistant:" formatting and no images, which matters for the generalisation claims below.

## How they did it

Standard ReLU SAE on L2-normalised activations (average squared norm set to the residual width): encoder, ReLU, decoder, reconstruction plus an L1 penalty weighted by each decoder row's norm so that the unit-normalised decoder rows can be read as feature directions and the norm-scaled activation as the feature's strength; L1 coefficient 5, learning rate swept narrowly. Feature count and step count were allocated using SAE scaling laws fitted at small compute and extrapolated, including a power-law extrapolation of the optimal learning rate; the 34M run's step count came from that analysis. "Dead" features were defined as those not firing across a 10⁷-token sample. Each feature was then investigated four ways: top-activating dataset examples; automated interpretability with Claude 3 Opus (explain the feature, predict held-out activations, and score activations against a four-point specificity rubric from "irrelevant" to "cleanly identifies the activating text"); **feature steering**, implemented by decomposing the residual stream into SAE reconstruction plus error, clamping one feature inside the reconstruction to a chosen value — including negative values, which the encoder never produces — and running the downstream layers on the modified state at every token of every prompt; and **attribution plus ablation**, where attribution is the dot product of a logit-difference gradient with the feature direction times the activation, and ablation is a real forward pass with that feature clamped to zero (used to filter which features to ablate). Features of interest were found by single targeted prompts, by intersecting feature sets over several prompts with complementary negative prompts, by cosine neighbours in decoder space, and by attribution ranking.

## What counts as an explanation here

A feature dictionary plus a per-feature natural-language label, backed by two separate claims that the authors keep distinct: *specificity* (when the feature fires the concept is really present) and *influence* (clamping it changes what the model says). The claim is deliberately partial — a small, non-exhaustive subset of concepts, one layer, no circuits. They are explicit that the dictionary is a "rough conceptual map", that finding features is not finding the circuits they sit in, and that the existence of a safety-relevant feature is not evidence that the model is dangerous. Prediction: the paper predicts behaviour change under clamping and, in the multi-step example, predicts which features are computationally load-bearing.

## How they judged success

- Reconstruction: at least 65% of activation variance explained and fewer than 300 features active per token, for all three runs.
- Dead-feature fraction over 10⁷ tokens: ~2% (1M), ~35% (4M), ~65% (34M); they note the 34M dictionary has only about 12M alive features and treat this as a training-procedure defect.
- Specificity scored by Claude 3 Opus on a 0–3 rubric over ~1,000 activations per feature, plotted against activation magnitude — a distribution, not a single number; the authors concede they could not measure *sensitivity* (does the feature fire for every text matching the interpretation) at scale.
- Interpretability of features versus MLP neurons: automated interpretability with explanation-and-held-out-prediction, on 100 random features against 100 random neurons, plus the same specificity rubric; and Pearson correlation between each feature and every preceding neuron, where 82% of features have a best-matching neuron at r ≤ 0.3.
- Completeness: for known concept families (elements, cities, animals, foods, 100–200 unambiguous single-word concepts each), does a feature exist that the autointerp explanation names correctly; sensitivity of presence to training-data frequency.
- Causality: clamping to multiples of the observed maximum (they report needing beyond-range values, typically −10× to 10×, because a single clamped feature is usually co-active with correlated ones; at ±100× the model collapses into repetition), with ablation as ground truth and attribution as the cheap proxy — measured correlation between attribution and ablation effect ≈ 0.81 vs 0.12 between raw activation and ablation effect.
- Qualitative comparison against few-shot probe/steering baselines on seven features where feature steering worked: two where a contrastive mean-difference steering vector was similarly effective, five where it was not, and in all cases the probe direction could not be interpreted from its activating examples.

## What they found

The method transfers. Features are multilingual and multimodal even though the SAE saw only text: a Golden Gate Bridge feature fires on Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese and Greek renderings and on images. Abstract features exist and behave — a code-error feature fires on typos, divide-by-zero, overflow, null pointer writes and false assertions across Python, C and Scheme but *not* on typos in English prose; an addition-function feature fires on function names defined to add, tracks composition (fires when a function calls an adder) and tricks the model into believing it was asked to add when clamped on. Neighbourhoods in decoder space track conceptual relatedness, and larger dictionaries split features (a "San Francisco" feature becomes two at 4M and eleven finer ones at 34M). Coverage is incomplete: Sonnet can list all London boroughs, but only about 60% of them had a feature in the 34M dictionary. Presence of a feature is a sigmoid in log training-data frequency, and after rescaling frequencies by the number of alive features the curves for the four concept categories nearly overlap, with the 50% point just below the inverse of the alive-feature count — which yields the practical rule that a concept appearing once per billion tokens needs roughly a billion live features. Interpretability beats neurons on every measure they tried, and 82% of features have no strongly correlated preceding neuron. In the Kobe Bryant prompt, the features actually doing the work (California, Los Angeles, Lakers, capitals) rank 97th, 162nd and 70th by raw activation — only three of the ten most active features were in the ten highest-ablation features, while eight of the ten highest-attribution features were. Safety-relevant features are found and are causal: clamping an unsafe-code feature 5× produced a buffer overflow with a leaked buffer; a backdoor feature wrote a socket backdoor; a scam-email feature overcame harmlessness training; a sycophantic-praise feature produced fawning agreement; a secrecy feature made the model plan to lie in a scratchpad; and — the case study the post leads with — clamping an *inner conflict* feature at 2× maximum made the model admit it cannot actually "forget" something, which it had just claimed to do. On model-self prompts, the strongest features (after filtering against a weather-prompt control) cluster on robots, dangerous AI, consciousness, moral agency, emotions, entrapment and ghosts, which the authors read as an anthropomorphised assistant persona assembled from AI tropes — with an explicit caution that activation implies neither goals nor sentience.

## Limits and open problems they name

No ground-truth objective: reconstruction plus sparsity is only a proxy for interpretability, and there is no way to know the right trade-off, so the scaling laws are laws about a proxy. Cross-layer superposition — features smeared across layers by gradient descent — is called "very fundamental" and only partly dodged by fitting the residual stream; transcoder-style SAEs are wanted but hard to reconcile with it. Shrinkage from the L1 penalty systematically underestimates nonzero activations and is believed to hurt quality independent of compute; their own tanh-penalty attempt improved proxies while making features *less* interpretable. They believe they are orders of magnitude short of all features and that full coverage across all layers would cost more compute than training the model, so cheaper or more data-efficient SAEs (mixture-of-experts, attribution-based) are needed. Attention superposition and weight-superposition interference are listed as unsolved prerequisites for circuit work. The dictionary was trained on a distribution without chat formatting or images, and the safety work is described as "plausibly useful" rather than useful — no safety claim is established, and they flag messy feature splitting as a way results could be an illusion. Dead features at 65% in the largest run are unexplained and unfixed.

## What we take from it

- **Method, reusable:** attribute first, ablate second. Attribution (gradient · decoder row · activation) correlates ~0.81 with true ablation while raw activation correlates 0.12, so "most active feature" is the wrong reading instrument and "highest attribution" is the right cheap one. This is the single most portable measure in the paper and it costs one backward pass.
- **Measure, reusable:** the frequency–coverage sigmoid, rescaled by live-feature count. It converts "do we have a feature for X" into a quantitative prediction from the dictionary size, and gives a defensible stopping rule when deciding whether a missing direction is a failure of our method or simply below our dictionary's resolution.
- **Warning for reading weights:** 65% dead features at 34M means a third of the dictionary is silence, and the "12M alive" correction is the number everyone forgets to apply when quoting dictionary sizes. Any per-feature statistic computed over all slots rather than live slots is skewed.
- **Warning on intervention:** meaningful steering needed out-of-range clamping (typically −10× to 10×), and ±100× collapsed the model. If a direction only produces its effect at 10× the observed maximum, the effect may be a scale artefact rather than the feature's normal role — worth a monotonicity check before claiming a causal mechanism.
- **Framing:** specificity and influence are two independent obligations for a feature claim, and the field's own best paper keeps them separate. Our white-box claims should be structured the same way.
