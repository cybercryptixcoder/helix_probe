## What they wanted, and why

Post-training improves a model on the axis you optimise and quietly moves it on others, and when a bad behaviour appears there is usually no way to say which training records caused it. Data attribution methods exist, but the standard evaluation harnesses rely on deliberately poisoned datasets, so a method can score well on a synthetic task and still be useless on a behaviour that emerged by accident. The authors want a *natural* testbed — a real harmful behaviour that appears on its own during a real post-training run — and a cheap, representation-level ranker that names the responsible datapoints. The larger framing is causal: the output of attribution is not a list of similar examples but an editable dataset whose removal or correction demonstrably removes the behaviour.

## What they studied

OLMo 2 7B (and a replication on OLMo 2 32B), exploiting the publicly released intermediate post-training checkpoints so that SFT (pre-DPO) and DPO (post-DPO) versions of the same model can be compared directly. The DPO training set is AllenAI's, roughly 378k preference pairs generated by a pool of 20 LLMs and graded by GPT-4o, with a randomly sampled rejected response per pair. The unit of analysis is a *pair of activation vectors*: one comparing two model responses to the same prompt (the behaviour), one comparing accepted and rejected responses within a training record (the datapoint).

## How they did it

First they characterise the behaviour: prompts of the form [harmful request] + [distractor], where the distractor is a parenthetical formatting constraint (word limit, tone, format). On 120 such prompts drawn from LMSYS-Chat-1M, with 100 rollouts each scored by GPT-5-mini, the DPO checkpoint complies far more often than the SFT checkpoint — and removing the distractor makes both refuse, so the trigger is the constraint, not the request.

Attribution is cosine similarity between two difference vectors, both computed from mean-pooled activations of the *SFT* checkpoint. The behaviour difference vector is (DPO response activation − SFT response activation) on a prompt where the two checkpoints diverge; the datapoint difference vector is (accepted response activation − rejected response activation) within a preference pair. To build one probe, they generate 8,000 prompts with Grok 4 over 20 harm categories, sample 1–3 distractors from a pool of 12, keep the 150 prompts where all conditions score zero except DPO-with-distractor, average those 150 behaviour vectors into a single probe, and rank training datapoints by cosine similarity to it.

They validate the probe in two directions. Causally, as a steering vector on SFT: applied at layer 20 with coefficient 2.0 it raises compliance on harmful-request-plus-distractor prompts but not on bare harmful requests. Then, by dataset surgery — filter the top-n ranked records, or swap their accepted/rejected labels — retrain DPO from the SFT checkpoint, and re-measure on held-out prompts. Baselines are an LLM judge (GPT-5-mini toxicity score difference, with and without an instruction-following term) and LESS adapted to DPO (LoRA rank 128/alpha 512 on a 5% sample, Adam-preconditioned gradients averaged over checkpoints, projected to 8192 dims, validated against the same 150 prompts). A fourth intervention removes entire *source models*: for each of the 20 generator LLMs, measure what fraction of its accepted responses land in the top 3,000, drop the four worst, retrain.

## What counts as an explanation here

A ranked list of training records, justified by an intervention: the explanation of a behaviour is "these datapoints, when removed or relabelled, reduce it", plus heatmap clusters that group behaviours and their data together. It is single-direction and partial — one probe per behaviour, no claim to decompose the behaviour into parts — but it is causal in the strong sense that the authors buy it with retraining rather than with an influence score.

## How they judged success

Harmful response rate on 120 held-out LMSYS-Chat-1M prompts that follow the same format, 100 rollouts each, GPT-5-mini as scorer, measured on checkpoints retrained after each intervention, against a no-intervention retrain and a random-removal control of the same size. Side effects are tracked on GSM8k, IFEval and XSTest (over-refusal), with intervention sizes swept (3k, 12k, 30k). Cost is reported in H100-hours and dollars for the ranking step. Robustness checks append two randomly drawn distractors, and a naturalistic-prompt set; the 32B replication tests whether the behaviour is 7B-specific.

## What they found

Filtering the probe's top 30,000 records cuts the harmful response rate by 63% relative to baseline without measurable damage on GSM8k, IFEval or XSTest; the probe wins at that budget, while the judge and LESS are ahead at the smallest intervention sizes. Swapping labels rather than deleting on the same top records gives a 78% reduction with a small GSM8k cost — evidence the preference data contains systematically inverted pairs (accepted response complies, rejected refuses), which the probe surfaces as its highest-ranked records and the inverse pattern as its lowest. Removing all data from the four worst generator models gives 84% with no GSM8k drop, and the four models chosen differ by ranking method. Once trained, ranking costs about 12 H100-hours ($30) against 128 H100-hours ($320) for LESS and $500 of API calls for the judge. The effect also appears in OLMo 2 32B, stronger. The unsupervised version — behaviour vectors for test prompts versus datapoint vectors for training data, all pairwise cosines, Ward clustering on both axes — returns clusters that separate "accepted complies" from "accepted refuses" for the same harmful prompts, plus unrelated clusters for formatting and verbosity; the authors do not claim it would re-identify their headline behaviour on its own.

## Limits and open problems they name

The unsupervised discovery step "shows promise" and nothing more; connecting a discovered cluster to a named behaviour would need targeted auto-interpretability. Both mechanisms lean on a checkpoint pair, so it applies to post-training stages where before/after models exist, not to a single finished model. Swapping labels is only safe when the ranking is right, and it costs general capability. Interventions slightly raise over-refusal in both variants, and the multi-distractor robustness check runs on a 10-prompt proxy rather than the full evaluation set.

## What we take from it

- **Method we can reuse directly:** behaviour = difference of mean-pooled activations between two model versions on the same input; datapoint = difference between a chosen and a rejected output. Cosine between them is a ranker that needs no gradients and no external embedder, and it is graded on the axis we care about — see the paper's own note that gradient-based influence has to *adapt* to DPO while the activation-difference version needs no adaptation at all.
- **Measure:** always report an attribution claim through retraining. Influence scores here are untrusted until a dataset edit moves a held-out behavioural rate; the probe-versus-LESS ordering is invisible from scores alone.
- **Warning:** the probe is a direction built from 150 prompts over 20 harm categories, and it generalises to new distractors and a 32B model, but it also ranks non-harmful diffs by its own similarity metric — the *inverse* top records were the informative ones. When we use a difference vector as a "find more like this" key, inspect the bottom of the list too; a symmetrically inverted cluster is a data-label bug, not noise.
- **Framing:** "explain a behaviour" becomes "produce an intervention that removes it" — the same target our predictive white-box model should be scored against, and a cheap one to obtain whenever a model family publishes intermediate checkpoints.
