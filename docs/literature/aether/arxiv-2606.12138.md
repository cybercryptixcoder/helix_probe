# Unstable Features, Reproducible Subspaces: Understanding Seed Dependence in Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.12138
- **Authors / organisation:** Gleb Gerasimov, Timofei Rusalev, Nikita Balagansky, Daniil Laptev, Vadim Kurochkin, Daniil Gavrilov — T-Tech
- **Date:** 2026-06-10
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12138
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text — arXiv HTML v1 (~92k chars), main body §1–§8 plus Limitations, references, and Appendices A–G. **No figure inspected**: every curve, histogram and phase panel (Figs. 1–23) is recorded here only from the prose and captions that describe it.
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central) — it is the folder's first entry whose main experiment is the question four of our own Patterns lines keep asserting, namely whether a read-out survives a rerun.

## What they wanted, and why
Prior work established that two SAEs trained on identical activations with different random initialisations learn substantially different features, and that SAE latents are not canonical units of analysis. That leaves a specific question unasked: when a single feature fails to reappear, does the *direction* it named actually vanish from the second dictionary, or did the second SAE choose a different basis for the same region of activation space? The distinction matters for anyone building a decomposition-based account, because automatic interpretation will happily write a plausible explanation for a feature that is not reproducible at all, so "this feature means X" cannot by itself be evidence that the model computes with X. The authors want a per-feature, cheaply computable signal that separates the reproducible part of a dictionary from the part that is an artefact of one seed, and then want to describe what the irreproducible part actually is. The larger goal is to know which units a downstream explanation is allowed to be built out of.

## What they studied
GPT-2 residual stream at layer 7 as the main setting, with Pythia-160M-deduped and Gemma-2-2B plus several layers as breadth, and a randomly initialised GPT-2 as a null. Five SAE families: vanilla ReLU+L1, TopK, BatchTopK, HierarchicalTopK and JumpReLU, trained on 1B FineWeb-sample tokens, dictionary sizes 2^14 to 2^16. The unit of analysis is a **decoder vector** — one dictionary column, unit-normalised — and the corpus of units is 96 independently seeded TopK SAEs at TopK=64, F=2^14, giving 95 comparisons per anchor. Statistics distinguishing the two regimes are token entropy, activation frequency, conditional magnitude, and explained variance / next-token loss under masking. A synthetic dictionary with a planted low-rank block supplies the mechanism check.

## How they did it
They define **feature stability** as a reappearance probability. One run is the anchor; for each anchor feature they ask whether any feature in each of the other 95 dictionaries has decoder cosine ≥ 0.7, which makes the count binomial(N, p) and gives an estimate of p per feature. They chose many-to-one argmax-cosine over the usual one-to-one Hungarian assignment for cost and because it yields a per-feature number; a check against Hungarian matching gave a matched-set IoU of 0.978 ± 0.001, so the shortcut is not doing the work. Features are then sorted into endpoint sets — unstable if p ≤ 0.05, stable if p ≥ 0.95 — and compared. For function, they mask features and measure explained variance, then patch the residual stream at the SAE site with the masked-feature reconstruction and read the change in next-token cross-entropy. Because unstable features fire less often, the masking protocol is frequency-matched: N stable features against 4N unstable ones, ten sampled masks per budget, with an optional norm-reweighted variant so that the drop is not simply a magnitude artefact. For explanation, they run the SAEBench auto-interpretation pipeline with Qwen3-32B as interpreter and evaluator and compare detection scores and explanation vocabulary. For geometry, they take the decoder submatrix spanned by each set, measure its effective rank (entropy of the normalised singular spectrum) under a size-matched subsampling protocol, and test whether the top-r singular subspace of one seed's set explains another seed's set — a cross-seed projection that a pure-noise account predicts should fail. Finally they plant a low-rank block in a synthetic dictionary, where features inside the shared span are identifiable only up to rotation, and check that seeded SAEs reproduce the empirical pattern.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a dictionary and not a feature description, but a **partition of a dictionary into reproducible and irreproducible parts, with a geometric account of why the second part is irreproducible**. The claim's form is: unstable features are individually non-identifiable yet collectively span a subspace that *is* reproducible across seeds, so seed dependence is basis ambiguity within shared structure rather than the absence of structure. It is explicitly partial — the authors say the low-rank subspace result is evidence for a concrete mechanism, not a complete identification of what generates instability in real SAEs. It does make predictions that can fail: a shared subspace learned in seed A should explain seed B's unstable features, unstable features should carry little reconstruction and prediction mass once frequency is matched, and a synthetic low-rank block should reproduce the whole pattern.

## How they judged success
Metrics named precisely: reappearance probability with cosine threshold θ=0.7 and endpoint cutoff ε=0.05, both swept for sensitivity; explained variance under masking (with one-sigma bands over 10 mask draws); Δ cross-entropy from activation patching at the SAE site; token entropy of the activation-conditioned vocabulary distribution; effective rank normalised by hidden dimension d=768; cross-seed SVD explained variance; SAEBench detection score, Sparse Probing, AutoInterp mean, SCR and TPP; a linear-probe F1 for separating unstable from other decoders, evaluated within-seed and transferred to a held-out seed; and GPT-5 accuracy at labelling stable vs unstable from explanation text alone under anonymised labels. Controls worth naming: the frequency-matched 4N masking budget, the norm-reweighted ablation arm, the Hungarian-matching robustness check, the size-matched effective-rank subsampling, and — the important one — **SAEs trained on randomly initialised GPT-2 activations**, compared across a sweep of θ.

## What they found
The reappearance distribution is bimodal with a mean around 0.75: a large group of features recurs in nearly every run, a smaller distinct group recurs in almost none, and few sit in between. **The asymmetry between the two groups is functional.** Unstable features fire less often (mean frequency ≈0.18% versus ≈0.44% for stable) and lack the stable group's high-magnitude tail. Under the frequency-matched protocol, masking 4N unstable features moves explained variance and next-token loss only slightly, while masking N stable features degrades both substantially. Their explanations differ systematically: unstable features are described in surface-form terms (substrings, prefixes, capitalisation, punctuation) and stable ones in structural terms (phrases, syntactic roles, semantic groupings); "substring" appears in 38.9% of unstable explanations against 11.3% of stable, "phrase" rises from 4.1% to 32.0%, and the fraction of stable features with a perfect detection score is about 4.5× the unstable fraction. GPT-5, seeing only explanation text with labels anonymised, classifies stability at 0.88 accuracy — so stability is legible in the language the pipeline produces, which is also the warning: interpretability scores are separable from reproducibility.

**Geometrically the unstable set is lower-dimensional but its subspace transfers.** Effective rank over hidden size is ≈0.587 (p≤0.01) and 0.649 (p≤0.05) for unstable sets against ≈0.808 and 0.804 for stable sets. A logistic classifier on decoder vectors separates unstable features from the rest at F1 ≈0.73 (ε=0) / 0.67 (ε=0.1), saturating as N grows, and crucially the within-seed and cross-seed F1 curves nearly coincide: the property is a property of the *direction*, not of the fitted classifier's seed. The top-r singular subspace of one seed's unstable set explains the corresponding subspace in other seeds well, so individual vectors are non-reproducible inside a reproducible span.

**The synthetic model makes the mechanism explicit.** With d=32, r=2, k=8, full-rank ground-truth features reappear at near-perfect probability with near-perfect cosine recovery, while features inside the shared rank-2 block reappear at ≈0.25 and are not one-to-one identifiable; the pattern holds across r∈{1,2,4}, k∈{4,8}, and the learned low-rank block has both lower effective rank and above-random cross-seed subspace similarity. A residual-based diagnostic (cluster residual-corrected activations on examples where exactly one unstable feature fires) partially recovers the ground-truth atoms, and the authors are explicit that this is a diagnostic, not a training method — freezing the replaced rows and retraining the encoder did not improve explained variance.

**Variant and budget effects are principled.** Vanilla ReLU+L1 is almost perfectly stable (unstable fraction 0.003) but pays EV (0.833 at sparsity 62 versus 0.892 for TopK at 64); HierarchicalTopK cuts instability to 0.028 at EV 0.876; JumpReLU sits intermediate at EV 0.902 with unstable 0.038; TopK and BatchTopK are nearly indistinguishable (0.098/0.097 unstable at matched EV 0.892), which they read as the hard top-k constraint itself being the source of seed-dependent freedom rather than per-token versus per-batch imposition. Within TopK, raising k buys EV and monotonically costs stability. **More training does not fix it:** unstable fraction falls early and then plateaus at ≈8% at 10B tokens. And the random-model control is the sharpest result in the paper — across a wide θ sweep the stable fraction collapses on random-GPT-2 activations while unstable features dominate, yet those SAEs still achieve high auto-interpretation detection scores. The proposed stability knobs are a Mahalanobis/whitening interpolation (α∈[0,1]) that reduces instability but substantially worsens EV, and a feature-pool construction: deduplicate decoder features from S seeds at θ=0.7, select the most probable 16,384, briefly tune for ~2M tokens, and recover near-baseline EV while the chosen dictionary is dominated by high-probability features after only a modest number of source SAEs. SAEBench on the constructed SAE: Sparse Probing 0.673 vs 0.656 and AutoInterp 0.850 vs 0.818 improve, SCR 0.258 vs 0.269 and TPP 0.090 vs 0.107 fall modestly.

## Limits and open problems they name
The stable/unstable partition depends on two arbitrary thresholds, θ and ε, and although the qualitative trends survive sweeps, the precise membership and sizes of the endpoint sets are conditional on those choices. The low-rank subspace story is evidence for a mechanism but does not show that every unstable feature in real models arises that way. Most importantly, the "no stability–EV trade-off" claim is scoped to the pooling construction, which aggregates several independently trained SAEs — it does not show that a single training objective can reach the same stability at no reconstruction cost. They also flag the future work that our own survey cares about: identifying which model components produce the reproducible low-rank subspaces, and recovering *identifiable* individual features inside them.

Mine, not theirs: the random-model comparison is made on the stable-fraction curve and on detection scores, but there is no matched-capacity baseline for the pooling construction (a second standard SAE trained for the same extra 2M tokens), so part of the constructed SAE's advantage over a single run could be the extra tuning budget rather than the pooling. The endogenous-uncertainty story is also not available: p is estimated from 95 sibling seeds that all share the same data order and the same hyperparameters, so an instability that comes from optimiser variance rather than initialisation would be invisible here. And nothing tests whether the *subspace* that transfers across seeds also transfers across dictionary sizes, layers, or the layer's role in a circuit — the cross-seed projection result is the paper's strongest claim and it is measured only within one layer of one model family at fixed width.

## What we take from it
- **A reproducibility score for a read-out, computed before any semantic claim is made.** Estimated reappearance probability across seeded reruns is cheap, per-unit, and turns "we found the feature for X" into "we found the feature for X, and it recurs in 94 of 95 reruns". This is the measure our Patterns block has been asking for and it costs a day of compute, not a research programme.
- **The measured dissociation between interpretability score and reproducibility is a warning aimed directly at our evaluation habits.** SAEs on randomly initialised transformers still earn high detection scores — automatic interpretation is not a faithfulness test, and a read-out with a beautiful description and a high probe accuracy can be a per-seed artefact. Report both or neither.
- **Instability has a shape, so it can be reported rather than hidden.** Unstable units are low-frequency, low-entropy, surface-form triggers with weak reconstruction and prediction mass, concentrated in a low-rank subspace. That means a stability audit is also a description of where a dictionary is describing the tokeniser instead of the computation — and the "substring" versus "phrase" vocabulary split is a free rough diagnostic on any explanation set we already have.
- **The EV–stability frontier is a reporting requirement, not a footnote.** Vanilla is stable and cheap to explain and worse at reconstruction; TopK reconstructs better and is less stable; Mahalanobis reweighting buys stability by wrecking EV; pooling is the one construction that appears to escape the trade-off. Any SAE-based account we publish should place itself on that plane with the two axes both quoted.
- **The pooling recipe is a concrete, reusable ensemble protocol** for a stable dictionary: deduplicate across seeds at a cosine threshold, keep the most probable, briefly tune. It is the same intuition as bagging but with deduplication and a stability-ranked selection, and the paper's own honesty about its scope (it is the construction, not a training objective, that escapes the trade-off) is the part to copy into our write-up.
- **For a predictive white-box model, the unit question is now unavoidable.** If individual directions are non-identifiable across seeds while their span is reproducible, then any claim about "the direction for X" is a claim about a basis choice, and the seed-invariant object is the subspace. A predictive model that must survive retraining should predict at the level of subspaces and interaction, and should say so rather than naming a single latents.
