## What they wanted, and why
This is a lab-meeting-style update rather than a paper: seven short notes on directions the Anthropic interpretability team was pursuing mid-2023, plus a digest of external work. The motivation is programmatic. Their superposition toy work had produced clean geometry in a setting with no data limit, and the question they now need answered is whether features can actually be *recovered* from real trained models — which requires deciding what a "feature" even is, choosing a factorization method and its capacity, and knowing where the story about features-in-superposition breaks down. Two of the notes (weight superposition, attention-head superposition) extend the theory from "features are packed into a direction" to "the computation over those features is itself packed in", which is the part that matters for anyone trying to predict what a superposed network can compute.

## What they studied
Six distinct toy/real-object settings in one post. (1) Synthetic dictionary-learning tasks: n_sparse-dimensional sparse vectors with n_active non-zeros, randomly projected into 32 dimensions, to be recovered by dictionary learning — used to calibrate their proposed notion of feature-count correctness. (2) Ad-hoc experiments on activations of trained dense language models (described only in passing). (3) Toy ReLU-output models reused from earlier posts, re-analysed for "feature pressure". (4) Small one-layer transformers with trivial embeddings and unembeddings, trained on token sequences that over-represent chosen skip-trigrams. (5) A new toy problem where "features" lie on a 1-D circular manifold at a controllable resolution length scale ℓ. (6) No real models are examined in the notes themselves — the real-model claims are hedged.

## How they did it
*Dictionary learning.* They define the "total information" of a sparse factorization A = SD as the sum of per-entry surprisals under a distribution fitted to the entries of S and D (a 100-bin histogram over a fixed entry range in the initial implementation, which they say will change), then sweep dictionary size and L1 coefficient and plot reconstruction MSE against total information, i.e. a rate–distortion frontier; a larger dictionary costs information, a sparser code saves it, and the hypothesis is that the Pareto frontier is occupied by the *correct* number of factors. *Dictionary-learning worries* is a conceptual comparison of full dictionary learning (compressed sensing / LASSO inner loop) against sparse autoencoders: the network needs to retrieve features cheaply, in one layer, or superposition has bought nothing, whereas a compressed-sensing decoder searches a much larger space and can invent pair-features that are convenient for a solver but implausible for a network; and activations are likely not zero-centred, i.e. A = SD + b, which breaks naive dictionary learning (though the fix is easy) — a point motivated by Hobbhahn's "shifted data point superposition" observation. *Feature pressure* re-plots earlier polyhedron results against the ratio of features the model would ideally represent to available dimensions, and against training length. *Weight superposition* is a derivation: with U^X and U^Y the maps putting features of two adjacent layers into superposition, the ideal weights W* are mapped by W*_{ij} e_i ⊗ e_j → W*_{ij} U^X_i ⊗ U^Y_j ≈ W, i.e. U^W = U^X ⊗ U^Y, with interference between two weights given by the Frobenius inner product of Kronecker products — ⟨U^X_i ⊗ U^Y_j, U^X_k ⊗ U^Y_l⟩_F. *Attention superposition*: they define an "attentional feature" as a linear combination of heads attending between a token pair, and an "attentional circuit" as the computation one head implements (for a one-layer model, a skip-trigram A…B → C); they deliberately choose *OV-incoherent* trigrams, ones that read from several different source tokens into one destination, because a single head's OV circuit cannot tell which token was attended and so cannot implement two such trigrams without corrupting its output. Models are trained when the ground-truth set contains more incompatible trigrams than there are heads, and their attention patterns and per-source logit edits are drawn as wiring diagrams. *Feature-manifold toy model*: features sit at evenly spaced angles φ around a circle; a data point is generated by a random angle θ and magnitude m, activating feature φ by m·cos((φ−θ)/ℓ) when the angular offset is within a quarter period in units of ℓ (otherwise zero); ℓ is then swept.

## What counts as an explanation here
Partial, provisional understanding of several forms. For dictionary learning, the proposed explanation is a *principle for choosing a decomposition* — "a feature is part of the simplest factorization of the activations" — operationalised as an information-budget criterion, so the explanation is a dictionary plus a principled stopping rule for its size; explicitly, they say a factorization producing simple-looking hypotheses is not yet evidence of "the fundamental truth" of the model. For weight and attention superposition the explanation is *algebraic*: a composition rule (Kronecker product of the two layers' superposition maps) that says which weights are representable without interference, and a statement of when a single head's mechanism must be read as a fraction of a larger one. For the manifold toy model the explanation is geometric: the model discretises a continuum into a family of localised units. Nothing here predicts network behaviour on a benchmark; the predictive content is about *what a superposed network can and cannot compute* and about the shape of its interference.

## How they judged success
Loose, appropriate to the format. For the dictionary-size criterion, the test is whether the Pareto frontier bounces at the true factor count across synthetic tasks with known ground truth, and they report that the maximum-MMCS points coincide with the bounce — MMCS being the recovery score for factorizations introduced by Sharkey et al., so the criterion is validated against a ground-truth-based metric rather than in isolation. For "feature pressure" the evidence is qualitative re-plots of polyhedra. For the attention-superposition work, the judged object is the readability of wiring diagrams across data complexity, with the authors admitting they become unreadable as the number of trigrams grows, and that the "beautiful geometry" they glimpse is unquantified. The feature-manifold note is presented as extremely preliminary with one explicit caveat that the smallest-length-scale discretisation may be an optimisation failure. No numbers, baselines, or ablations are given anywhere; the post itself invites readers to treat it as informal notes where mistakes are likely.

## What they found
On real models, they assembled enough ad-hoc evidence to conclude that extracting features from superposition is *not* low-hanging fruit, and committed to "Approach 2" — dictionary learning on activations of a dense model — with the stated alternative outcome being a significant update against that approach; spontaneously, sparse factorizations of real activations sometimes yield components that suggest simple hypotheses, but they do not accept that as evidence of ground truth. On synthetic data, the information-vs-MSE frontier's corner does track the true number of factors. On features as the simplest factorization, they note that the field lacks a satisfying definition of "feature" at all, and propose the minimum-description-length framing as a way to answer both the definition and the dictionary-size question together. On pressure, clean polytetrahedral geometry turns out to depend on how many features compete and how long training runs, not only on sparsity — more competition and longer training can create order that is absent otherwise — and they do not know why features the model ultimately declines to represent still shape the solution (hypotheses: injected noise, or effective "epsilon features"). On weight superposition, the interesting asymmetry: weights admit *constructive* interference (the Kronecker structure means some weight pairs can reinforce each other), while feature interference appears only harmful — offered as a route to reasoning about which computations are implementable under superposition. On attention superposition, when the number of OV-incoherent ground-truth trigrams exceeds the number of heads, models place each trigram across at least two heads rather than failing, so a head studied alone yields a misleading mechanism — a direct caution for head-level circuit claims. On the manifold toy model, sweeping ℓ shows the model going from many localised overlapping units (a curve-detector-like family) to a coarser arrangement as the required resolution changes, which they connect to Cammaranda-style curve detectors in vision and to the neuroscience "value coding vs variable coding" distinction, and which they float as a candidate meaning for "feature": *emergent discretisation* of an underlying continuum.

## Limits and open problems they name
They say outright that the real-model work is inconclusive and that they have no evidence the discovered factors are fundamental. The definition of "feature" is called a major open question; correlated features are flagged as a further unsolved difficulty for any factorization method. Sparse autoencoders find fewer spurious features but are hyperparameter-fragile, and they want a method with SAE-like selectivity and dictionary-learning-like trainability — an explicitly unsolved design problem at the time of writing. The information measure is admittedly provisional. The pressure results have no explanation for why unrepresented features change geometry. The attention wiring diagrams stop being readable as complexity rises, and the manifold-discretisation result may be an artefact of optimisation. The post is not a paper and makes no claims beyond preliminary interest.

## What we take from it
Two things we can use, and one framing. *Usable (a selection criterion):* the rate–distortion/Occam move — score a decomposition by reconstruction error *against* the code length needed to describe its components, and look for a corner in that frontier — is a model-free way to choose how many components a white-box account should have, and it is validated on synthetic ground truth before being applied to a real model. It converts "how many features does this network have" from an arbitrary choice into a measurable claim, which is exactly what a predictive model needs if its internal basis is going to be compared across models or checkpoints. *Usable (a coverage warning):* the attention-superposition note is the cleanest statement we have seen of why partial circuit readings are dangerous in principle, not just in practice — a set of mechanisms can be jointly encoded across heads such that each head alone looks like a partly-broken version of one mechanism, and the failure mode is silent. That is a coverage statement, and it argues for testing a candidate account with an interference-aware control rather than per-head ablations that happen to work. *Framing:* the weight-superposition algebra (U^X ⊗ U^Y, with constructive as well as destructive interference) is the natural extension of a linear-basis account to a network that computes on features it has not stored explicitly, and it explains why a linear dictionary over activations can be right about *what* is represented while being wrong about *how it is used* — the interference structure lives in the weights, so it has to be measured there. Also worth remembering: their admission that real activations are plausibly A = SD + b (offset, not zero-centred) and that this breaks the textbook pipeline — a reminder that "the offset" is a free coordinate that changes the geometry of the decomposition, and that the toy models' decision to normalise examples to unit norm quietly made memorisation easier.
