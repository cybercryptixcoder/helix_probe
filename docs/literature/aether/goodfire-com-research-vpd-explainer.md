## What they wanted, and why
The field has spent its effort on activations — the model's "thoughts" at each layer — because that is where things are readable, but reading what the model thinks at a point does not tell you which computation produced it. VPD is their bid to decompose the *parameters* instead: split the weight matrices into pieces that are simple enough to hold in one's head and specific enough to be removable, so the algorithm can be studied inside the model's own code rather than through a top-down vocabulary of concepts that an outside analyst imposes. The stated end state is deliberately ambitious: networks understood well enough that one could *design* them, with the properties one wants and not the ones one does not. The summary is positioned as the first time the lineage (they abbreviate it VPD, renaming the earlier APD/SPD line) has been run on an actual language model rather than toy models or a single matrix.

## What they studied
One 67M-parameter language model, and all 24 of its weight matrices — including the attention Q/K/V/O matrices, which is the part they emphasise, because attention decomposition is the acknowledged hard case for the field. The unit is a **rank-one matrix subcomponent**: a single outer product added into a weight matrix, so the object is not a feature in activation space, not a neuron, not a head, but a piece of the parameters that a particular token position can be shown to need. Decomposing everything yields on the order of ten thousand subcomponents, which is the claim's most striking quantitative feature: the model is being described by a dictionary of pieces that is small enough for a person to browse, in contrast to activation dictionaries whose feature counts run into the millions. Earlier Goodfire work on toy and story models is named as the antecedent but is not described.

## How they did it
Four requirements are stated as the objective, and the design follows from them literally. **(i) Completeness:** the rank-one pieces must sum exactly to the original weights, so the decomposition is a re-expression of the network rather than an approximation of part of it. **(ii) Mechanistic simplicity:** rank one, so each piece is its own tiny computation with no internal structure left to explain. **(iii) Minimal causal importance:** for any given prompt, as few pieces as possible should matter — reasoning that because a language model carries a great deal of unrelated knowledge, the piece that encodes one fact ought to be droppable on prompts that do not need it. **(iv) Behavioural fidelity:** whatever is kept must still reproduce what the model does. The awkward part is (iii), since "what matters on this prompt" is not observable, so they train an auxiliary **causal importance network** whose job is to predict, per prompt, the minimum set of subcomponents needed to reproduce the behaviour; the pieces it selects are then trained to reproduce the model's computation more faithfully, so the decomposition and the importance predictor are fitted against each other. On top of this they add an adversarial search for combinations of nominally unimportant pieces whose joint ablation *does* break the model's behaviour, which both stress-tests the pieces and stress-tests the importance predictor's claim that those pieces were irrelevant. The important consequence is structural: nothing here is a post-hoc analysis of a frozen model. The decomposition is itself a training run, with its own loss, its own adversary and its own auxiliary model, and its claims about what is causally irrelevant are the kind that can be attacked.

## What counts as an explanation here
A parameter-level partition — the weights expressed as a sum of named rank-one subcomponents, with a per-token causal importance attached to each, plus per-prompt attribution graphs over those pieces that show how much they interact on the way to a given token. It is meant to be *complete* in the weights-reconstruction sense and *minimal* in the per-prompt sense, and it makes two kinds of testable prediction: removing anything outside the claimed-important set, even adversarially, should leave behaviour intact; and rewriting a single subcomponent should change one behaviour in the direction the rewrite implies, without collateral damage. This is a stronger form of explanation than a feature dictionary, which explains the activations a model produces but not the machinery that produces them.

## How they judged success
The summary names the four desiderata and shows examples but, unlike the paper, reports **no metrics at all** in the text: no reconstruction or cross-entropy figure, no ablation-damage number, no off-target measurement, no comparison table against activation-side methods. What stands in for metrics are three worked demonstrations and one qualitative statement. The demonstrations: interpretable attention algorithms recovered in weight space, a hand edit that changes a behaviour with no training, and a per-prompt subnetwork for one next-token prediction. The qualitative statement is the comparison they choose to highlight — the single hand edit has off-target effects at a level they describe as comparable to uninterpretable fine-tuning, while conceding the fine-tuned reference still wins on raw performance. The strength of the evidence claim is therefore entirely carried by the argument that the pieces satisfy four separable constraints simultaneously, and by the reader's ability to browse the subcomponent viewer; anyone wanting effect sizes has to go to the paper.

## What they found
Subcomponents tend to come alive in coherent semantic or syntactic company, which is the interpretive claim the browsing interface is there to let one check. Decomposing all 24 matrices gives roughly ten thousand pieces — the low count being the news, since it implies the parameter side is far more parsimonious than the activation side has looked. **Attention is decomposed natively**, and this is the result worth underlining even without numbers: previous-token behaviour and a syntax-boundary routing behaviour are both recovered directly in weight space, each as a *pair* of query and key subcomponents that interact to do the job while being distributed across heads. That is exactly the computation head-by-head analysis has been unable to isolate automatically, and the authors flag, in a footnote, that such algorithms are usually spread across heads rather than living inside one. Their point about generality is that nothing in the procedure is transformer-specific. **Editing**: a subcomponent recognising the eyes of emoticons was rewritten to dump the model's unembedding row for "o", turning every emoticon into a shocked face — a prediction that came true from a local parameter edit with no gradient steps on behaviour. **Subnetworks**: for "the princess lost her crown", the graph over required subcomponents shows two readable paths converging on "her" — a femaleness signal propagated forward from "princess" through attention, and a detector for "lost" that raises the probability of object pronouns. That example is the summary's best single argument, because it is a verbal mechanistic story that a reader can check against the input.

## Limits and open problems they name
They name three, and they are the right three. Scaling is untested — one 67M model, with the explicit admission that further iteration will probably be needed at larger scale, bundled with the prediction that later versions will still resemble this one. Editing is early: the emoticon result is described as the first approach they tried, better on off-target effects than an uninterpretable fine-tune but worse on performance, so "no training required" is not yet a win on the measures that matter for editing. And the understanding of the subcomponents themselves is the open frontier — they want "deeper understandings" of individual pieces and of how pieces interact, which is an admission that finding the pieces is not yet explaining them. Mine, not theirs: a causal-importance network trained jointly with the decomposition is a second model whose errors are indistinguishable from the decomposition's, so a piece labelled unimportant on a prompt is only as trustworthy as that auxiliary predictor — and the adversary that searches for combinations of "unimportant" pieces is the right control precisely because it is a direct test of that dependency. Also mine: a decomposition validated by its own reconstruction and its own adversary is still a claim about one checkpoint, so nothing here yet bears on whether the same pieces recur across seeds or training runs — the invariance question the folder's dictionary work has learned to ask first.

## What we take from it
- **The load-bearing idea for us is the comparability of four constraints, not the pieces.** Simplicity, completeness, per-prompt minimality and behavioural fidelity are each cheap to state and each individually gameable; the claim is that a single decomposition satisfying all four at once is unlikely to be an artefact. That is a reusable standard for judging any weight-side account we produce, including our own.
- **The auxiliary predictor is the part to copy.** Making "which pieces matter here" a *learned, attackable* prediction rather than a hand-picked set converts causal importance into something that can be wrong in a checkable way. The adversary that hunts for combinations where the predictor's claim fails is the cheapest available faithfulness test for a sparse account, and it is the same reason the folder's circuit entries keep finding that importance-pruned subgraphs look faithful until stress-tested.
- **Attention decomposition is the bottleneck this addresses, and its output shape is a warning for us.** The algorithms came back as query/key *pairs spread across heads*, so any read-out of ours that assumes a head is a unit, or that assumes a circuit's members are contiguous in parameter space, is assuming away the case this work says is usual.
- **Rank one is a real constraint, not a formality.** A sum of rank-one terms is a decomposition of the weight's *action*, so the "simple piece" is simple in the functional sense rather than the storage sense. If we describe a weight matrix in any other basis we are back to an object nobody can read.
- **Framing worth borrowing for our write-up:** activations tell you what the model is computing at a position, parameters tell you what it *can* compute at all. A predictive white-box model needs both, and most of this folder has only the first half.

Cite it for the method and the three demonstrations, not for any effect size, and read `core/goodfire-research-interpreting-lm-parameters.md` for the numbers.
