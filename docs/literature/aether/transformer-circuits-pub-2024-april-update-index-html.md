## What they wanted, and why
The densest of these monthly updates: it asks whether dictionary learning behaves like any other scalable learning problem (so hyperparameters can be planned rather than guessed), whether SAE features are merely human-legible summaries or are the abstractions the model actually computes with, and whether dictionary learning can be redesigned to respect gradients as well as activations. There is also a candid essay arguing that a wrong theory can still be scientifically useful, and an announcement that the team will start building architectures chosen for legibility.

## What they studied
Scaling: SAEs on the residual stream after layer 3 of a four-layer transformer, swept over number of features and training steps. Leverage: SAEs on the residual stream after layer 3 of a four-layer transformer, perturbing features and controls. Training recipe: one-layer and four-layer models, MLP outputs and residual streams, 131,072 features, 200k steps, batch 4096, across three lambda values. Attribution dictionary learning: briefly, a one-layer transformer. The rest are reviews of other groups' models (Pythia-70M/6L, GPT2-small, GPT2-medium, LLaMA up to 30B).

## How they did it
*Scaling.* Treat MSE + 5·L1 as the objective, then sweep (features × steps) at fixed other hyperparameters and, for each compute budget, read off the lowest achievable loss and the allocation that attains it; fit power laws to loss-vs-FLOPs and to both optimal allocations. Cross-check with L0-based objectives by sweeping the L1 coefficient and picking the value that minimises the L0 objective.
*Leverage.* For each feature, take a sample of datapoints where it fires, multiply its activation by 0 or 2, propagate through the model as in *Towards Monosemanticity*, and record the average loss increase. Controls, matched in perturbation magnitude each time: a random perturbation of the residual stream; a random perturbation of the feature activation vector (a fairer baseline, since SAE directions concentrate in important dimensions); a perturbation antiparallel to the SAE reconstruction (spreading the ablation proportionally over all features); a perturbation antiparallel to the model activation (which leaves the SAE bias and reconstruction error untouched); and a perturbation along the reconstruction error direction.
*Recipe.* Just report the loss of each variant and the resulting L0, L1, normalised MSE and fraction of cross-entropy recovered, and require roughly a 1% consistent loss gain (or an equal-loss simplification) to adopt a change.
*Attribution dictionary learning.* Add two loss terms to the SAE — an L1 penalty on the feature-space attribution vector y ⊙ ∇_y L, and a penalty on the part of the activation attribution the reconstruction fails to explain (x−x')·∇_x L — where ∇_y L = Dᵀ∇_x L, so no extra backward pass through the model is needed.

## What counts as an explanation here
Four different things, which is worth separating: (1) a *scaling law* — a quantitative predictor of how loss and the compute-optimal recipe move with budget; (2) a *leverage claim* — features as high-impact levers on downstream computation, established by controlled intervention; (3) a *training recipe* — a fully specified loss, initialisation, normalisation and schedule with defaults, plus a normalisation trick that turns unconstrained decoder norms into the standard norm-1 dictionary for downstream analysis; (4) a *dual view of a feature* — sparse in representation going backwards, and asked to be sparse in attribution going forwards, i.e. a feature should be both a compact description of the input and a compact cause of the output.

## How they judged success
Loss under the chosen composite objective as proxy for dictionary quality (with the explicit caveat that it is imperfect and may break down at scale); power-law fit quality over the swept range; compute-optimality of the allocation; average loss increase per feature against five magnitude-matched perturbation controls with standard error over features; adoption of recipe changes at a ~1% loss threshold; and for outside work, their own metrics (human interp ratings, zero-ablation faithfulness and completeness for sparse feature circuits, KL divergence against norm- and angle-matched random perturbations for the reconstruction-error paper).

## What they found
Loss falls roughly as a power law in FLOPs at the compute-optimal allocation, and both allocations (features, steps) are themselves power laws; the optimal feature count grows faster than the optimal step count — steps scale with features with an exponent between one half and one — and weighting the sparsity penalty more heavily pushes the optimum toward more steps, which they explain as reconstruction being learned faster than sparsity. L0-based objectives need more steps for the same feature count, plausibly because extra steps are what zero out small activations. Feature ablation hurts the model significantly more than either random control, and more than doubling the feature by the same amount — influence appears to saturate at high activation. Spreading the same perturbation over all features proportionally hurts about as much as ablating one feature, and more than perturbing the model activation antiparallel, so the effect is not just an activation-norm effect (in later layers the model-activation version nearly vanishes). Perturbing along the reconstruction error hurts more than random but less than a feature ablation, which they read as evidence that reconstruction errors lie mostly in feature directions. The whole effect is stronger for residual-stream features than for MLP features. On the recipe, the single most important change from *Towards Monosemanticity* was dropping the unit-norm decoder constraint and weighting the sparsity penalty by each decoder column's L2 norm instead — a feature's activation is then f_i‖W_d,i‖ — which they follow with a re-parameterisation that restores norm-1 columns for analysis code; they also scale the dataset so mean activation norm equals the square root of the input dimension (making lambda comparable across model sizes), ramp lambda linearly from zero over the first 5% of training, decay the learning rate linearly over the last 20%, and report under 1% dead features with no resampling and no ghost grads. For a one-layer MLP at lambda 5 they get L0 ≈ 38.7 and 96.4% of cross-entropy recovered; at lambda 10, L0 ≈ 20.1 and 91.4%. From the reviews, the two most load-bearing points are that a small set of feature-circuit nodes removed from a 6-layer 70M-parameter Pythia can wipe out task performance while reconstruction errors are still pathological (SAE reconstructions raising KL more than matched random perturbations at every layer of GPT2-small), and that after DPO every parameter vector in a fine-tuned GPT2-medium has cosine similarity at least 0.99 with the original while toxic behaviour collapses, because many small shifts across earlier MLP neurons collectively lower the pre-activation of a few toxicity neurons — the mechanism stays in place and can be turned back on by upscaling those inputs.

## Limits and open problems they name
Scaling trends are measured on one small model and one layer and may shift with learning rate optimised jointly or with scale; the loss is a proxy and its relation to usefulness can break down. The leverage comparison controls for magnitude but the residual-stream perturbation baseline is admittedly weak, and the residual-vs-MLP difference is unexplained. The training recipe was optimised for loss and did not grapple with loss not being the objective they care about; initialisation worsens with larger hidden width, shrinkage is unresolved, and resampling "low value" features might still help. Attribution dictionary learning got one cursory one-layer check and looked roughly no better than ordinary features — they explicitly call that non-dispositive and defer it. The circuit-path algorithm's motivating cases are not enumerated, and the page is presented as lab-meeting material rather than a result.

## What we take from it
- **Measure (directly actionable):** the feature-ablation-with-controls protocol is the cheapest possible test of whether a recovered direction is a real computational lever rather than a decorative summary. Use five controls, match perturbation magnitude every time, and note that a random feature-space perturbation is the honest baseline — a random residual-stream perturbation is too easy to beat because SAE directions concentrate where it matters.
- **Prediction (a genuine regularity):** influence saturates with activation size, and ablating hurts more than an equal-magnitude amplification. Any predictive model of behaviour under intervention should be asymmetric in this way — do not assume symmetric local linearity around a feature.
- **Method/warning for our own dictionary work:** the sparsity penalty should be weighted by decoder column norm (or equivalently, feature activation should include the norm). Unconstrained decoder norms were the biggest single improvement; a norm-1 constraint changes what the objective is asking for, not just the parameterisation.
- **Framing worth stealing:** a feature has a dual nature — a representation looking backward to the input and an action looking forward to the output — and the proposal is to demand sparsity in *both* the activation and its attribution. ∇_y L = Dᵀ∇_x L makes the attribution-side gradient free once the input-side gradient is cached. This is the closest thing in the literature to training a dictionary that is, by construction, a model of causal influence rather than of reconstruction.
- **Warning (faithfulness of reconstruction):** an SAE can reconstruct well and still change the model's distribution more than random noise of the same size. Never treat low reconstruction error as evidence the dictionary is faithful; test by substitution and measure KL against matched controls.
- **Warning (weights):** cosine-0.99 parameter vectors can hide a total behavioural change, and the mechanism survives. Reading weights for behaviour is therefore not optional — it is the only way to know that a fine-tune suppressed rather than removed something.
- **Framing (on being wrong):** the caloric-theory essay is a useful licence — a wrong abstraction (superposition) can still yield durable tools, as Carnot's cycle came out of a wrong theory of heat. Build the tool, but design the experiment that could kill it.
