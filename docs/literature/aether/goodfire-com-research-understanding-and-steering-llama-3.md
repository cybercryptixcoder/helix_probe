# Understanding and Steering Llama 3 with Sparse Autoencoders

- **Date:** 2024-09-25

- **Read from:** full text — the whole prose body, all inline formulas, safety section, acknowledgements, citation and reference/footnote blocks (~25.8k chars after tag stripping). **No figure inspected**; the only figure in the body is the pipeline diagram described in prose, so no plotted value is quoted anywhere in this entry. - **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The post is the public launch of a chat interface in which a user can watch a sparse-autoencoder (SAE) dictionary over one model's residual stream and change feature values mid-generation. The stated motivation is a claim about how the field's knowledge should be transferred: interpretability had existed mainly as papers and feature browsers, and the authors argue that the only way to see a technique's strengths and weaknesses is to put it in people's hands under normal use. Read as research rather than as marketing, the post is an argument that *intervention quality*, not interpretability labels, is the property that makes a dictionary useful — the demo exists so that a human can attempt a behavioural edit and watch it succeed or fail. It is a step toward treating a dictionary as an editable handle on a deployed chat model rather than as a catalogue of descriptions.

## What they studied
Llama-3-8B-Instruct, with a single SAE trained on the residual stream at layer 19. The unit is the SAE feature (a latent in a width-expanded ReLU hidden layer). The post gives the architecture family but not the width, expansion factor, feature count, training-token count or training hyperparameters; it commits only to an L1 penalty coefficient tuned by hand and otherwise "the same settings as the Anthropic April update", a reference rather than a number. The intervention surface is a single layer at every token position, plus a gradient-attribution score used to choose which features to offer the user.

## How they did it
Activations are harvested from the instruct model by running text through it and buffering per-token vectors; the buffer is shuffled before writing to disk so that training batches do not inherit autocorrelation from document order. The SAE is a two-layer ReLU autoencoder (reconstruction plus L1 sparsity), and the authors say explicitly that they chose this over TopK and JumpReLU architectures on purpose — those variants looked like they produced very high-frequency features, and investigating that properly would have delayed the launch, so simplicity won over the newer architectures. Training data is LMSYS-Chat-1M, chosen because the product is a chat product; the authors report that web-text and mixed-source dictionaries produced more diverse features but many more dead and near-dead features, and that the features they did get were worse intervention targets. For interventions they substitute the SAE's reconstruction for the true activation and edit the latent vector before decoding, which leaves the reconstruction error term untouched and therefore, in their words, everything the SAE has not captured is unaltered. Candidate features are ranked by backpropagated gradient of a single output token's logit minus the mean logit, summed across token positions; they report that summing is more reliable than taking a maximum or keeping positions separate. Interventions are scaled to lie between the maximum latent value observed in the autointerp data and its negative, and a negative setting is described as "blocking" the feature even though the encoder itself cannot emit a negative value.

## What counts as an explanation here
Two distinct objects, and the post is clear about the difference. The first is a natural-language label per feature, produced by an automated pipeline: representative activating examples sampled across the activation distribution, not only the maximum, are handed to Claude, which is asked what they have in common. The second, and the one the product is built around, is a *ranked, editable feature list per output token* — a partial causal attribution rather than a description of the model. Nothing here is claimed to be complete: the dictionary is one layer of one model, the attribution is a first-order gradient signal at a single token, and the authors' own phenomenology section is essentially a list of ways the edit fails. Prediction is present in the sense that an edit is a commitment about what the output will do, but the post reports no prediction that was scored.

## How they judged success
Partly by explicit measures, partly by inspection. (i) Standard SAE scalars are tracked — average L0 as an exponential moving average over batches, and *fidelity*, defined as the fraction of the damage from zeroing the activation entirely that the SAE's own reconstruction recovers: (loss with SAE − loss clean) / (loss ablated − loss clean). The authors note, correctly, that L0 says nothing by itself about interpretability. The fidelity definition is the single most reusable formula in the post, because it is an ablation-normalised score and therefore comparable across dictionaries of different widths. (ii) Automated-interp scoring is contrastive: an explanation must pick the true activating sample out of a distractor set, and a harder variant requires distinguishing a feature from the nearest neighbours of its label in embedding space over repeated trials. They note the contrastive form weakly penalises polysemanticity, which makes it usable for comparing dictionaries as well as explainers. (iii) Interventions are judged qualitatively, by whether the visible output change is the one intended while the rest of the response stays coherent — no metric, no baseline, no random-direction control. (iv) Data-source choice is evaluated by counting dead and low-frequency features, again without published numbers.

## What they found
No numeric results are reported for any of the metrics; the post's findings are qualitative and are stated as such. The training-data finding is the strongest: a chat-format corpus beat web text and mixed web/chat corpora not only on feature diversity but on intervention suitability, and the authors' explanation is that behaviourally relevant features often sit on chat-specific special tokens — for example the features that encode what style of response is expected — which only chat data exposes. Their conjecture for why diverse mixes underperform is a training-time-shortfall story: a feature becomes interpretable before it becomes a good intervention target, so holding dataset size fixed while raising diversity leaves each feature undertrained. Two intervention phenomena are named: first, features split across layers (cross-layer superposition) will only be partly edited from a single-layer dictionary, so the untouched remainder is computed downstream, which they offer as an explanation for why visually similar features differ so much in editability; second, repeated near-duplicate or *echo* features, which L1 tolerates because two half-strength copies cost the same penalty as one full-strength feature, mean that a positive edit usually works with one feature while a negative edit typically needs several set to negative. The mechanism they propose for that asymmetry is self-repair at the level of the behaviour: an added feature can be amplified downstream while a single removed one can be restored.

## Limits and open problems they name
They name: the automated labelling pipeline failing on the most influential features, which tend to sit on punctuation, special tokens and turn boundaries, where the describer reports the token rather than the context the token carries; the architecture choice being a time-driven bet rather than a measured one, with the TopK/JumpReLU high-frequency concern left uninvestigated; single-layer superposition leaving part of any feature out of reach; and intervention dynamics, i.e. why echoes and self-repair behave as they do, described as an open problem. Limits I would add, not theirs: every intervention result in the post is reported as a demonstration, with no pre-registered success criterion, no random-feature control and no measure of output degradation outside the word "minimizing"; the attribution loss is a single-token margin, so features that shift *distributions* rather than the top token are invisible to it by construction — the same reason they chose it over log-probability applies against them; fidelity is measured with the SAE in place, which prices the dictionary's own reconstruction rather than its behavioural coverage; and the 2026 deprecation notice means the reported interface behaviour cannot now be re-checked by a reader.

## What we take from it
Four things. (1) A definition to import: ablation-normalised fidelity as the primary dictionary-quality number, and dereference the L0-as-interpretability belief the post itself gently doubts. (2) The mismatch the post stumbles on — features are interpretable before they are intervenable — is a warning for us in a specific form: a dictionary can pass a labelling or contrastive-explanation test while its directions are useless as handles, so any claim of the shape "we have the feature for X" must be paired with an edit that moves behaviour, and the two properties should be measured separately rather than assumed to come together. (3) The positive/negative asymmetry is a design constraint on our experiments: single-feature suppression is not the mirror image of single-feature amplification, so a null suppression result needs an inventory of how many directions were set negative, and a positive result from one feature cannot be generalised into "the behaviour is caused by this feature". (4) The correction they make to attribution needs copying exactly: summing gradient across token positions beat max-pooling and per-position display, and subtracting the mean logit removes features that raise every token equally — both are cheap, both are stated as findings, and both are the kind of detail that determines whether an attribution list is meaningful at all.
