## What they wanted, and why

The team had been selecting SAEs on a Pareto frontier of reconstruction error against L0, with no independent check that a better frontier point is a more interpretable dictionary. Outside groups (Gao et al., Rajamanoharan et al., Jermyn et al.) had begun shipping variants that sit strictly better on that frontier, largely by removing shrinkage or by optimising L0 more directly, and Anthropic had no way to tell a genuine improvement from a variant that wins the proxy and loses the thing the proxy stands for. The month's goal is therefore to turn "is this dictionary interpretable?" into a number that can be compared across dictionaries. Two evals are built, they are validated against the one signal the team already trusts (evaluation loss under changes in feature count and training tokens), the surviving three metrics are used to referee six SAE variants, and a third short note asks the opposite question: can the model itself say what a feature means, rather than a second model reading its activations?

## What they studied

One medium-sized, non-frontier production model (size not given), SAEs of roughly 260,000 features trained on 400 million tokens, with three hyperparameters swept: feature count, training tokens, and the sparsity coefficient. Checkpoints from a full-length run are reused when sweeping tokens, without re-cooling the learning-rate schedule. Six SAE variants are compared on the same model and dataset: vanilla L1 (the April 2024 recipe), TopK without auxiliary loss, TopK with the post-training top-k replaced by a fixed activation threshold that yields k active features on average, Gated, Tanh, and JumpReLU. All judging is done by Claude 3 Haiku; the self-explanation note uses an 18-layer model and Claude 3 Sonnet. Unit of analysis: the feature (a dictionary direction) and, in the third note, the residual stream position where its decoder vector is injected.

## How they did it

**Contrastive eval.** A hand-written list of a few dozen concepts spanning concrete nouns, rhetorical devices, code constructs, scientific ideas and social situations is turned into positive/negative prompt pairs by asking the model for two near-identical sentences that differ only in whether the concept is present, with the explicit instruction that all other content be preserved across both. The dictionary is run on both prompts, features active on exactly one of them are collected, and Claude is then given the pair plus that feature's own max-activating dataset examples — fragments with the top firing tokens and their scores — and asked which of the two prompts fired the feature. Two aggregations are recorded: an unweighted accuracy, and an accuracy weighted by the feature's activation on its firing prompt, normalised by its maximum over the training corpus, so that strongly firing features count more.

**Sort eval.** Two features are chosen either at random or adversarially — one random, then its nearest neighbour by decoder-vector cosine, which is the harder case because related features in a dictionary cluster semantically while splitting contexts. Claude sees the top ten examples per feature, formatted with per-token activation information, plus a query example known to fire one of the two, and must name which. This tests monosemanticity (do the strongest examples generalise to other examples) and resolution (can two cosine-adjacent features be told apart at all).

**Validation of the evals themselves.** After iterating on the prompt templates, the authors check that human labellers also struggle on the cases Claude gets wrong, that the chain of thought reads sensibly, that scores rise with training steps and with dictionary size, and that scores track evaluation loss. Then, for the variant comparison, sparsity coefficients are swept with features and tokens held fixed, and each variant's MSE is plotted against each surviving metric.

**Self-explaining features.** Reproducing the SelfIE/Patchscopes style approach: take a prompt of the form "The meaning of the word X is", inject the feature's decoder vector (optionally scaled) at the X position, and sample a multi-token completion. This substitutes a generator for a judge: nothing reads the activation, the model's own continuation is the explanation.

## What counts as an explanation here

Two different things, worth keeping apart. The evals' explanation is a *behavioural test of a dictionary entry*: a feature is explained to the extent that a reader given its examples can predict where it fires, and the evals produce a number, not a description. Self-explaining features produce the older currency — a short natural-language gloss — but generated by the network rather than by an interpreter of the network, which makes it a weaker but more independent artefact: nobody has to trust the autointerp model's reading of the examples. Neither form is a model of the computation, and neither predicts anything under intervention. The case study's own framing is explicitly comparative: the evals' job is not to be right in absolute terms but to have enough resolution to rank variants, and if a metric cannot separate two variants, the authors say so and decline to interpret the gap.

## How they judged success

The evals are judged by their correlation with evaluation loss (reconstruction plus sparsity) across two sweeps — feature count and token count — with the strong prior that lower evaluation loss means better features; three metrics survive that screen (sort eval on random pairs, contrastive eval weighted, and a third not named in the prose around the plots). The variant comparison is judged by the whole MSE-versus-metric tradeoff curve rather than a single point, plus L0. Named sanity checks for the evals: human disagreement on Claude's errors, chain-of-thought plausibility, monotonicity in steps and in feature count, and loss correlation. Reported as an honest negative: JumpReLU could not be reproduced as better than TopK/Gated on the L0-MSE frontier, despite correspondence with the authors and a reimplementation on Anthropic's own infrastructure except for model and dataset, with a bug in their code, a difference in the model, or something else all left on the table. The one non-external-replication concession is stated too: they applied each paper's headline change to their own hyperparameters rather than copying each setup.

## What they found

The headline is that the variants are real: every non-vanilla SAE beat vanilla on the validated metrics at matched MSE, which both derisks switching recipes and validates the eval suite by showing it recovers an ordering the loss-based prior also endorses. But the variants are nearly indistinguishable *from each other* on these metrics, and the authors read that as a resolution limit of the evals rather than a tie — they explicitly warn against over-interpreting small score differences and suggest that when the metrics are this close, engineering properties (ease of use, robustness under scaling, how easily a circuit can be read out of the dictionary) should decide. On the tanh penalty, the February update's embarrassing result is withdrawn in effect: the pathological population of very dense, uninterpretable features no longer appears, tanh now produces about the same number of high-density features as the other variants, and those features are about as interpretable as everyone else's; the team's best guess is that the intervening changes to SAE training fixed it, and they say plainly that they ran no ablations to find out which. On self-explanation, the method works well where features are early and simple — a first-layer "big" feature injected at high scale completes the sentence with the word "big", and a "sport" feature yields a definition of sport as bodily activity — and degrades with complexity and with scale. An early-layer "Jordan" feature produced nothing legible at low multipliers and only "greatest of all time" at a multiplier of 410, never the name; a layer-4 Jordan feature produced the name at high scale but degenerated into speculative word-splitting at 260. On Claude 3 Sonnet the successes are partial readings of the wrong sense: a spreadsheet-related "sheet" feature explained itself as a thin flat material without any spreadsheet content, and a "(" token in a programming context as "to tie or fasten". Larger models succeed less often and are more scale-sensitive. One feature that the Claude-based autointerp tool labelled usefully (a layer-3 feature on "of" in colour contexts, labelled with colour/shade/pigment words) could not be explained by injection at any scale or prompt tried, which the authors offer as possible evidence that injection reaches an aspect the visualisation cannot show.

## Limits and open problems they name

The evals' resolution is the main one, stated as a finding: they cannot separate variants that differ only slightly, and building better SAE evals is called an active research area. The concept list is a manual rough first pass, and the contrastive eval depends on the model's ability to write a minimal pair — a task where a small wording difference can smuggle in a correlate of the concept. The weighting scheme privileges strong activations, so it measures interpretability where features fire hardest. The correlation-with-loss validation assumes the loss-to-quality prior that the whole exercise exists to check. The JumpReLU non-replication is left undiagnosed. For self-explanation: success is scale- and layer-dependent, inconsistent between features of the same concept, better on the small model than on Sonnet, and it fails outright on some features that a separate interpreter handled fine — so the two methods are complementary rather than one being the truth.

## What we take from it

- **A reusable eval design for any claim that a direction means a concept (method).** The minimal-pair construction is the important part: generate two prompts that differ only in the presence of the concept, find directions that discriminate them, then test whether a reader given the direction's examples can name which side fires. Our equivalent question — do we predict behaviour, not just discriminate text — can hang off the same scaffold, with a held-out pair set and the semantics of "only one of these fires" kept strictly separate from any prediction of the downstream logit.
- **The nearest-neighbour hard case (measure).** Sampling the second feature by decoder cosine rather than at random converts "is my direction interpretable" into "is my direction finer than its neighbour", which is the version of the question a split dictionary makes hard. Any dictionary we compare across sizes should be scored on the hard case, or it will reward over-splitting, which is exactly the failure mode flagged in June's IOI critique.
- **A warning about the tie (warning).** Two dictionaries whose summary scores are within noise are not equal but indistinguishable by the instrument; the honest report is "this eval cannot separate them" plus a different criterion, not a decimal-place ranking. This applies directly to any composite metric we invent.
- **An independent explanation route with a known failure shape (shortcut and warning).** Injecting the decoder vector and letting the model talk is cheap and needs no judge model, so it is a useful cross-check against our own autointerp — but its reliability is monotonic in layer and inverse in model size, and it silently yields the wrong sense of a polysemous feature rather than failing loudly. Treat a self-explanation as a hypothesis about a feature, never as its label, and check whether it changes under a scale sweep, because scale sensitivity is itself diagnostic.
- **The retracted February warning (framing).** The tanh-penalty density pathology was not a property of the penalty but of the training setup around it, and the team does not know which change fixed it. Any of our own "this objective is pathological" findings should be dated and re-tested after the surrounding recipe changes, since a penalty and its training context are not separable in the evidence we usually collect.
