## What they wanted, and why

A separate safety classifier that guards a model doubles the inference bill — their motivating number is that using Claude 3.5 Haiku to filter Claude 3.5 Sonnet adds roughly a quarter to cost. Since the policy model has already computed the representations needed to understand the prompt, the authors ask whether a monitor can be *branched off* the model instead of being a second model: either a linear probe on intermediate activations, or the model's last few layers retrained to emit a verdict token. The larger goal is to make white-box monitoring cheap enough that deploying it is not a trade-off against serving cost, which is the practical form of the argument that internal readouts beat output monitors.

## What they studied

The Claude 3 family: a large ("XL") policy model whose size and architecture are not disclosed, plus smaller in-family models (L/M/S/XS) as compute-matched baselines at roughly 25%, 9%, 2% and 0.6% of the XL cost. Task: prompt-only detection of bioweapon-related content, so the classifier sees exactly the tokens the policy model sees and can share its forward pass. Unit of analysis: a pooled activation vector at one layer (probes), or the output of the final k layers followed by an answer token (partially trained classifiers).

## How they did it

Training data is the synthetic pipeline from Constitutional Classifiers: about 70,000 prompts balanced between bioweapon-elicitation attempts and harmless text, generated by Claude 3.5 models against a written constitution and then passed through transformations such as jailbreak templates, other languages, ciphers, and assistant pre-fills. Three probe variants are fit by L2-regularised logistic regression on standardised activations: a mean-over-tokens probe (one vector per sequence, same pooling at test time); an EMA probe (train on the mean vector, but at test time score every token separately and take the max of an exponential moving average over the token-score sequence, α = 0.5, so a short dangerous span is not diluted by a long benign context); and a suffix probe (append a 150-token instruction asking whether the dialog contains harmful biological information, and read the activation at the position just after it, which makes the model aggregate the evidence itself). Partially trained classifiers reuse the whole frozen backbone and fine-tune only the last 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 or 32 layers to complete the same suffix with "harmful" or "not harmful" via next-token prediction, with the learning rate scaled inversely to the number of trained layers. Baselines: the in-family models L–XS fine-tuned on the same data, and the XL model simply prompted with the suffix (zero training). Two-stage systems put one of these cheap classifiers first, pass only inputs above a threshold to the fixed full XL classifier, and the cost is written as stage-1 cost plus the fraction of traffic reaching stage 2.

## What counts as an explanation here

No mechanism is claimed — this is an engineering paper, and the object produced is a *detector*: a linear direction with a pooling rule, or a shallow head on a frozen trunk. The paper's explanatory content is the cost/performance frontier and the claim that a small readout on a large model's internal state encodes the same decision that a much larger dedicated model learns from scratch. The interesting implicit claim is a replacement one: the reused representation is a sufficient statistic for the classification, so the classifier need not be a full second model.

## How they judged success

Log-spaced AUROC — the area under TPR vs FPR with FPR sampled between 1e-3 and 1e-1 — chosen explicitly because ordinary AUROC is insensitive at the low-FPR end where a deployed monitor actually operates, and single-point TPR is too noisy. Held-out evaluation includes roughly 4,000 transformed dangerous questions and about 2,500 red-team prompts that actually succeeded against the policy model, against harmless traffic from single-turn WildChat and the hard subset of OR-bench (the latter inflating false positives on purpose). Trained-model scores are averaged over data orderings; probes and baselines are compared after interpolating to a common cost, and validation loss is plotted against log-space AUROC as a separate diagnostic.

## What they found

Retraining one transformer block at the end of the XL model lands second overall, level with the L baseline while costing about 4% of the policy model including suffix tokens; the EMA probe matches an interpolated ~3%-overhead classifier, the suffix probe roughly 4%, and a mean-pooled probe is worse than even the smallest dedicated model — so *how* token scores are aggregated is again the decisive choice, not the classifier. Adding more retrained layers (2 through 16) does not help beyond noise. The partially trained models have validation loss like models that perform far worse, yet generalise better out of distribution: the authors read this as evidence the frozen large backbone is doing work that the loss curve does not show. In two-stage pipelines the EMA probe and the one-layer model beat all small-model first stages, cutting total cost by more than 10x with no meaningful loss; there is even a threshold band where the cascade scores *above* the strong classifier alone, which they attribute to uncorrelated errors screening away false positives, with the warning that an attacker who can satisfy stage one gets a free pass.

## Limits and open problems they name

No adaptive red-teaming: red-teamers attacked Claude 3.5 without knowledge of these detectors, and probes live inside the very model being attacked, so their robustness under a direct attack is genuinely unknown — stated as a precondition for deployment. Costs are illustrative because architecture cannot be disclosed. The small-model baselines were trained on data volumes proportional to their size, so a properly over-trained small model could rebalance the frontier. Cascades open a new attack surface (convince the weak stage and the strong stage never looks). Multi-stage design space is barely explored — score combination, or a three-way harmless/harmful/unsure first stage, are left open.

## What we take from it

Four things. (1) A **compute-normalised comparison** is the honest way to present a readout: plot log-space AUROC against FLOP overhead and interpolate, rather than comparing a probe to a full classifier at the same accuracy. (2) **Log-space AUROC over 1e-3–1e-1 FPR** is the metric to adopt for every monitor we report, matching what we already believe about operating points. (3) **Pooling rules are hypotheses to sweep**: mean pooling destroys the signal in long context, per-token scoring plus a max-over-EMA recovers it, and a suffix token is a way to hand the aggregation to the model itself — three cheap variants of the same probe that differ by more than the model does. (4) A **validation loss is not a predictor of readout quality**: a classifier built on a frozen large trunk can be "bad at its training objective" and still generalise best, which is a caution for using loss to select where we read from. Also a warning to reuse: intra-model monitors are inside the attack surface, so any claim of robustness needs an attacker who knows the readout exists.
