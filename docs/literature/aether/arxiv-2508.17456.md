# Adversarial Examples Are Not Bugs, They Are Superposition

- **Key:** arxiv-2508.17456
- **Authors / organisation:** Liv Gorton, Owen Lewis — Goodfire (San Francisco)
- **Date:** 2025-08-24 (v1), 2025-09-13 (v2); arXiv:2508.17456v2
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17456 (Goodfire index page https://www.goodfire.com/research/adversarial-examples-are-not-bugs-they-are-superposition redirects here)
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text — arXiv HTML v2 (~46k chars): main body §1–§6 (Introduction, Background, §3 causal evidence in toy models incl. §3.1 setup, §3.2, §3.3, §3.4 geometry, §4 real models incl. §4.1–§4.3, Discussion, Related Work), Appendices A–C, References and the short Revision History note. **No figure inspected** — every number below is either a prose sentence or a Table 2 cell; the superposition graphs, interference heatmaps, the superposition-number-line plot and the two curve panels in Figures 2 and 7 are known only from their captions and the prose that explains them.
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Interference between non-orthogonal feature directions is what superposition costs, and the original toy-models work suggested in passing that this interference could be what an adversarial attack exploits. Nobody had tested that suggestion. The authors ask whether superposition is not merely correlated with adversarial vulnerability but *causally* responsible for it, and they push the stronger claim too — that superposition is potentially sufficient to explain six well-known phenomena at once (existence, noise-like perturbation structure, transferability between independently trained models, the difficulty of adversarial training, the interpretability of robust models, and the finding that training on attacks transfers to clean performance). The larger point for them is a trade-off claim: if robustness requires un-superposing features, then interpretability and robustness are aimed at the same target, and the growing interpretability toolkit becomes a robustness tool. For our survey the paper matters for a different reason: it is the clearest published case of a mechanistic-interpretability concept being used as an *explanatory variable for a failure mode* rather than as a description, and it shows exactly how much causal evidence is available when the underlying quantity cannot be intervened on in a real model.

## What they studied
Two settings. In toy models: the standard 100-feature → 20-hidden-unit → ReLU-reconstruction setup with uniform feature importance and mean-squared-error loss, swept over feature sparsity S (feature density exponentially from 1.0 down to 0.1, 30 models trained simultaneously at different sparsity). In real models: adversarially trained ResNet18s on ImageNet taken from Salman et al. (2020), at several L2 perturbation sizes (ε ∈ {0, 1, 3, 5}), with sparse autoencoders fitted to the outputs of the four residual stages (conv2_x through conv5_x: 256, 512, 1024 and 2048 channels). The unit of analysis flips between the two halves: in the toy model it is the *feature geometry* (the Gram-like matrix of pairwise direction dot products, and features-per-dimension as a scalar count of superposition); in ResNet18 it is the layer's feature maps as seen through an SAE (its reconstruction loss at fixed sparsity, and the L0 of the sparse code).

## How they did it
1. **Toy-model instrument check.** Vary feature density, measure features-per-dimension (k/n, the count of features represented per available dimension) to confirm the manipulation moves superposition as expected, and reproduce the original phase transitions at roughly 1.7 and 4 features per dimension, including a plateau where the model sits in an antipodal arrangement.
2. **Superposition → robustness.** At each density, construct L2-bounded adversarial examples by finding the worst perturbation inside an ε-ball that maximises reconstruction error; the main results use a one-step gradient attack with a little added noise to avoid ReLU gradient masking, and an Appendix A replication uses the original paper's analytic per-feature attacks. Vulnerability is reported as a multiple of the vulnerability of the no-superposition model (the highest-density model, where every feature appears in every input). Design defence: because the density sweep also changes the evaluation distribution, the authors argue the confound runs *against* their trend, since fewer active features should make a model easier rather than harder to fool this way.
3. **Robustness → superposition.** Train fresh toy models at the same densities with a mixed objective, half clean and half adversarial, using an L2 attack at ε = 0.1‖x‖₂, for 150,000 steps, then re-measure superposition and vulnerability.
4. **Geometry.** Draw each toy model's interference graph with edges weighted by the squared dot product between feature directions, and compare clean-input versus adversarial-input highlighted subgraphs, plus the full interference matrices for a robust/non-robust pair.
5. **Real-model proxy for robustness → superposition.** With no way to intervene on superposition in ResNet18, they use the SAE loss as the proxy: train L1-ReLU and TopK SAEs (TopK k ∈ {8, 16, 32}, expansion 8×) on standardised activations from each stage, and check whether reconstruction error at matched sparsity improves as the model gets more robust.
6. **Real-model proxy for the other direction.** The same SAEs give a second read-out: if attacks work through interference, adversarial inputs should activate *more* features. They compare L0 under FGSM and PGD against clean inputs, per layer, over 100,000 images per condition.

## What counts as an explanation here
A causal claim with an explicitly named missing leg. The explanation is: superposition (non-orthogonal directions plus negative biases that suppress on-distribution interference) is the mechanism, and worst-case interference accumulates across layers because each layer's corrupted activations become the next layer's input. It is meant to be *sufficient-in-principle but partially-evidenced*: the authors claim superposition can account for all six phenomena they tabulate, while conceding that in real models they can establish only one direction of causation and only through proxies. There is no quantitative prediction of a specific perturbation, and the paper never claims to predict the magnitude of an adversarial example — so in our vocabulary this is a causal-abstraction argument for a failure mode, not a predictive white-box model.

## How they judged success
No single headline metric; the evidence is a set of dependencies. In the toy models: (a) a monotone relationship between features-per-dimension and vulnerability multiple, with the appendix reporting a linear correlation between superposition and vulnerability of r ≈ 0.99 (p < 0.0001) across the density sweep, and (b) the reverse experiment where adversarial training lowers features-per-dimension while robustness rises. Controls are indirect rather than matched: a no-superposition reference model for the vulnerability multiple, the argument that the changed evaluation distribution biases against the observed trend, and cross-validation from the robust models, which shift superposition separately from the data distribution and reproduce the same trend. The geometry section is qualitative. In ResNet18: SAE reconstruction error at fixed sparsity, compared across ε values, with the authors' own alternative-explanation search stated as "we have no hypothesis for another change that would lower SAE loss independent of superposition"; and L0 ratios (means ± SEM) for clean versus FGSM versus PGD inputs across the five layer positions. No seed variance, no confidence intervals, no random-direction or norm-matched control on the L0 comparison.

## What they found
- **Toy model, both directions hold.** Vulnerability rises with feature sparsity and with features-per-dimension, with one conspicuous dip exactly where the models sit in the antipodal superposition arrangement. Training adversarially reduces features-per-dimension for a given input sparsity and reduces vulnerability — the authors say this is the first demonstration of causation running from robustness to superposition.
- **The claim with an arm attached: robust models are more robust than their superposition level alone predicts,** which the authors read as evidence that the *structure* of the superposition (the interference matrix) matters, not just its amount.
- **Geometry differs in exactly the expected way.** Non-robust toy models show sparse, low-interference activation for clean inputs but dense interfering activation under attack, whereas robust models look similarly sparse in both conditions; the mean off-diagonal interference for non-robust models is about twice that of robust ones. Robust models at matched sparsity retain strikingly similar graph structure while sitting at a lower superposition level on the paper's number line.
- **ResNet18, robustness → superposition.** At matched sparsity, more robust models reconstruct better across TopK settings; the authors take lower MSE at fixed sparsity as evidence of less interference and therefore less superposition. This is the only real-model result for that direction and it rests entirely on the SAE-loss proxy.
- **ResNet18, attacks activate more features.** FGSM raises L0 modestly, roughly 1.3× at conv1, holding near that through the middle stages and falling back to nearly baseline at the last stage; PGD rises less at the front (1.1–1.3×) and then jumps to about 2.8× at layer4. From Table 2 the means go from 126.0 (clean) to 128.1 (FGSM) and 351.4 (PGD) at layer4, so the PGD effect is large and the FGSM effect at that layer is essentially nil. The reading offered is that iterative attacks exploit interference accumulated in deeper representations.
- **A stated trade-off.** If superposition is a primary cause, capacity and robustness are in tension — superposition is what lets a network behave like a much larger sparse model — and adversarial training buys robustness by giving that up.

## Limits and open problems they name
They name two. First, the analysis leans heavily on proxies, especially for real models, and those proxies may not capture the phenomenon. Second, without a way to intervene on superposition in a real model they can establish only that the causal relationship is possible, not how much of adversarial vulnerability it accounts for; and the real-model results are compatible with superposition being one of several causes. Two unexpected findings are handed to future work: the temporary robustness improvement at antipodal superposition, and the fact that models with equal superposition but different structure differ in robustness.

What I would add from reading it:
- **The Figure 6 numbers were once wrong.** The paper publishes a revision-history note saying a plotting bug in that code had been *exaggerating* the clean-versus-adversarial gap and has since been fixed. The prose and Table 2 as read agree with each other (2.79× at layer4), but the digits reported in the text are described as calculated from n = 100,000 images with error bars around 0.1 or smaller on means of order 100, which makes the SEMs implausibly tiny unless they are computed over something other than image-to-image variation. A reader should treat the precision as unverified.
- **FGSM at the last stage contradicts the narrative smoothly.** The prose says adversarial examples "consistently activate more features across all layers", but its own number for FGSM at layer4 is 1.02×, i.e. no effect; the interesting result is specific to PGD and to depth.
- **The SAE-loss proxy has a plausible rival the paper dismisses too quickly.** Adversarially trained networks are known to have smoother, lower-frequency features; smoother features are easier for a fixed-size dictionary to model regardless of how much superposition is present. "We have no hypothesis" is not a control.
- **One direction of the toy-model result is confounded by the training objective itself.** Adversarial training minimises worst-case interference by construction, so "it reduces superposition" is close to restating the objective, and the interesting content is in the other experiment (sparsity → vulnerability).
- **The multiple used as the vulnerability metric is defined against a model trained on a different data distribution,** so part of the growth may be a distribution-shift penalty rather than superposition.

## What we take from it
A framing, a caution, and two measures.
- **Framing (adopt):** interference is the object a mechanistic account can *predict with* rather than describe. The paper's structure — measure the geometry, show the failure moves with it, then show the intervention intended to fix the failure moves the geometry back — is the causal-abstraction ladder we want for any of our own claims, and it is worth copying with the missing arms filled in.
- **Caution (important for us):** this is what a mechanistic explanation looks like when the mediating quantity is unobservable in the target model. Everything real-model in it is a proxy, and the two proxies are the SAE loss and the SAE's L0. If our white-box model is going to make causal claims about a trained network, we need to know which of our quantities are interventions and which are proxies, and we should say so in the same sentence as the claim.
- **Measure (copy):** features-per-dimension as a scalar superposition count is only available in toy models, but the *interference matrix* — pairwise squared dot products between units or dictionary atoms, and specifically its mean off-diagonal magnitude — is computable in any model with a dictionary, and here it separates robust from non-robust models by about a factor of two. That is a cheap geometry statistic worth reporting next to any robustness or steering result.
- **Measure (copy, with a control):** the L0 of a sparse code under clean versus perturbed input is a nice one-pass attack-detection statistic — but the paper's own FGSM-at-layer4 null says it is attack- and depth-specific, so anyone reusing it should report the attack type and add a norm-matched random perturbation as the control the paper lacks.
- **Warning:** the erratum. A published curve was wrong in its own published direction. Any number we lift from a figure should be marked as such, and any number we publish should be reproducible from a table.
