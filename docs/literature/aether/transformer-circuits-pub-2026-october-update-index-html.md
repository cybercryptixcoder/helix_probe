## What they wanted, and why
The previous workspace paper (Gurnee et al., which this folder reads as `anthropic-com-research-global-workspace`) introduced the J-lens as a way to read the verbalisable part of an activation, but its readouts only became coherent in the back half of the layers and it plainly missed intermediate variables that later reasoning demonstrably used. The authors list four explanations for the misses — the workspace idea is wrong, the variables are not linearly represented, the model does not represent them at all, or the lens itself is a bad instrument — and set out to test the last one, because if it is the lens then the readout can be repaired and the workspace programme survives. The larger goal is a monitoring surface: they want a reliable linear readout of unverbalised reasoning for eval awareness and scheming, precisely because models are increasingly doing work without writing it out. A second, methodological goal is to say what *faithfulness* means for a lens, and they define it as two things at once: readouts surface the intermediate variables the model actually uses, and the lens vectors act as those variables when patched into the model.

## What they studied
Six open-weight models from 9B to 284B parameters: Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.6-27B (the default for most experiments), Gemma 4 31B, Olmo 3 32B, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash (a 284B mixture-of-experts model). Unit of analysis is a direction in the residual stream indexed by a vocabulary token — the rows of an averaged Jacobian composed with the unembedding, which they call lens vectors — one linear map fitted per readout layer, with seven equally spaced readout layers and the final block as target. Fitting data is unlabelled text (WikiText-103, 64 sequences of 128 tokens, first 16 positions skipped for attention sinks, ~7.1k fit positions total), so the object is a fitted operator rather than a trained feature dictionary. Evaluations are five latent-variable-extraction tasks adapted from the earlier paper (multihop, multilingual, typo, association, poetry) plus an intermediate-swap causal task.

## How they did it
The baseline J-lens averages the Jacobian of future-token logits with respect to an intermediate activation over many source positions, weighting every position equally. The paper first argues that this average is corrupted by *biased* noise: unbiased noise cancels under averaging, but gradients from some source activations are systematically wrong, and their norms grow towards early layers. Three changes follow. (1) **Jacobian Filtering**: cluster the activation space at each layer with k-means (8 clusters per layer, on 64-dimensional PCA projections of unit-normalised activations), fit one "Expert Jacobian" per cluster, then combine the experts with learned weights fitted on a small dev set of extraction problems — in practice the weights act as an include/exclude switch, so the mixture is still a single linear map. (2) **LRP backward pass**: compute each Jacobian with layer-wise relevance propagation instead of the plain gradient, following the R-lens; reuse the LN-rule on the RMSNorms, identity/half rules on the gated MLPs, plain gradients elsewhere, freeze MoE routing and residual-mixing coefficients. (3) **Readout Filtering**: drop the 6,116 tokens that contain no letter or digit from the readout vocabulary, because punctuation and whitespace crowd the top of the lists (the transport map is homogeneous, so it cannot itself learn an input-independent reweighting of frequent tokens). Readout is scored as recall@k over the seven layers; the causal eval patches in a swapped concept's lens vector and asks whether the model's answer becomes that concept, over 67 trials. Crucially, they fit the J- and R-lens themselves for the main model at matched data and budget, and they run ablations removing each of the three changes, plus a leave-one-task-out version of the weight fitting and a single-router variant, to separate a genuine filtering effect from dev-set overfitting.

## What counts as an explanation here
A content readout in vocabulary space: for any activation, the ranked list of tokens the model is most disposed to verbalise, per layer. It is explicitly a *transport-map* account — a single linear approximation to how an intermediate state would be decoded at the final layer — so it predicts an end-of-model distribution rather than describing a circuit, and it is deliberately partial (workspace content only; single tokens only). What makes it more than a description is the second half of their faithfulness definition: the same vectors are used as interventions, so each readout claim comes with a patch that should move the answer in a stated direction. It does predict: patching the "ant" lens vector in place of "spider" should turn 8 into 6, and readout rank at a given layer should fall and rise in a stated order under an early clamp if their suppression hypothesis is right.

## How they judged success
Primary metric: **recall@10** on the five extraction tasks, macro-averaged, with an item counted as a hit if its target intermediate appears in the top 10 readouts at any of the seven layers; a stricter recall@1 is reported in a table. Secondary metric: **intermediate-swap success rate** — the share of trials where the patched-in concept's lens vector becomes the model's top answer word. Baselines are the **Logit Lens**, the **J-lens** and the **R-lens**, all scored with the same readout filtering where the comparison is about Jacobian filtering. Ablations: drop each of the three changes; vary the number of experts (4/8/16); replace learned weighting with k-means routing, random routing, best-expert-per-layer, a single shared router fitted at layer 8, pooled-average-minus-two-worst, and leave-one-task-out weights; token-type source filtering (semantic only, mid-word only, end-of-word only, a three-class token router); fitting-budget and sequence-length sweeps. For the case study, predictions are stated in advance (concept enters the workspace early under both focus and suppress prompts; salience plateaus or declines under suppression; removing it early disrupts later suppression) and the third is tested by a clamp over layers 16–28 with a same-category control concept. They are also careful about the null: they report a section of failed ideas (regression transport maps using future tokens, bias terms and z-scoring, extended unembedding, one Jacobian per token, gradient clipping).

## What they found
Headline: J++ reaches **55.2%** recall@10 on Qwen3.6-27B against **35.7%** (J) and **37.7%** (R), and the median relative uplift over six models is **63.5% over the J-lens** and **42.2% over the R-lens**; the median score across models is **55.0%**. The advantage grows with scale in their sample — **+98%** relative on Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and **+101%** on DeepSeek-V4-Flash, where the absolute score is the highest they report at **61.4%** against **30.5%** for the J-lens. On the strict recall@1 macro, J++ gets **32.4%** against 18.2% (J) and 18.4% (R), a ~78% / ~76% relative improvement. Causal importance is retained rather than traded away: intermediate-swap top-1 success is **59.7%** for J++ versus **49.3%** (J) and **50.7%** (R), and 75.9% at top-5. Task-level detail is the informative part: multihop 67.3% recall@10, multilingual 74.6%, typo 91.7%, association 37.3% (from 10.8% for J), and **poetry 4.9% for everything** — no lens can read rhyme where they look.

Attribution of the gain: Jacobian Filtering is **−11.7 pp** when removed and accounts for **60%** of the 19.5 pp gain over the J-lens; LRP is **−6.6 pp**; readout filtering **−2.9 pp**. More experts help monotonically in their sweep (4 experts −3.7 pp, 16 experts +4.7 pp). Learned weighting beats every cheap alternative: k-means routing −11.1 pp, random routing −7.5 pp, best-expert-per-layer −5.0 pp, pooled-minus-two-worst −8.3 pp; a single layer-8 router shared across layers is as good or slightly better (+0.8 pp), and leave-one-task-out weight fitting costs only 0.8 pp, which is their case against dev-set overfitting.

Why it works, in their account: Expert Jacobians are wildly unequal — only **24 of 52** experts beat the pooled Jacobian when applied globally, and **17 score below 2% per-layer recall@10**; at layers 8 and 16, **10 of 16 experts score below 2%**. The pooled map therefore averages in the bad ones, and "formatting experts" fitted mainly on punctuation are over-represented among the failures, though not exclusively. With LRP, gradient norms grow towards early layers up to **5× slower**, and they read the residual growth as accumulated noise rather than signal. Two structural findings sit in the appendices: the averaged Jacobian is numerically full rank at every layer (at least 5,119 of 5,120 singular values above the fp32 precision floor) but its **entropy effective rank rises from 1,746 at layer 8 to 4,765 at layer 56**, which they offer as one reason early lenses generalise worse; and Expert Jacobians disagree in direction at layer 22 but largely agree by layer 52, which they read as the transport map being more globally linear later — and which they say *increased* their credence in the "surprisingly linear" assumption they set out to break, because the piecewise experiment ended in a linear map.

Cost: no extra inference compute or memory — after mixing, each layer is one linear map of the same shape as the J-lens. Fitting costs ~7% more than a J-lens plus a clustering step: about **9 H200-GPU-hours** and roughly an hour of wall clock for all 63 layers.

Case study (retrieve-then-suppress): with the J++ lens the instructed concept enters the workspace by **layer 24** under focus, suppress and neutral-mention prompts alike, well above a no-instruction baseline; under focus it keeps rising to **26%** hit rate by layer 56, under suppression it plateaus at **7–8%**, and under neutral mention it falls from 14% to 8% between layers 24 and 48. Clamping the concept's lens vector out over layers 16–28 under the suppression prompt makes its later rank *rise* where it otherwise falls. The key discrimination: in a priming task where a sentence implies a concept, early-layer ablation of the implied concept raises the probability the model names it by only a small, non-significant amount, but raises the probability it fails to *avoid* it by **2.8 pp** beyond a same-category control (the J-lens vector gives 0.36 pp, not significant). So the early workspace is implicated in suppression and not in naming — and on their accounting that is exactly why the concept sometimes appears even when the model was told to avoid it. A retrieved-then-suppressed pattern appears far more often with J++ than with earlier lenses across the 440 topic × carrier-sentence items.

## Limits and open problems they name
Single-token readout only: propositions are not readable, and the dependence on the tokeniser is made concrete by an arithmetic task they could not evaluate on Qwen models because numbers are tokenised digit by digit. They do not know the ceiling of the readout score for a given model, because models sometimes shortcut to the final answer without representing the intermediate at all. Poetry fails for every lens and may need a different token or a different eval. k-means clustering is "relatively coarse". They have no account of *which* activations produce noisy gradients — cluster size does not predict expert quality, non-semantic over-representation explains some bad experts but many others are neither large nor punctuation-heavy — and gradient clipping did not help. They also flag in a footnote that it is unclear whether workspace readout performance is yet good enough to be practically useful for monitoring. In the comments, a question about weights overfitting to tasks with specified target tokens is answered with WorkspaceBench numbers (alignment scenario 0.9 vs 0.73; multi-token tasks 7.2 vs 4.5), which are outside the post itself and not inspected here.

## What we take from it
Method, and the single most reusable idea in this folder's recent entries: **filter the estimator, not the data**. Their object is a mean over gradients, and the fix is to notice that averaging kills unbiased noise but accumulates biased noise, then to build a cheap classifier of "which estimates are systematically wrong" — here, a clustering plus learned include/exclude weights — and to price it as a fitting-time cost with zero inference cost. That shape transfers directly to any averaged quantity we compute in our own white-box model (mean-difference directions over contrast pairs, averaged attributions, pooled probes over contexts): report the per-stratum quality of the average's ingredients, and check whether a small learned reweighting over strata recovers most of the gap. The ingredient table (24 of 52 experts better than the pool, 17 below 2%) is the diagnostic we should be publishing.

Measure: the two-sided faithfulness definition is worth importing wholesale — *readout accuracy* and *does the vector act when patched* must be reported together, because a better readout that is no longer causal is not a better lens. Note that J++ improves readout margin without losing patch efficacy; a written-up paper gains here would very plausibly have traded the second for the first.

Framing: the four hypotheses for a failed readout (wrong theory / not linearly represented / absent / bad instrument) is the honest decomposition to put in front of any null of ours, and the practical move — repair the instrument before concluding anything about the model — is exactly the asymmetry our own write-ups should avoid. Also worth carrying: their explicit note that the space is *numerically* full rank while the *effective* rank is far lower in early layers, which is a warning against reading "full rank, therefore nothing lost" from a Jacobian.

Warning: the linearity conclusion is reported as a surprise in the direction of *more* linearity, and the piecewise structure they built ended up collapsed into a single linear map with weights — so "linear representation" here is a statement about a fitted transport map's global approximation quality, not about the model's internals, and the effective-rank gradient across depth is the quantitative statement we should cite instead of "the model is linear".

Shortcut: fitting budget is tiny (~7k positions, 64 sequences, one hour of GPU) and the gains are large, which makes this a cheap thing to try on any model we have access to before building anything more elaborate.
