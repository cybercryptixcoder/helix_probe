## What they wanted, and why

Several groups had recently claimed that replacing the L1 penalty in a sparse autoencoder removes the shrinkage problem, in which the L1 term drags the optimum activation of a live feature below its true value and so costs reconstruction quality at fixed sparsity. The candidates were gated encoders, a hard top-k activation, and post-hoc fine-tuning of a parameter subset. Anthropic's own February note had found that a tanh penalty bought the same trade-off improvement while making features noticeably harder to interpret, so the team's question was narrower than the outside claim: do the L0/MSE gains survive a *blind* check on feature quality, or does every shrinkage fix buy its numbers with legibility? The post also digests three outside papers — an evaluation suite for interpretability methods, a principled critique of SAE metrics, and a challenge to the linear representation hypothesis — which together frame the second question the team cares about: how should a dictionary be judged at all, when the thing we want has no ground truth.

## What they studied

An 18-layer model, residual-stream SAEs at an expansion giving 256k features, compared across four training recipes: a vanilla ReLU SAE with an L1 penalty, a gated SAE, and top-k SAEs at K=20 and K=50. Ties are to the SAE recipes, not to a particular capability, and no model-internals claim is made anywhere in the post — it is a comparison of dictionaries. Specific features are inspected in two places: the highest-density features of a K=20 run, and a K=100 run used as a stress case. The digest part covers GPT-2 and GPT-2-small (the outside papers' models) and Llama 3 8B and Mistral 7B (the multidimensional-feature paper).

## How they did it

They retrained the two shrinkage-free variants on their own pipeline: top-k caps the number of active features at exactly K so L0 is set directly, and the gated variant learns a separate pathway deciding whether a feature is on at all, both with tied encoder/decoder initialisation and — for the gated model — the unconstrained decoder norm from the April recipe, which forced them to stop freezing the decoder during the auxiliary loss because the decoder norm kept collapsing while the magnitude term grew. They then plotted the two objectives against each other across sparsity settings rather than reporting a single point, and separately tabulated how many features sit above a density threshold, since the recipes are known to differ in the shape of the density distribution and not only in its mean. For quality, they ran a blind manual rating: 100 features, origin hidden, showing the top-activating contexts plus the first seven activation buckets only, because the low buckets differ systematically between recipes and would have unmasked the run. Each feature was scored on two five-point rubrics, one for how consistently the firing contexts share a pattern and one for how complex the shared theme is. They additionally ran 200–250 features per run through their automatic interpretation system, which scores a feature by the correlation between its real activations and activations predicted from a generated natural-language explanation. Finally, for the digest, they read and summarised the three outside papers, adding their own caveats where the evaluation design has a known soft spot.

## What counts as an explanation here

As in every monthly, the explanation is a dictionary plus human-legible feature descriptions — not a circuit and not a predictive model of behaviour. The new content is meta: it is an argument about which *measurements* of a dictionary are trustworthy. Two of the three measures used are internal to the SAE (the L0/MSE frontier, the density histogram) and one is a proxy for human legibility (blind rubric scores, machine-scored explanation-activation correlation), and the authors are explicit that none of them checks whether the basis corresponds to how the model actually computes. Their own conclusion is that an SAE should be judged by what it lets you *do* — debug, steer, find circuits, explain a fine-tuning change, improve robustness — and that the field's current evaluations do not reach that.

## How they judged success

Four comparisons: (a) L0 versus MSE across recipes; (b) the count of features above 1e-2 and 1e-3 firing density at matched-ish sparsity; (c) the blind manual rating on the two rubrics, with the footnote that the error bars are probably too tight because activation-consistency scores are heavy-tailed at the low end and a couple of unintelligible features can move the mean a long way; (d) the automatic-interpretation score compared against a stronger vanilla baseline (L1=10) that has worse MSE and lower L0 than the candidates. The digest section adds the outside benchmarks as alternative protocols: best-single-feature linear-probe accuracy on existing classification datasets plus sparsity of the ablation effect on logits; attribute-disentanglement with activation patching; and sufficiency/necessity/sparse-controllability/interpretability on the indirect-object-identification task.

## What they found

Both shrinkage-free recipes land well below the vanilla SAE on the L0/MSE frontier and are close to each other, and the authors note the two new recipes are *less* optimised than their baseline (they had not ported the outside paper's dead-feature remedy), so the gap is a lower bound on the improvement. The density distributions differ sharply and in the direction nobody wanted: at a nominal L0 of 50 top-k has 261 features above 1e-2, at K=20 it has 54, the gated run at L0 37 has 8, and the vanilla run at L0 38 has 13 — and above 1e-3 the ordering is the same with a much larger spread, so the recipes that win on the frontier concentrate firing into few features. Blind manual scoring found no discernible quality difference at this scale of analysis. On the density worry they looked at the tail directly: in the K=20 run the densest feature fired about 9% of the time, seven features fired more than once in forty tokens, and the dense features were mostly end-of-turn related (firing on the control token or the text just after it), with the 1–2% band containing common words, Italian, Russian and pre-20th-century English, plus a few opaque code features the authors are careful not to call failures; a K=100 run instead produced dozens of features firing on more than a tenth of tokens that were largely unintelligible, which they read as "pathological only at high L0". Machine scoring agreed with the human result: the gated and K=20 runs matched a vanilla run with much worse MSE and L0, with the caveat that removing the very low activations may simply make the explanation task easier. Together this partially rehabilitates the February tanh result: high-density features accompanying a shrinkage fix look less like a pathology than the team had assumed, at least in the many-features/high-sparsity regime. From the digest: top-k removes the L1 pressure so L0 becomes a direct and interpretable knob; the best-single-feature probe accuracy bottoms out around L0 64–128 and improves with dictionary size, ablation sparsity around L0 128–256, and probe quality moves only modestly over training; the disentanglement benchmark shows plain dictionaries falling well short of supervised ground-truth dictionaries on interchange interventions, and a learned linear-subspace method winning; and the IOI critique finds unsupervised dictionaries sufficient-but-failing on necessity and controllability, with two named recurring artefacts — *feature occlusion*, where the indirect-object names get features but the subject names mostly do not because the former have larger magnitude, and *feature over-splitting*, where a binary relative-position attribute comes apart into several opaque features that are nonetheless consistent across seeds, which the authors of that paper read as possibly not a failure at all since the binary split was a human's choice. The multidimensional-feature paper reports irreducible two-dimensional ring representations of weekdays and months in two open 7–8B models, located by unsupervised clustering, causally confirmed by patching, and driven by *angle*: patched points anywhere in the plane produce the same answer as long as the angle matches, including points outside the unit circle; MLP layers carry the computation while attention heads mostly copy it forward; and no other comparably interpretable multidimensional feature was found.

## Limits and open problems they name

No ground-truth objective for a dictionary, so every metric is a proxy and a shrinkage fix could improve MSE while leaving the underlying basis unchanged; it is unclear whether sparse single-feature probes are a thing one should expect features to support at all; the blind rubric is 100 features rated by the authors of the methods being compared, the low-activation buckets had to be hidden to preserve blinding, and the error bars understate the tail; high-density features may be pathological at high L0 and the boundary is not established. In the digest they flag that some concepts may not be independently manipulable in a model at all (a language's geography being entangled with its identity), which would cap a disentanglement score's absolute value though not its use for comparisons, and that the multidimensional result may be an artefact of the clustering method or evidence that such features are simply rare.

## What we take from it

A *measurement* recipe and a *warning*. The measurement: if we build anything that claims to read a basis out of a network, report the trade-off curve rather than one operating point, report the density histogram alongside it, and blind the human rating while matching the rating task across methods — hiding exactly the buckets that would give the method away. The machine-scored explanation-activation correlation is cheap and, here, agreed with the humans, which makes it usable as a pre-screen before any expensive rating. The warning is sharper and applies directly to our goal: this post is the field admitting it cannot tell whether a dictionary is real, only whether it is convenient, and the one proposed escape is behaviour — can the proposed structure predict what the model does under an intervention. That is the slot our predictive white-box model is meant to fill, and the IOI result gives the checklist we would have to pass: sufficiency (reconstruction preserves the behaviour), necessity (the residual error cannot do the job), sparse controllability (a small set of edits produces a predicted output change), and interpretability against named ground-truth attributes. Two further carry-overs: *feature occlusion* is a failure mode where the higher-magnitude member of an attribute pair takes the feature and the lower-magnitude one is invisible, so any read-out that infers "concept absent" from "no feature fires" inherits an asymmetry that tracks salience rather than presence; and the angle-invariance result — answer determined by angle, not radius — is a concrete example of a probe-style read-out that generalises off-distribution because it reads a coordinate rather than a magnitude. Finally, a note for the January 2024 joint-superposition counterexample: an irreducible ring in two dimensions is exactly what a single linear dictionary should refuse to represent, so we should expect our own read-outs to split it and should not count that split as redundancy.
