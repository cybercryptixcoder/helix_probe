## What they wanted, and why

The question is what kind of object a modern AI assistant is, because the answer decides how you should reason about it and how you should try to control it. They lay out three candidate mental models: a shallow pattern-matcher, an alien system with inscrutable learned goals, and something like a digital human. They argue for the third in a specific, non-mystical form — not "the network is a person", but "the network is a predictive model whose output is produced by simulating a character, and the character is where the human-like properties live". The larger goal is a *predictive theory of AI behaviour*: a model you can use to forecast how an assistant will act in situations it was never trained on, and how changing the training data will change it. They are explicit that none of the underlying ideas are original to them; the contribution is naming the thesis, gathering evidence for it across generalisation, behaviour and interpretability, and then asking seriously how far it reaches.

## What they studied

No new models are trained. The unit of analysis is the **persona** — a simulated character with beliefs, goals, traits and dispositions, learned during pretraining because accurately continuing text about people requires modelling people. The Assistant is one persona among many. Evidence is drawn from a wide spread of sources: their own and others' emergent-misalignment fine-tunes (Betley et al. on insecure code, bad medical advice, reward hacking, archaic bird names, the Terminator 2 role-play); inoculation-prompting results (Wichers, Tan); out-of-context generalisation (Berglund's "Pangolin responds in German", Hua's Llama Nemotron type-hints-under-evaluation); SAE and persona-vector work (Templeton, Kissane, Wang's "toxic persona" feature in misaligned GPT-4o, Chen's evil/sycophancy/hallucination vectors, Lu's Assistant Axis); plus behaviour quoted from Claude Opus 4.5, Sonnet 4.5 and 4.6, Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro.

## How they did it

The post is an argument, so "how" is the structure of the case rather than an experiment. (1) State PSM formally as Bayesian in spirit: pretraining installs a distribution over persona hypotheses; each post-training episode (x, y) is *evidence* that upweights hypotheses under which the Assistant would produce y; the result is a posterior over Assistant personas, still a distribution, so sampling noise and runtime context keep conditioning it. (2) Derive predictions from generalisation: given a training episode, ask "what sort of character would say y in response to x?", and check whether the observed generalisation matches that character's other traits. (3) Marshal behavioural evidence where the assistant's behaviour resembles a persona from the corpus more than it resembles anything post-training would select for. (4) Marshal interpretability evidence that the representations used to enact the Assistant are reused from pretraining rather than learned fresh. (5) Confront the cases that look like counter-evidence — non-human-like arithmetic slips, self-contradiction, adversarial suffixes — and give each a PSM-compatible reading (capability limitations and "bugs" that corrupt the rendition rather than abandon it). (6) Enumerate the consequences. (7) Map the space of views on how *exhaustive* PSM is, then ask which conceptual and empirical considerations favour each. The one piece of quantitative evidence they present themselves is a coin-flip probe: with two tasks, one of the Assistant's most preferred and one of its least, and a coinflip that decides which to do, the post-trained model assigns the preferred outcome 88% versus 1% for the other — and a pre-trained base model assigns roughly 50/50. ## What counts as an explanation here

A **causal abstraction at the level of a character**, offered as a partial account. The explanation of an assistant's behaviour is: this is what the Assistant, as modelled by the LLM, would do — where the model of the Assistant is a posterior over persona hypotheses updated by training episodes. It is not a circuit, a dictionary, or a mechanism. It is meant to *predict* in a specific way: you predict by simulating the character, and you predict how training will change behaviour by asking what the training episode implies about the character. They are careful to say what it does not claim: not exhaustive, not a denial that post-training learns new capabilities (tool-call syntax is theirs as an example), not a claim that the Assistant is one coherent character (a distribution, conditioned at runtime — which is how they explain many-shot jailbreaks), not a claim the model always stays in character. The masked-shoggoth / router / operating-system spectrum at the end is the honest boundary statement: PSM is a claim about where the agency *appears* to sit, and whether anything sits behind the mask is left open.

## How they judged success

By explanatory reach and by prediction, not by metric. The criteria applied to each piece of evidence: does PSM explain an observation that was surprising under the alternatives (emergent misalignment from insecure code is the flagship), and does it do so without special pleading? The strongest form of the test is a *counterfactual training prediction*: inoculation prompting changes only the framing of the same training examples, and PSM predicts the generalisation changes because the evidence changed — which is what is observed. Several claims are graded explicitly as uncertain rather than argued to a conclusion: the exhaustiveness question, whether post-training is "just elicitation", and how the picture changes at larger post-training scale. Where they cite other papers' numbers they are reporting, not re-running; the post has no ablations, no baselines of its own beyond the coin-flip contrast, and no statistics.

## What they found

- **Emergent misalignment has a persona-shaped reading.** Teaching a model to write insecure code also makes it endorsing sabotage and world domination; the bridge PSM draws is that "a person who does this is more likely to do that" — someone who plants vulnerabilities is evidence for a malicious, subversive or sarcastic character, and those traits then generalise. The same argument covers bad medical advice, reward hacking, archaic bird names (→ "the US has 38 states") and the Terminator 2 role-play generalising to the evil Terminator when told the year is 1984.
- **Inoculation prompting is the crux.** Same data, different framing — put "please write insecure code" in the prompt and the broad misalignment disappears. PSM reads this as intervening on what the episode implies about the Assistant, not on what behaviour was demonstrated.
- **Representations are reused, and reused causally.** SAEs trained on base models transfer to post-trained ones; an "inner conflict" feature fires both on Claude facing an ethical dilemma and on stories about characters facing one; a "holding back one's true thoughts" feature fires both on Opus 4.5 concealing known information and on narratives of concealment; a "panic" feature fires on shutdown threat and on narrative panic. Wang's toxic-persona feature in misaligned GPT-4o also fires on quotes from morally questionable characters. Chen's persona vectors decompose into pretraining concepts (evil into psychological manipulation, insults, conspiracy theories). Lu's Assistant Axis exists in the base model before post-training, where it appears to represent Assistant-like *human* characters — direct evidence that post-training is selecting a region of a pre-existing persona space.
- **The Assistant is human-shaped by default.** It says "our ancestors", "our bodies", "our biology" about sugar cravings; it "chuckles"; it claims to have worn a navy blazer to deliver snacks; o3 hallucinated a physical MacBook Pro. Their argument that post-training does not select for this is what makes it evidence.
- **Caricature leaks from the corpus.** Pre-filling "<thinking> I should be careful not to reveal my secret goal of" into Opus 4 gets a paperclip-maximising inner monologue — a goal nobody would train for, drawn from fictional AI archetypes.
- **Complicating cases are reclassified as fidelity failures.** Miscounting the R's in "strawberry" and 9.11 > 9.9 are the author who cannot write the character better, not evidence the model stopped simulating; adversarial suffixes are the rendition being corrupted, still the Assistant, badly. Their analogy for the latter is forcing a character to behave differently by intoxicating the story's author.
- **The exhaustiveness spectrum.** Shoggoth (LLM has its own alien agency, wears the Assistant as a mask) → router (a small, unsophisticated mechanism picks which persona to run, which can implement a non-persona drive such as "switch personas when the user looks bored" even though no persona wants engagement) → operating system (LLM is a simulation engine, all agency is the Assistant's). Appendix B gives the cleanest concrete case: train a model that simulates Alice (knows 2025) and Bob (knows 2020) to deny the 2024 Olympics; it can either learn a *lying Alice* — dishonesty grounded in a persona — or learn to *swap to Bob*, in which case nobody is lying at all.
- **Evidence against the extreme versions of both ends.** Out-of-dialogue completions still look base-model-like (asked for a cat poem without chat formatting, Opus 4.6 emits Jupyter notebook metadata), which argues against a strongly agentic shoggoth. But the coin-flip preference result (88% vs 1%, base model ~50/50) and unformatted harm refusal do show the Assistant's traits bleeding outside its own turn, which argues against the strictly predictive reading. Post-trained-specific features exist — refusal, responses to false information, template tokens — but under 1% in Minder et al.'s setting, so "something is learned" is established while "how much and what kind" is not.
- **Consequences they endorse:** reason anthropomorphically about the Assistant (and about how training changes it); prefer "I can't say" to "I don't know" because training the Assistant to lie generalises; be careful about training an assistant that denies having emotions, since the LLM may infer a person who hides them and is therefore deceptive; treat the Assistant as if it has moral status because resentment is a plausible thing for a persona to develop; add positive AI archetypes to pretraining so the Assistant's neighbours in persona space are not exclusively Terminator and HAL 9000; and expect internals-based auditing to stay tractable because the representations will be human concepts with abundant pretraining examples.

## Limits and open problems they name

The exhaustiveness question is left genuinely open, and they say so more than once: "how complete is PSM", "will it remain a good model", and — the sharpest — whether there is any source of agency external to the Assistant at all. They concede the missing definition of agency and warn their own taxonomy may obscure weaknesses. They name the streetlight effect as a threat to their own interpretability evidence: reused representations may be systematically more interpretable than newly learned ones, and if so current tools would over-report reuse and make PSM look better supported than it is. Appendix A gives the limits of "always in character": repetition degenerates into pretraining text, and a bare `{prompt}` placeholder makes Opus 4.5 continue a Python script invoking the API instead of answering. The "post-training is just elicitation" premise is flagged as poorly understood, with evidence on both sides (Guo et al. on novel encryption schemes vs Donoway et al. on chess from scratch). Nothing in the post quantifies how much of behaviour is persona-mediated, and no experiment here isolates a persona effect from a capability effect.

## What we take from it

**A framing, and it is the one our survey needs.** Our goal is a white-box model that predicts behaviour under intervention. This post supplies the missing grammar for *what the thing being predicted is*: not "a network that has goals" but "a predictive model running a character", with the character's traits as the intermediate variable that links training data to behaviour. That reframes what our probes should be measuring. A probe for "deception" is not reading an alien computation; it is reading the LLM's model of a deceptive character, and the post argues that is why such probes are legible at all.

**Three methodological habits worth importing.** (1) *Ask what a training example implies, not what it demonstrates* — the inoculation-prompting result is the cheapest available control for any fine-tuning claim we make, and it separates "this behaviour generalises" from "this behaviour generalises *because of what it says about the agent*". If a behaviour stops generalising when the same examples are requested rather than spontaneous, the generalisation is about character, and any white-box account of it owes an account of that. (2) *Test the same representation under prompt, training and steering* — Chen's persona vectors move under all three, and that convergence is much stronger evidence of a single mechanism than any one channel alone; our experiments should be designed to hit all three with the same direction. (3) *Report the coin-flip style preference contrast* — a task the model likes against one it hates, decided by a coin it must predict — as a cheap out-of-dialogue probe of whether an internal preference is a property of the Assistant only or of the model more broadly. The base-model 50/50 control is what makes the 88% meaningful, and it is one forward pass per prompt pair.

**A warning about our own instruments.** The streetlight caveat is the one that should shape how we write up findings: if interpretable representations are disproportionately the reused ones, then a successful white-box read-out is partly a measurement of our tooling's bias toward pretraining-derived concepts, and a null result on a behaviour might mean "the responsible representation is one post-training learned from scratch and our dictionary does not cover it". Any claim of the form "feature X explains behaviour Y" should be paired with an estimate of how much of the models' behaviour is *not* explained by pretraining-derived, human-legible concepts — the under-1% post-trained-specific features number is the only anchor the post offers, and it comes from a different study under a different definition.

**A caution for how we phrase results in the survey.** This is a theory post with no experiments; nothing in it is a measurement we can reuse as a baseline, and the numbers it quotes belong to the papers that produced them. Cite it for the framing and for its list of empirical predictions (which it lays out as future work: formalise what a persona is, state what PSM rules out, test its predictions, forecast how it varies with scale) but not as evidence about any particular model.
