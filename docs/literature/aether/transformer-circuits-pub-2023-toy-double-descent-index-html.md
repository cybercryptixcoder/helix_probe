## What they wanted, and why
Their earlier superposition work studied the infinite-data, underfitting regime, where a narrow bottleneck learns to represent more features than it has dimensions by packing them into regular polytopes. Here they ask whether the same toy setup, trained on *finite* datasets, can say something mechanistic about overfitting and memorisation — because if memorisation has a representational form, then a model that overfits is doing something a white-box account should be able to name rather than merely flag by a train/test gap. The larger goal is to give mechanistic interpretability a story about the regime that most real training actually lives near, rather than only the clean infinite-data limit. They also want to test whether double descent — more data making test loss *worse* before it gets better — falls out of that story.

## What they studied
A ReLU-output toy model: hidden vector h = Wx, reconstruction x′ = ReLU(Wᵀx + b), i.e. a tied-weight linear down-projection into m dimensions followed by a nonlinear up-projection back to the input space. Inputs are n = 10,000-dimensional sparse non-negative vectors (each coordinate zero with probability S = 0.999), rescaled to unit L2 norm so training examples are comparable in magnitude; training sets have finite size T. Hidden width is deliberately tiny — m = 2 for the figures that need explicit visualisation, m = 4 where optimisation failures in 2-D were confounding results. The unit of analysis is therefore *weights and the geometry they induce*: the columns of W (feature directions) versus the hidden vectors W·X_i of the training examples. Later, one non-toy extension looks at a single-hidden-layer 512-unit MNIST classifier.

## How they did it
Models are trained by full-batch AdamW for 50,000 updates on mean-squared reconstruction error with uniform per-coordinate importance, a 2,500-step warmup to LR 1e-3 then cosine decay, weight decay 1e-2 by default; the authors state that full-batch training, the step count and the annealed LR are all necessary to get these results, and that extremely sparse features make gradients noisy. For each dataset size T they retrain with four different random dataset draws (and, where noted, several initialisation seeds, keeping the lowest-training-loss run) and average the test loss. They then re-plot the same models from two viewpoints: the feature view (columns of W, or the rows/atoms the model uses) and the *data-point* view (the hidden vectors of the training examples). Both are scored with a dimensionality measure D = ‖h_i‖² / Σ_j (ĥ_i·h_j)², the effective number of directions a vector participates in, giving D_{f_i} for features and an analogous D_{X_i} for examples; a T-gon embedding of T examples in m = 2 predicts D_{X_i} = m/T, so the measure is calibrated against a known construction. The main sweep varies T and m and maps test loss over the (T, m) plane, with appendix sweeps over weight decay and number of epochs. Finally they re-derive the memorisation-vs-generalisation threshold with a loss-benefit argument and test it against runs with deliberately repeated data points.

## What counts as an explanation here
A mechanistic/geometric account, not a replacement model: the claim is that "overfitting" is a *strategy the network adopts* — storing individual training examples as if they were features, in superposition, rather than storing generalising features in superposition. The explanation is stated as a two-regime picture (data-point features vs generalising features) with a transition, and it is explicitly partial: the authors say up front that the naive theory is probably too simple, and they leave the messy middle regime unexplained. Predictive content is limited but real: the geometry of a memorising model in the very-small-data regime is analytically constrained (T-gons for orthogonal examples), and the onset of generalisation is predicted by how often each feature recurs.

## How they judged success
Success is judged primarily by *visual and geometric diagnostics on the fitted weights* rather than by a benchmark: whether the data-point hidden vectors form a clean polytope while the feature view looks like an unstructured scatter (and the reverse in the large-data regime), and whether D_{X_i} tracks the predicted m/T. Double descent is established by averaging test loss over four dataset seeds per T and finding a bump at the regime boundary, with the (T, m) sweep showing regions where more data or more width hurts; consistency with prior double-descent literature is checked by the expected sensitivity to weight decay (going from 1e-2 to 1.0 removes the bump) and to epochs (more epochs, bigger bump). External validation is unusual and worth noting: the thread ships with invited replications from outside researchers (Jermyn, Hobbhahn, and others), and the original authors respond in print to quantitative mismatches — Jermyn reproduces the three regimes qualitatively but sees the transition at T≈20,000 instead of ~5,000 and middle-regime test losses up to ~5,000 versus ~1.01, and the authors reply that they care about the existence of the transition, not its exact T. There is no held-out behavioural benchmark, no intervention, and no direct measurement of memorisation rate against ground-truth training-set membership.

## What they found
In the small-data regime the feature view is uninterpretable but the *example* view is clean: T examples in two hidden dimensions arrange themselves as vertices of a regular T-gon, so the model has effectively decorated each training point with a direction of its own. In the large-data regime five features claim high dimensionality in a pentagon while the remaining 9,995 are largely ignored, and most examples activate at most one of those five, which reproduces a pentagon-shaped example scatter for a different reason. Between the two, everything is messy, and parameter norms (‖W‖_F, ‖b‖) peak around T ≈ 30 and then collapse through the middle regime. Averaged over seeds, test loss shows a clear bump at the boundary — double descent *without label noise*, since inputs and targets are identical and the "noise" comes from the unavoidable lossy compression of 10,000 features into 2 dimensions. An invited extension by Jermyn argues the generalisation onset is set not by weight decay but by recurrence: models learn generalising features once each feature appears roughly ten times (T(1−S) ≈ 10), with the caveat that the curves align less well at larger m. A second extension adds repeated data points: at 2–3 repeats nothing changes, at 5+ repeats the model preferentially memorises the repeated examples and each one displaces a generalising feature it would otherwise have learned, with a rough loss-benefit balance giving R ≳ T/N repeats as the threshold (observed data consistent with R ∝ T/N at larger T/N, noisy below). They also report "near misses" — models that memorise a point and then decide against it, mirrored by models in the intermediate regime that briefly learn features and forget them — and an optimisation failure mode at m = 2 where examples settle on two circles of different radii instead of one. Olah's MNIST extension introduces *maximal* data dimensionality (supremum over probe vectors v of (v·h_i)² / Σ_j(v·h_j)², a Gaussian-log-likelihood-flavoured measure) and finds a handful of training examples with close to 100× the typical value, which he tentatively reads as memorised, special-cased examples — framed as a possible mechanistic anomaly detector, with the caveat that superposition could hide such anomalies.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is a toy: the obvious open question is whether the "data-point features" picture generalises to real models at all. They explicitly ask what happens when a model learns a *mixture* of features and single-data-point features (suggesting partially repeated data as the probe), what is actually happening mechanistically in the middle regime where losses spike, and whether any notion of "feature" can unify generalising features and single examples. Hyperparameters matter more than one would like — the phenomenon needs full-batch training, tuned LR schedules, low weight decay and careful hyperparameter search at m = 2 — and the shipped replications disagree quantitatively on where the transition sits, which the authors accept as unresolved. The 2-D circle-merging failure and the near-miss cases are reported as curiosities without explanation.

## What we take from it
A *measure* and a *warning*. The measure: effective dimensionality computed over a set of activations, D_i = ‖h_i‖² / Σ_j(ĉ·h_j)², plus its maximal variant D*_i, is a cheap, label-free way to ask "is this representation organised around feature directions or around particular examples?" — and it can be computed on a probe's reference set without knowing what the features are, which is exactly the position we are in with a black-box network. Computing it per input over a corpus of activations and looking for the heavy tail is a plausible memorisation/special-casing detector to run alongside our behavioural probes, and the D* story links it to a Gaussian model of activations, which is the same assumption a linear probe already makes. The warning: the same weights can support two completely different computational strategies, and *one of them is invisible in the feature basis* — a scatter of feature vectors that looks like noise may be a perfectly clean example-polytope. So any reading of weights that only looks for feature directions can conclude "no structure" when the model is in fact executing a crisp, describable program. Conversely, a small test-loss bump crossed with more data is not evidence of randomness; here it is the signature of a strategy switch, which is what we want a predictive model to anticipate rather than merely record. Also useful: they treat the regime boundary as a *function of feature recurrence*, and derive the memorisation threshold from a loss-benefit comparison — a template for predicting, from data statistics alone, which internal strategy a network will pick.
