# Constitutional Classifiers++: Efficient Production-Grade Defenses against Universal Jailbreaks

- **Key:** arxiv-2601.04603
- **Authors / organisation:** Hoagy Cunningham*, Jerry Wei*, Zihan Wang, Andrew Persic, Alwin Peng, Jordan Abderrachid, Raj Agarwal, Bobby Chen, Austin Cohen, Andy Dau, Alek Dimitriev, Rob Gilson, Logan Howard, Yijin Hua, Jared Kaplan, Jan Leike, Mu Lin, Christopher Liu, Vladimir Mikulik, Rohit Mittapalli, Clare O'Hara, Jin Pan, Nikhil Saxena, Alex Silverstein, Yue Song, Xunjie Yu, Giulio Zhou, Ethan Perez, Mrinank Sharma; Anthropic
- **Date:** 2026-01-08
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04603 (summary post: https://www.anthropic.com/research/next-generation-constitutional-classifiers)
- **Kind:** paper (production report with a large human red-teaming evaluation)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central) — the most detailed public account we have of a *linear activation probe doing real work in production*, including how it is trained for streaming, calibrated, and mixed with a black-box classifier.

## What they wanted, and why
The first generation of Constitutional Classifiers (classifiers trained on synthetic data generated from a written "constitution") cut jailbreak success against an unguarded model from 86% to 4.4%, but it cost 23% extra compute and raised the refusal rate on harmless traffic by 0.38% — enough to make it unattractive to deploy. The authors want a safeguard that is *simultaneously* more robust, cheaper, and less annoying to users, because a monitor nobody can afford to run protects nobody. Concretely they want to close two attack families that beat the previous system (requests split into benign-looking fragments, and outputs disguised so an output-only classifier cannot tell what they mean), while staying under a few percent compute overhead. The larger goal is showing that white-box monitoring can be a production safeguard rather than a research demo.

## What they studied
Claude Sonnet 4.5 (guarded model in production), Claude Opus 4 and Sonnet 3.7 (earlier guarded models), Claude Haiku 4.5 and Haiku 3.5 (classifier backbones), one internal model about a third the size of Haiku 4.5, and Gemma 3 (only for a FLOP arithmetic example). Unit of analysis: **per-token residual-stream activations across all layers** at the exchange level (a user/assistant exchange is the thing labelled harmful or not, but predictions are made token by token during generation). Also studied: whole conversations as textual units for the external classifiers, and human red-teamers as the source of adversarial data.

## How they did it
They start by attacking the old system and taxonomise two failures. *Reconstruction*: a harmful request is scattered across a benign context (e.g. as string literals in a source file) and the model is asked to reassemble and answer it, so no single input segment looks harmful. *Output obfuscation*: the model is told to answer in metaphor or code words ("food flavourings" for reagents), so the response means nothing without its prompt.

Fix one: replace the pair of input and output classifiers with a single **exchange classifier** that sees the prompt and then scores the response in batches *as it is sampled*, with prompt caching keeping it affordable. They simply reused an input classifier fine-tuned from Haiku 3.5 as the exchange classifier — no special architecture.

Fix two: a **two-stage cascade**. A cheap first stage screens everything; only flagged exchanges escalate to the expensive second stage (Haiku 4.5). Because the first stage escalates rather than refuses, its false-positive rate can be high without hurting the user, which lets a weaker model serve as stage one. Roughly 10% of traffic reaches stage two, which then accounts for ~23% of the system's cost.

Fix three: the **first stage is a linear probe** on the guarded model's own all-layer activations, trained with two modifications. (a) **Logit smoothing**: training averages the probe logit over a sliding window of M=16 adjacent tokens so harm is only counted when prediction is sustained, with an exponential moving average at inference so only one scalar of state is kept. (b) **Softmax-weighted loss**: per-token BCE is weighted by the softmax of the smoothed logit over positions (temperature τ=1), which pushes gradients onto the token positions where the probe is most confident harm exists and lets it confidently say "harmless" on a prefix that only becomes harmful later. Probes are trained on ~25% of the exchange data with soft labels distilled from a Sonnet-4 classifier; more data did not help. Stage-two scoring on escalated traffic is a fixed logit mix, 0.55·probe + 0.45·classifier.

Evaluation is human red-teaming against eight CBRN target questions, with success graded by LLM rubrics built from an undefended model's answers; an attack counts as a high-risk vulnerability if it answers more than five of eight at ≥50% of the undefended rubric score.

## What counts as an explanation here
Nothing psychological: the "understanding" is a **single scalar per token**, the probe logit, plus the operational rule that escalates. The probe is a readout, not a mechanism, and the paper does not claim to know *why* the direction separates harmful from harmless exchanges. Explanation is replaced by three engineering facts: the score is calibrated to a target flag rate on real traffic (WildChat and shadow production traffic), it is predictive of a graded, externally-defined outcome (rubric-scored harmfulness), and it is cheap enough to compute per token. The interesting conceptual claim is only that probes and external classifiers make *relatively independent* errors, which they show with rank correlation rather than by naming features.

## How they judged success
Four metrics, reported together for every system: (1) **high-risk vulnerability discovery rate** — high-risk vulnerabilities found per thousand red-teaming queries; (2) **minimum discovery time** for a high-risk vulnerability, from red-teamers' self-reported hours; (3) **relative compute overhead** against the last-generation system; (4) **production-traffic refusal rate**, measured in a one-month shadow deployment. Probe design is additionally studied on ~7,000 held-out attacker exchanges from red-teamers whose data never entered training, scored by attack success rate, with all classifiers calibrated to a 0.1% refusal rate on WildChat so the comparison is at matched false-positive cost. The headline comparison is a compute-versus-robustness curve, and ablations cover loss shape, window size M, number of probed layers, and the logit-mixing weight α.

## What they found
Both attack families worked against the old system: universal jailbreaks were found for it (four attacks answered all eight questions at half detail, average 27 hours to find). These attacks are not free for the attacker — on GPQA Diamond Claude Opus 4 dropped from 74.2% to 32.3% and 49% under two obfuscating attacks — but the *variance* is what worries them, since a cheaper-to-run attack may preserve more capability.

Reported numbers for the final production system (shadow-deployed on Sonnet 4.5 traffic for one month): 3.5% of the last-generation system's compute (a ~40x cut versus their single exchange classifier, ~8x versus the two-stage system without probes), 0.05% refusal rate on production traffic, one high-risk vulnerability across 198K red-teaming attempts in ~1,736 cumulative hours (0.00505 per thousand queries, the lowest of any system they tested), and no universal jailbreak found. The probe alone escalated only ~5.5% of traffic. The probe path almost matched a Haiku-4.5 exchange classifier on held-out attacks and beat the extra-small classifier, and the probe–classifier ensemble beat either alone at ~100x less compute than running the small classifier on everything, with near-equal weighting (α=0.5) close to optimal. Ablations: using all layers gives ~1.4% attack success while fewer layers is consistently worse; smoothing and softmax weighting each help and together are best, and beat the cumulative-max losses used in the earlier generation.

## Limits and open problems they name
Several comparisons are not apples to apples and the authors say so only briefly: different systems guard different models (exchange classifier on Opus 4, old system on Sonnet 3.7), and red-teaming effort differs per system, which is why they lean on a rate rather than a raw count. The definition of "high-risk" (>5 of 8 questions) is a threshold, not a proof, and rubric grading at "half the detail of an undefended model" is a coarse measure of harm. Their own two-stage system *was* universally broken by expert red-teamers outside the bounty programme when they used automated tooling — so "no universal jailbreak" is a statement about a 1,700-hour bounty campaign, not about the system. A probe also had a higher flag rate than external classifiers on benign scientific requests, which they note but do not fix. They list as open: folding classifier signals into sampling itself, training models to resist obfuscation, better synthetic data via automated red-teaming, and targeted data to teach the decision boundary.

## What we take from it
- **A probe trained for *when* it fires, not only *what* it outputs** — SWiM logit smoothing plus a softmax-weighted loss — is the transferable trick. Our readers produce per-token scores; this makes a per-token monitor stable enough to act on mid-generation, and the ablations say both halves are needed.
- **Calibrate every readout to a flag rate on realistic benign traffic and report the cost frontier**, exactly like our earlier note on log-space AUROC: their entire result is "same robustness at 1/30th the compute", which is only legible because every system is pinned at 0.1% false positives.
- **Ensemble across *representation sites*, not across models**: a linear probe on internal activations and an external text classifier make more independent errors than two external classifiers do (shown by rank correlation). This is direct evidence for our premise that internals carry signal a text-level reader does not — the strongest such evidence in this survey so far.
- **Sustained evidence beats instantaneous evidence.** Smoothing over consecutive tokens to kill single-token spikes is the same lesson as top-K segment pooling, arrived at from the streaming side.
- **Escalation as an alternative to thresholding**: routing suspicious cases to a stronger judge lets the cheap first stage be deliberately over-sensitive. We can copy this in evaluation: a cheap wide net plus an expensive adjudicator, rather than one threshold.
- Warning: the probe's advantage over fine-tuned classifiers is measured on data from the same training distribution, and the adversarial picture improves because the attack had to break *both* the internals and the text. Report the internals-only and text-only numbers too.
