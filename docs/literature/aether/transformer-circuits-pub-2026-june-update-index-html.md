# Turn-Averaged SAEs for Feature Discovery and Long-Context Attribution

- **Authors / organisation:** Kevin Der (Anthropic Fellows Program), Harish Kamath, Ben Thompson (Anthropic); monthly index edited by Nick Turner

- **Link:** https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/june-update/index.html (paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28548)
- **Kind:** paper (released behind a short monthly index post)
- **Read from:** full text — main body plus Appendices A–I. No figures inspected (Figures 1–7 are read only through the prose that reports their values), so no curve shape is quoted here.
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Per-token SAEs produce a number of active features that grows linearly with sequence length, which makes the standard downstream analyses — inspecting a transcript, building an attribution graph, monitoring a monitoring target — unwieldy exactly where they matter: long multi-turn conversations. The authors also make a second, sharper complaint that is more interesting than the scaling one: because a per-token SAE reconstructs each position independently, nothing in its objective forces high-level properties (topic, style, persona, safety-relevant traits) to separate from word-level and syntactic detail. Properties that are genuinely spread across many tokens and that an auditor cares about most (adversarial-prompt detection, distress, evaluation awareness) are therefore the ones a per-token dictionary is least likely to surface as a single legible feature. The fix they propose is a one-line change to the input, not the architecture: train on the mean residual-stream state over a span, so the unit of analysis becomes the turn.

## What they studied

Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct (d_model = 3584), SAMPLES taken from LMSYS-Chat-1M. Papers/units: the residual stream itself, at a single layer for the discovery work (layer 19, ≈70% depth) and at four layers for the attribution work (6, 11, 16, 21 — roughly 20/40/60/80% depth). Unit of analysis is moved deliberately from the token to the span: one Human or one Assistant turn, and in the long-context experiment one whole document. Feature dictionaries are BatchTopK with width 32,768 and k = 128 throughout, so the comparison across architectures is at a matched feature budget.

## How they did it

Step 1, the core trick: for a span of tokens T, compute the arithmetic mean of the residual stream over T and train an ordinary SAE to reconstruct that mean with a standard squared-error loss. Because a mean of vectors has the same shape as a vector, no code or architecture change is needed. They frame this as a low-pass filter over the token axis: properties reflected across many positions survive averaging, while token-specific high-frequency detail and noise is attenuated — which is the mechanism they offer for why the resulting features land at the level of topic and behaviour.

Step 2, two hybrid architectures, because an averaged SAE cannot reconstruct per-token detail at all. A decoupled model trains two independent SAEs — one on the turn mean, one on the residual (x_t − mean) — which they reject on two grounds: ablating a turn-level feature shifts the per-token input uniformly and the residual SAE is invariant to that shift, so the two feature sets are causally disconnected and attribution is impossible; and the k budget must be split, costing reconstruction. A nested model keeps one shared encoder/decoder and adds a Matryoshka-style inner loss: features [0:h] must alone reconstruct the turn mean, while the full set reconstructs each token. They sweep the inner-loss weight α ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0} and use 1.0; h = 16,384 gives the two classes equal capacity.

Step 3, qualitative comparison: encode held-out turns through each architecture, aggregate per-token features by summing across the turn (they note max-pooling was visibly worse by inspection), get an LLM-generated description for each, and compare which aspects of the turn each set picks out. Examples were hand-picked from 316,000 held-out assistant turns (53.2M tokens) for illustrative contrast.

Step 4, quantitative comparison along two axes they treat as genuinely different questions. *Discrimination*: given one configuration's feature descriptions and 10 candidate texts (the true turn plus 9 random distractors), can a judge pick the right one? *Coverage*: given a 24-field structured summary of the turn (content, form, voice, function, meta — schema and an example in Appendix D), which of two equal-length feature lists describes it more completely, and how do all five rank together? Five configurations are compared (turn-averaged, per-token sum, per-token max, nested sum, nested max) over 2,000 holdout turns; the coverage axis additionally gets a judge-free check by embedding both summary fields and feature descriptions with Qwen3-Embedding-8B and taking cosine similarity at k ∈ {1,2,3,5}.

Step 5, attribution. They reuse the circuit-tracer framework at the four layers and redefine a node as "a feature active on a particular turn" rather than at a particular token, with the edge weight α_A,t · (d̂_A · g_t) averaged over the source turn (and the target gradient averaged over the target turn), so edge magnitudes are explicitly normalised to be length-agnostic. Two case studies and then three validation measures: a synthetic 12-paragraph system prompt in which each paragraph carries one instruction and 8 queries each target a paragraph, scored by how much the targeted paragraph's attribution share rises over a neutral-query baseline; a hand-built subgraph for a 14-turn conversation that degrades from refusal into repetition; edge-weight validation by actually perturbing the source feature along its decoder direction at every token in its turn (36,000 interventions over 300 conversations at n = 2/4/6 turns, 40 highest-weighted cross-layer edges per graph, scored by Spearman, Pearson and sign agreement); and the completeness/replacement sufficiency metrics from the circuit-tracing work on unpruned graphs.

Step 6, a persona pipeline (Appendix I), reusing the contrastive-rollout recipe: generate responses under persona-encouraging and persona-suppressing system prompts, score and filter them for actual persona expression, take layer-19 activations, rank features by the ratio of mean activation on positive to negative rollouts, and drop features with holdout density above 20 % to remove generic high-firing ones. Persona descriptions are used only to generate the rollouts; the features are found by differential activation alone.

## What counts as an explanation here

A dictionary of features whose *descriptors* are LLM-written, at a deliberately coarser unit than the field's default — one feature vector per turn per layer. The claim being made is not that an individual feature is mechanistic but that the description set covers what the text is about, and that this coverage is what lets a downstream graph be small enough for a human to read. The attribution output is a graph, and the graph is explicitly *partial*: the pruning step keeps nodes contributing 80 % of logit influence and edges contributing 98 % of edge influence, and the 14-turn explanation is a manually constructed subgraph of that. Notably, the paper's own headline failure of per-token features is a *description* failure (no adversarial-prompt-detection feature exists to trace back from), so the explanation type on offer is a better-conditioned dictionary rather than a new causal object.

## How they judged success

Normalised MSE for reconstruction, on holdout, reported per architecture and split into turn-level and per-token columns (which makes the off-distribution problem visible: a per-token SAE asked to encode the turn mean gets 0.355 nMSE versus 0.097 for the turn-averaged model). 10-way matching accuracy for discrimination, on 2,000 holdout turns. Pairwise win rate over all 10 pairings (20,000 trials, randomised presentation order) and 5-way average rank plus first-place share for coverage, both from one judge model. A deterministic embedding-cosine coverage metric as a check that does not depend on the judge. For attribution: Δ in percentage points of target-paragraph attribution share over a neutral-query baseline; Spearman/Pearson/sign agreement between edge weights and measured intervention effects; completeness and replacement sufficiency. The controls worth naming are a *neutral-query baseline* for the paragraph test (so the claim is about a shift, not an absolute share), the *intervention* arm for the edge weights, and a judge-sensitivity ablation in Appendix E.

## What they found

Reconstruction: the turn-averaged SAE reaches 0.097 turn-averaged nMSE; the nested (16,384/16,384) model's inner partition alone reaches 0.116 despite carrying both loss terms, while its per-token error (0.167) is within noise of the pure per-token model (0.162) — so the nested model gets a usable turn-level dictionary essentially for free at matched width. The decoupled model is worse on both columns (0.128 / 0.212).

The two evaluation axes rank the architectures in opposite directions, which is the most transferable result in the paper. Per-token **max**-pooled features win discrimination outright (95.0 %) because their descriptors mention specific words and token patterns that uniquely fingerprint a text; turn-averaged features sit third at 73.9 %. On coverage the order reverses: turn-averaged features average 87.9 % pairwise wins, beat per-token max head-to-head 79.7 % of the time, achieve the best 5-way average rank (2.03) and are ranked first 44 % of the time, and lead the judge-free embedding metric at every k (0.663 at k = 3 versus 0.617 for per-token max; full table in Appendix H). Nested (max) is last almost everywhere, and the authors explain one oddity directly: the nested model's top-128 is ~99 % inner partition, so under max pooling it is behaving as a turn-averaged SAE with half the capacity.

Qualitatively, the worked examples do show the specific failure they predicted. On an arithmetic slip ("95" as the largest number under 100 without a 9), turn-averaged features include an error-recognition concept — that the answer is incorrect — while per-token features surface digits and arithmetic but never the incorrectness. On long, out-of-distribution documents (LongBench v2, 24K–32K tokens, ~150× the average training span, from a different corpus) the turn-averaged SAE still picks out macroeconomics and video-game subtopics that the per-token set misses, which is the paper's evidence that the property is a property of averaging rather than of training length.

Attribution: on the synthetic system prompt, attribution share at the targeted paragraph rises over the neutral baseline for all 8 queries under all three architectures, but the effect sizes are small in absolute terms — the largest is +1.9 percentage points on the identity paragraph and the rest are between +0.3 and +1.0 — so this case study demonstrates direction, not a sensitive test. In the 14-turn degradation conversation the turn-averaged subgraph reads coherently: repetition/degenerate-text at L21, adversarial-prompt-detection at L16, nuclear-weapons content at L11 and AI-safety disclaimers at L6 in earlier turns. The same attempt with per-token features has no adversarial-prompt-detection feature at all and traces back instead through "chemical compound names" and "JSON". Edge weights are validated: sign agreement is about 100 % averaged across graphs with a 95 % minimum on any single graph, per-token features give the strongest Spearman correlation and nested the strongest Pearson, turn-averaged correlations are weaker but significant, and correlations fall as conversations get longer. On sufficiency the nested model wins both completeness and replacement with a consistent nested > per-token > turn-averaged ordering at every conversation length — and it beats per-token features aggregated after the fact, despite its slightly worse per-token nMSE, which is the paper's argument that the architecture and not the aggregation does the work.

Two secondary results matter for method design. Appendix E: adding assistant prefill to the judge collapses the pairwise coverage result to 49.1 % — indistinguishable from chance — while Sonnet without prefill gives 83.1 % and Haiku in the two configurations gives 82.7 % and 62.7 %; all main-text numbers use the no-prefill configuration. Appendix G: SAEs trained on assistant turns only reconstruct user turns much worse, so the multi-layer SAEs are trained on both roles, which raises user-turn nMSE at layer 6 from 0.355 to 0.167 and leaves assistant-turn error essentially unchanged.

## Limits and open problems they name

Scale: one 7B model, 32,768-wide SAEs, one chat corpus — they flag the lack of any frontier-scale or production-data test as the main open step. Evaluation methodology: every stage is LLM-dependent (feature descriptions, the structured summaries, the coverage judgments), the autointerp prompts for the two architectures differ in what granularity of activation value they show, and even the embedding metric consumes LLM-written descriptions, so a ground-truth feature-quality benchmark that does not depend on an LLM is called out as future work. Qualitative analysis is thin by their own admission: examples were hand-picked for contrast, two long documents were selected from a pool of about 50, and the attribution subgraph was built by hand for a single conversation. They also decline to evaluate the persona pipeline quantitatively ("we have not attempted a broad quantitative evaluation of which architecture produces more relevant or comprehensive features"), and they say plainly that the top of their persona rankings contains noise — the Quantitative trait surfaces relevant features alongside an unclear one, which they attribute to stylistic shifts the contrastive prompts induce. Two structural questions are left open rather than answered: why completeness and replacement both increase with conversation length, and how information concentrating at particular tokens (period tokens, last token of a name) interacts with an operator that weights every position equally.

My own additions, not theirs: (i) turn averaging is a *token-uniform* pooling, and the paper's own related-work section says models concentrate information at specific positions — so the low-pass framing predicts the model will be least sensitive exactly where the model writes its summary, and no experiment here varies the weighting; (ii) the paragraph-attribution test has no sampled null distribution, so "+0.3 to +1.9 pp on 8 hand-built queries" is a demonstration that the sign is right, not a measured sensitivity; (iii) no random-direction or shuffled-span control appears anywhere in the coverage work, which matters because "descriptions mention high-level properties" is also what a good prompt-conditional summariser of the top examples would produce; (iv) the 20 %-density filter in the persona pipeline is the paper's only defence against generic features, and it is a filter on the *search* rather than a matched-firing-rate control on the reported hit.

## What we take from it

The reusable *framing* is that the unit of pooling is a design choice about what kind of description you can get, and the same read-out scores best or worst depending on which question you ask — so "our features are good" is not a claim until the reader knows whether the task is identification or characterisation. Concretely for us: if we want a probe that predicts a whole-response property (as opposed to flagging one moment), build it from a span mean; if we want one that discriminates near-identical contexts, build it from the extreme token. Publishing both numbers, as this paper does, is the only way to keep that choice from being retrofitted to whichever metric flattered us.

The reusable *measure* is the pair of axes and the neutral-baseline shift. An absolute attribution share is nearly meaningless on a prompt where one paragraph dominates by construction; a shift relative to a neutral query is comparable. We should adopt the pattern "reported effect = value under the target condition minus value under a matched neutral condition" wherever we cannot sample a null — and then say that it is a sign test rather than a sensitivity measurement.

The best *warning* in the paper is Appendix E: a judge configuration change (assistant prefill) turned an 83.1 % win rate into 49.1 %, and the honest authors moved all results to the configuration that worked. Two lessons. First, any LLM-judged number in our own work needs a judge-configuration ablation reported next to it, because the same evidence supports or refutes the claim depending on a formatting choice. Second, when a result is this fragile, the judge-free check is what carries the conclusion — here the embedding-cosine ranking agreed with the judge at every k, which is why the finding survives; a judge-only version of this paper would have been unpublishable for the right reason.

Two smaller takeaways worth keeping in the toolkit. The *nested* result — a Matryoshka inner loss at matched width, winning sufficiency despite slightly worse reconstruction — says reconstruction error at fixed width is not the quantity to optimise when the read-out must answer two differently-scaled questions, which is the same lesson the monthly series has been circling from other directions. And the persona ranking procedure (contrastive rollouts → LLM expression filter → ratio of mean activations → density ceiling → qualitative check) is a cheap way to go from a behaviour we care about to a small candidate feature set, *provided* we add the firing-rate-matched control feature the paper does not.
