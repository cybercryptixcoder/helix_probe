# Replicating Circuit Tracing for a Simple Known Mechanism

- **Key:** goodfire-com-research-replicating-circuit-tracing-for-a-simple-mechanism
- **Authors / organisation:** Jack Merullo, Connor Watts (co-first), Max Loeffler, Liv Gorton, Elana Simon, Tom McGrath, Owen Lewis; Goodfire Research
- **Date:** 2025-06-11
- **Link:** https://www.goodfire.com/research/replicating-circuit-tracing-for-a-simple-mechanism
- **Kind:** blog/research post
- **Read from:** full text (whole page, ~28.0k chars extracted; introduction, background, methods, per-feature analyses, discussion, limitations, references; the page ends at the citation block and the site's next-post carousel, so there is no appendix)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central) — it is the field's cleanest case of asking an interpretability instrument to *recover a mechanism that is already known*, and it publishes the mismatches rather than only the hits.

## What they wanted, and why
Cross-Layer Transcoders (CLTs) are the sparse-replacement architecture behind the attribution-graph style of circuit tracing: a model whose MLP computation is substituted by sparse features that are decoded into every later layer. Reports of the method had been validated mostly on tasks the method itself had been used to open up, so the open question was whether the pipeline recovers structure that independent, prior work already established by other means. The authors pick a task whose circuit was reverse-engineered neuron-by-neuron years earlier and ask whether a semi-automated feature-based pipeline lands in the same place. Their stated aim is validation of *interpretability as a discovery tool* rather than of any single feature: if an instrument cannot reproduce a mechanism we already know, its account of a mechanism we do not know is hard to defend. The larger goal is a pipeline that finds circuits without bespoke, hand-designed experiments for each task.

## What they studied
GPT-2 Small, with CLTs trained by the team: fine-tuning web text (100M tokens, the FineWeb corpus), expansion factor 64, which gives 49,152 features per layer and 589,824 features over the 12 layers, ReLU activations rather than the JumpReLU variant used in the original work. Their best dictionary is reported at L0 of 79 with 59% token accuracy, which they say is in line with the numbers in the method paper, and that dictionary is used for every experiment here. The unit of analysis is the **CLT feature**, with error nodes and input/output nodes as graph elements; the task is the digit-completion "greater-than" prompt family, where a two-digit year is masked and the model must emit a plausible later year. The comparison target is the published neuron-level account of that task in GPT-2 Small.

## How they did it
The pipeline runs in three stages. First, attribution graphs are built for a **single** prompt ("The war lasted from the year 1711 to 17") using their own reimplementation of the graph-construction and pruning algorithms, with the open-source graph visualiser for display, and without the manual clustering of near-duplicate features into supernodes that the original reports used. Second, features are ranked by edge attribution magnitude to the top-10 logits, taking the top 16 features per logit, which yields 160 candidate features before de-duplication. Third, each candidate is put through the same logit-attribution analysis the earlier neuron work used: because a CLT feature is decoded into all later layers rather than injecting one vector into the residual stream, its contribution is the activation-weighted **sum of its later-layer decoder vectors**, and that summed vector is dotted with each possible output-year unembedding to fill a years-by-years attribution matrix. That matrix is computed by running a local replacement model — the base model with its MLP outputs preserved and the CLT reconstruction added — over roughly 100 prompts, one per input year, so that the heatmap can be read as an input-output map for a single feature. Alongside each heatmap the authors show the feature's maximum-activating text examples, so every claim about what a feature *means* can be checked against what it *does to the logits*. Judgement is by hand: they group features into families by perceived function and describe representative members.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature dictionary plus an attribution graph, read at two levels at once: the feature's textual identity (its max-activating examples) and its causal footprint on the output distribution (the attribution heatmap). The explainable object is partial by construction — one prompt's graph, one prompt's worth of feature selection — and its predictive content is not a single number but the *shape* of the heatmap: whether the feature's contribution depends on the input as the known mechanism says it should (suppress everything below the input year, boost everything above it). The authors are explicitly repairing an instrument against ground truth rather than proposing a new method, so the explanation is judged by correspondence with an existing mechanistic story, not by coverage of the whole model.

## How they judged success
Three informal criteria, all qualitative: (1) does the graph put the computational weight where the known mechanism says it belongs — from the final digit of the input year, through middle-to-late layers, to the prediction position; (2) do the discovered features' heatmaps show the characteristic wedge of the greater-than mechanism, and do they do so *with input dependence*; (3) do the max-activating examples agree with the heatmaps. There is no held-out task, no faithfulness metric, no ablation arm, and no random-feature baseline — the comparison standard is a figure from the prior neuron-level paper. The honest description is expert visual inspection against a remembered figure, and the post is written that way.

## What they found
The pipeline recovers the mechanism. Feature ranking concentrates on the masked year's final digit and drains toward the final token position, with the densest flow in later layers, and the strongest features show the greater-than signature: promotion of years above a threshold, suppression below it. Feature 425104 is the top input feature for one output logit at layer 8, with examples that fire before the largest year in chronological contexts — including a date written in Roman numerals, which the authors read as evidence of semantic rather than character-level date representation. Feature 407684 does the same job but generalises outside dates, to prices, percentages and measurements. Two families beyond the known mechanism are reported as genuine discoveries: a **parity/opposition feature** (Feature 399423) that promotes even outputs after odd inputs and fires in ordinary contrastive constructions ("pros and cons", "right or wrong"), which the authors read as a general primitive for generating an opposite; and **structured-number features** keyed to multiples of five, or to numbers ending in 1 followed by 2. Feature 402486 ranks high (14th of 137 for its logit) yet implements the *inverse* comparison, favouring small numbers — they interpret it as a general detector for small-number contexts rather than a task component. Discrete properties in the text: 160 candidate features pre-deduplication; 49,152 features per layer and 589,824 in total; L0 79 / 59% token accuracy; a single selection prompt against ~100 analysis prompts. The paper's own most important finding, however, is a **disagreement**: several top features have heatmaps that barely depend on the input year (Feature 425104 promotes years above roughly 12 throughout and strongly above 60 *regardless of input*), while their examples clearly do depend on it; conversely Feature 349410 has a heatmap that is non-zero only for input year 11 yet fires before values under 30 in its examples. Feature 461858 is a narrow band (inputs 6–14, outputs 10–30) and Feature 349410 looks, in their words, like a special-case lookup — i.e. the discovered space is *less* clean than the neuron-level account, with no clear analogue in the prior work for the structured-number features.

## Limits and open problems they name
The heatmap-versus-examples divergence is unexplained and is the main limitation: they speculate that logit attribution scores a feature in isolation and therefore ignores interaction in the full-model context, and they flag a methodological worry of their own — attribution techniques were developed and validated for Transformers, whereas a CLT feature contributes several vectors across layers, so the transfer of the technique itself is unproven. Feature selection used one prompt, and aggregating over several prompts might remove distractors; they did not do it. They also did not use the position-to-position machinery that is CLT's advertised advantage, because logit attribution needs final-position features, so the experiment leaves the interactional half of the method untested. Open directions they name: a general account of number representation in CLTs across tasks, and a comparison against geometrically structured representations (helical number codes) in non-sparse models, as a case study in what a sparse replacement does to structured geometry.

## What we take from it
- **Method — the ground-truth recovery drill.** This is the template for validating any white-box predictor we build: pick a task where the mechanism is already established by independent work, run the pipeline blind, and score correspondence, including *negative* correspondence. It is cheaper than a new benchmark and strictly harder to game than a demo.
- **Warning — heatmap and examples are two different measurements, so report both or neither.** The post's central failure is a feature whose textual identity (fires before the largest number) and causal footprint (promotes big numbers regardless of input) disagree. Firing and effect are separable facts — the same lesson this folder has now met from steering, from crosscoders and from CLT routing — and a pipeline that reports only one of them would have called Feature 425104 a clean greater-than feature.
- **Warning — a sparse replacement changes the mechanism it is meant to describe.** The authors find narrow lookups and inverse-computation features that have no counterpart in the dense model's neuron-level story, and their candidate explanation for the structured-number extras is that a sparse dictionary fragments a smooth geometry into special cases. So "the circuit we found" in a replacement model is a claim about *that* model, exactly as this folder's dictionary entries keep insisting; the honest phrasing is recovery *of a related* mechanism.
- **Measure — summed multi-layer decoder vector.** For any architecture whose features write to several later layers, the analogue of a single-vector logit attribution is the activation-weighted sum of the decoder columns over the layers it targets, dotted with the unembedding. It is one line of code once a global replacement model exists, and it is what makes a cross-layer dictionary comparable at all to a neuron-level account.
- **Shortcut — build the local replacement model early.** Attribution heatmaps over a sweep of inputs were generated by re-running the local replacement model (base MLPs intact, CLT reconstruction added as error correction) on ~100 prompts. That arm is what makes the analysis diagnostic instead of illustrative, and it is reusable for any inversion question we ask of a sparse model.
