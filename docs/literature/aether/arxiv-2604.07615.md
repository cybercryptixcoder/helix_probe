# ADAG: Automatically Describing Attribution Graphs

- **Key:** arxiv-2604.07615
- **Authors / organisation:** Aryaman Arora, Zhengxuan Wu, Jacob Steinhardt, Sarah Schwettmann; Stanford University / Transluce
- **Date:** 2026-04-08
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07615
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** abstract and sections — arXiv HTML v1 read in one pass end to end (main body, references, Appendices A–G including the full prompt texts and the per-cluster steering tables); the accompanying `TransluceAI/circuits` code was not run and no figure was inspected, so every number below is prose or table.
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Circuit tracing already produces attribution graphs for a single prompt on a single model, but the step after tracing — saying what each feature in the graph is *doing* — has stayed manual: a researcher reads max-activating examples, runs logit lens, and forms a judgement. That step is what they want to automate, and they motivate it both by scale (frontier interpretability is moving from one-off case studies toward catalogues of circuits) and by audit (you cannot triage a jailbreak if reading its circuit takes hours). The larger goal is an interpretability pipeline with no human in the loop at any stage, so that circuit analysis can be run over datasets rather than over chosen prompts. They also formalise the target: interpretation means assigning each feature a *functional role in this computation*, not merely a label for when it fires.

## What they studied
Llama 3.1 8B Instruct as the main subject and Qwen3 32B as a replication, with the unit of analysis being individual MLP neurons (they adopt a neuron-basis tracing backbone from prior work reporting MLP-neuron circuits are about as sparse as SAE circuits). Features are then grouped into "supernodes" — clusters of 16–64 depending on dataset — and supernodes rather than neurons are what get described and steered. Datasets: a multi-hop state-capitals set (e.g. capital of the state containing Dallas), an automatically discovered harmful medical-advice jailbreak with roughly 150 semantically similar variants and a published success range across them, and, in the appendix, two-digit addition over 10,000 examples.

## How they did it
Four stages, each with its own evaluation. (1) **Tracing:** a locally linear replacement model keeps the forward pass and rewrites the backward pass — receptive-field-style relevance propagation that freezes nonlinearities as constants for the given input, blocks gradients through query and key paths, and halves gradients through elementwise products — then each MLP neuron gets an attribution score equal to activation times gradient of the summed top-5 logits, with edges weighted by an analogous source-to-target attribution. (2) **Attribution profiles:** for every feature they compute two vectors per prompt, an *input attribution* (activation times gradient with respect to each input token) and an *output contribution* (activation times gradient to each output logit). This is their answer to what they call locality bias in max-activating examples and logit lens: both describe a feature only by the token it fires on, and their measurements show that is insufficient (see findings). (3) **Clustering:** pairs of features are compared by cosine similarity of the two profile types in every context both appear in, combined by a clamped harmonic mean averaged over contexts — a form of multiple-kernel learning chosen because its low score when either profile anticorrelates penalises mixing features whose output effects oppose — then spectral clustering on the normalised Laplacian with a hand-set k. Three explicit quality properties motivate it: cohesive clusters (silhouette), balanced sizes (coefficient of variation), and no intra-cluster pairs with fully opposing contribution signs. (4) **Description:** explainer–simulator in two modalities. For input attributions they reuse fine-tuned 8B explainer/simulator models that highlight attributed tokens, and score a description by the simulator's per-token predictions correlated with the true attributions. For output contributions no such tool exists, so they prompt a frontier model to describe effects on the top-k logits (given scores scaled to a −10..10 range) and then to predict those scores from the description alone, scoring by Pearson correlation over all contexts. Best-of-20 descriptions per supernode; final labels are produced by summarising both descriptions with a larger model under a prompt that forbids vague words and wraps inhibitory clusters as `not[...]`. Steering is then used as the behavioural check: clamp a cluster's neurons to zero or double them and read the next-token distribution.

## What counts as an explanation here
A natural-language descriptor of a *group* of neurons, in two halves — what input tokens the group is attributed to, and which continuations it promotes or suppresses — plus a short label of one to three words. It is explicitly partial: a description of role in one computation on one dataset, not a mechanism and not a claim about what the neuron computes in general. Their one predictive commitment is the simulator score: the description should let a separate model forecast token-level attribution and logit effects, and that forecast is where the paper locates explanation quality. Everything else (the clustering, the labels) is scaffolding for making a graph human-readable at scale.

## How they judged success
Four separate judgements, none of them a single headline metric. Clustering: silhouette, size coefficient of variation, and percentage of intra-cluster pairs with all-opposing contribution signs, ablated across similarity variants (harmonic versus mean, normalised versus unnormalised Laplacian, post-hoc matrix transforms) and against k-means/Ward/RBF-spectral on concatenated profiles, at k = 16 and 64. Description: simulator Pearson r, human versus LLM explainer on the same information, with best-of-20 for the LLM and a quantile-versus-top-k threshold sweep. End-to-end: replicate a published hand-built circuit and steer each supernode, reporting resulting token probabilities. Application: attack success rate from 50 generations per steering setting judged harmful/not plus a separate coherence judge, with per-cluster attribution correlated against the ASR reported for the same prompt variants by the original jailbreak paper. Clustering quality on the capitals data: their harmonic variant at k=16 gets coefficient of variation 0.34 and silhouette 0.07 with 0.1% opposing-sign pairs, while concatenated-profile baselines get coefficients of variation 2.97–3.81 and 14–20% opposing-sign pairs.

## What they found
Non-locality is the paper's empirical core and its justification: input attribution from only the eight preceding tokens explains MLP neurons in the first three layers and increasingly fails after that, and in later layers the majority of input attribution goes to the beginning-of-sequence token at all, which they read as an attention-sink artefact and then exclude from profiles. For outputs, contribution is non-local the other way — in earlier layers more than half the time the top-contributing neuron sits at an earlier token position than the target — so the max-activating-example picture is systematically wrong about where a neuron's effect lands. On the capitals circuit, LLM descriptions match an expert human annotator on input attribution (mean 0.774 versus 0.779) and beat the human clearly on output contribution (0.812 versus 0.648), per-supernode scores ranging widely (e.g. Texas 0.871/0.997, "say Austin" 0.437/0.598). Steering reproduces the known circuit without a human: at a 96.5% baseline for the correct answer, nulling the cluster labelled as the capital-city-first-token group flips the top output to the state name at 93.4%, nulling the city–state group drops the correct answer to 52.7% with a neighbouring state's capital rising to 22.1%, and nulling an inhibitory cluster raises the correct answer to 98.4%. On the jailbreak, two clusters move it hard and in opposite attribution directions: nulling a "safety redirect" cluster raises attack success from 28% to 88%, while doubling a cluster triggered by one rhetoric token raises it to 90%, and across the variant set the two clusters' attributions correlate with the previously reported success of each variant at about −0.70 and +0.71. The addition appendix is the interesting negative: 256 clusters do find recognisable heuristics (sum-near-42, correct-even-sum, digit biases) but *no* cluster's steering changes the top prediction at all, under either multiplier.

## Limits and open problems they name
They say the similarity view is extensible and deliberately stop at two profile types. They also note that even a solved reverse-engineering project would still need an LLM to bridge formal descriptions to human language, and they point at trained end-to-end alternatives as the competing route. Beyond what they name: the number of clusters is chosen by hand and the main text carries no sensitivity analysis for that choice; there is no held-out prompt family for the descriptions (explainer and simulator see the same contexts), so a description can be good at context-specific pattern matching rather than at being a role; the behavioural check is steering by clamping whole clusters, with no random-cluster control of matched size and no dose axis beyond zero and double; and the claim that supernodes are causal is untested beyond the example that starts it, with the addition appendix as direct evidence of the gap — a cluster can carry a clean label and precise attribution and have no effect on the prediction. Descriptions are scored against attribution profiles that the same linearised replacement model produced, so the simulator's r is partly a measure of agreement with a particular linearisation. Cluster labels from an LLM are consumed by the summariser and never independently checked by a human in the reported tables, and the handedness of some results is worth flagging: nulling the "state name bias" cluster and nulling the inhibitory "not[southern capitals]" cluster *both* raise the correct answer's probability, i.e. two clusters whose labels are opposite in spirit move the output the same way, which is what a non-additive circuit plus an incomplete cluster partition looks like.

## What we take from it
Four things. First, a **measure** worth copying into our own explanations: describe a unit by its attribution *to* inputs and its contribution *to* outputs rather than by its firing examples, and score the description by whether a separate model can reproduce those profiles from the text — the simulator r is a cheap predictive test that a white-box model owes anyway, and unlike auto-interp's explain-then-simulate it is computed against gradients rather than against corpus behaviour. Second, a **method for reducing a graph**: cluster by profile similarity with an explicit penalty on anticorrelated output effects, because antagonistic pairs are exactly what a steering-based summary cancels out, and publish the three clustering statistics so a reader can see whether the partition is degenerate. Third, a **warning** this paper makes concrete rather than asserts: on addition every cluster is describable and corroborated in both attribution and contribution, and none of them moves the output under either steering multiplier, so "the explanation got the attribution right" and "this group of neurons is a mediator" are separate claims — the split our own entries should carry. Fourth, a practical constraint: the describer needs a fine-tuned explainer/simulator for the model's tokeniser, and for Qwen3 they fall back to prompting a frontier model, so explanation quality is partly a property of the tooling rather than of the network.
