## What they wanted, and why
The authors frame probing as having two established uses — monitoring a model at test time, and steering it — and propose a third: probes as a *reward function* for training. Their motivating problem is supervision cost. For open-ended behaviours such as factual accuracy there is no cheap verifier; the only faithful signal is a human or a web-search-equipped judge, which is far too slow and expensive to run inside an RL loop. If a model's own features already encode the concept, the argument goes, a cheap read-out of those features can stand in for the expensive verifier, amortising fact-checking into one probe-training run. Hallucination reduction is chosen as the testbed precisely because it is the canonical hard case: if a feature reward works there, the recipe plausibly transfers to other open-ended objectives. The larger goal is what Goodfire calls intentional design — using interpretability to shape training rather than just to describe a finished model.

## What they studied
Gemma-3-12B-IT as both student policy and the source of activations; Gemini 2.5 Pro (with web search) as the expensive ground-truth grader used only to label probe training data and to evaluate. Data is LongFact++ — roughly 20k long-form prompts across eight factual domains, with 999 prompts held out as the test set. The unit of analysis is not a feature dictionary but four purpose-built *probes* reading the residual stream at layers 20 and 30: a token-level localization probe, a span-level hallucination-classification probe, and two reward probes grading corrections and retractions respectively. The reported "feature" is the probe's scalar output, interpreted explicitly as the model's posterior belief that an entity is (un)supportable.

## How they did it
Data collection first: sample completions from the base model on LongFact++ prompts, have Gemini extract falsifiable spans ("entities") and grade each as supported, not supported, or insufficient information, then train the two monitoring probes to imitate that pipeline — localization predicting whether a token and its predecessor sit in the same entity, classification predicting the hallucination label from activations pooled across the entity. Probes are attention-based rather than linear: a 4-layer sliding-window causal transformer for localization, and single-query multi-head attention probes (2048-dim, 16 heads for classification; 1024-dim with softmax heads for the two reward probes) over multi-layer inputs from layers 20 and 30 concatenated along the sequence. Training follows a two-stage loop: when the classification probe fires, the policy is given a sub-context in which it must choose to maintain, correct, or retract the flagged entity and write that intervention; the two reward probes then grade the sampled interventions; the grades plus a multiplicative rubric (legibility and on-topic-ness judged by the base model in token space) form the RL reward. Critically, the reward probes are run on activations from a **frozen copy of the base model**, not the student, so no gradient flows through the probes. Training is 360 steps of ScaleRL with CISPO at a reported cost of roughly $2.5k. At test time, best-of-n sampling over interventions is scored by the same reward probes and aggregated by majority vote on the action before taking the highest-reward sample; interventions are either placed inline in the completion (letting later text condition on them) or kept aside.

## What counts as an explanation here
A *probe with a declared semantic role* — the scalar is read as a calibrated belief, and the claim attached to it is that the belief is a control handle, not a description of a mechanism. Nothing here is claimed to be complete or legible: the post never says what the classification probe reads, only that it predicts well and that its output can be optimised against. The predictive content is narrow but real: the belief read-out predicts where the model will hallucinate and predicts the quality of an intervention, and the authors' own demonstration that the read-out survives training and can drive test-time selection is the strongest evidence they offer that the quantity is a stable internal variable rather than a coincidence.

## How they judged success
Headline metric is the fraction of entities that are hallucinations on the 999 held-out prompts, as graded by Gemini 2.5 Pro, decomposed into three additive components: *policy reduction* (the policy alone hallucinating less), *in-context reduction* (inline interventions suppressing downstream compounding), and *direct reduction* (interventions actually fixing or withdrawing an individual claim). Probe quality is judged by AUC-ROC on held-out data plus calibration (the claim that a probe score of .XY corresponds to an XY% chance of the positive class) plus a qualitative sweep of predictions along sample text. Test-time behaviour is measured at a fixed classification threshold of 0.7, never tuned for evaluation, giving precision and recall for the monitoring pipeline on policy-generated text. Off-target effects are checked on ten standard benchmarks (HellaSwag, PIQA, ARC-c/e, WinoGrande, BBH, MMLU, MATH, GSM8K, GPQA), by the average number of falsifiable claims per completion (with seeds), by a blind pairwise Gemini preference rating over 1000+ pairs, and by per-token forward KL divergence between base and trained policy, split into supported and not-supported tokens. Baselines: base model with the monitoring pipeline but no training, RLFR without best-of-n, RLFR without inline interventions, and the strongest external one — Gemma-3-12B grading its own interventions on a 1–10 scale as an LLM-as-judge scorer at matched sample counts.

## What they found
The topline is a 58% reduction in the hallucination rate on the held-out set with best-of-32 sampling inside the probing harness, decomposed as 10% policy, 35% in-context and 13% direct — note that the three sum to the 58 only loosely in the text and the paper's own Figure 4 caption gives the direct component as 13%. Dropping best-of-n costs a little, mostly through worse interventions. Dropping inline placement removes the in-context component but still leaves 31% overall, which the authors themselves say is comparable to the untrained base model with the same monitor and inlining. The honest reading of the decomposition is that roughly two-thirds of the headline came from a structural choice (inserting corrections into the stream, which the untrained model already exploits to 24.9%) rather than from learning. Per-token KL is 10–20% larger on not-supported than supported tokens for completions from both models, which is the closest thing to a mechanistic claim in the paper, and the authors flag large seed variation. On the trained policy the monitoring pipeline reaches 0.85 precision / 0.56 recall on not-inline interventions and 0.88 / 0.61 inline at the untuned 0.7 threshold, against 0.94 AUC in the validation environment. Claim count is unchanged (63.4 ± 0.4 vs 63.6 ± 0.3 entities per completion over three seeds), so the policy did not get out of hallucinating by saying less; inline runs sit lower at 59.1 ± 0.8. Benchmarks are flat within noise, and Gemini prefers the trained policy's completions 50.9% of the time over 1000+ pairs. At best-of-256 the feature-reward scorer beats Gemma's own token-level self-grading by nearly 15 points — the model ranks interventions well in activation space but cannot express that ranking in text. Cost is the other headline: about $3,818 to compute rewards for the first 300 steps versus about $344,064 for the same steps with a Gemini-with-search judge, roughly two orders of magnitude, and 3–5 RL steps versus 360 at equal budget. The reward probes trained on base activations transfer: fixed/retracted rates are essentially identical when the same probes are run on the trained policy's activations, which is what makes best-of-n at inference affordable.

## Limits and open problems they name
They name the monitor-evasion worry directly and give it their mitigation rather than a proof: the probes run on frozen parameters, the student never optimises the probe directly, and in this setting learning the behaviour proved easier than evading the probe — with the caveat that further optimisation might flip that. They concede their evaluation reuses the same prompting and tooling stack as data collection, so most labels are internally consistent by construction, and answer it with red-teaming and manual auditing rather than with an independent grader. They report one remaining degeneration mode: an early wrong intervention that contradicts the question's premise makes the policy keep intervening on its own later text, giving up on the question. Appendix G is explicitly preliminary and says the mechanism behind the intervention effect (and behind the large in-context component) remains elusive.

Limits I would add: (i) the headline is measured against an absolute hallucination rate with no control for the sampler — best-of-32 with a *shuffled* reward would establish how much of the gain is rejection sampling with any monotone scorer; (ii) the reductions are attributed by ablation of the system's parts, not by an additive decomposition that was verified to be additive, so the 10/35/13 split should be treated as an accounting convenience; (iii) the strongest external baseline (Gemma self-grading) is a weak judge, so "features beat judges" is really "features beat this one cheap judge"; (iv) nothing in the paper measures the classification probe's accuracy at the *decision boundary* the RL loop actually queries, i.e. on entities the student newly produces after training; (v) grading by Gemini with web search means the target itself moves with a vendor's search index, and no second grader is used to bound label error.

## What we take from it
- **A method:** the frozen-teacher reward loop. Reading a *frozen copy* to score the student's outputs is the design that lets you optimise hard against a read-out without the student learning to fool it, and it is the cheapest available answer to "won't the model just change its representation?" — a question our own planned interventions must answer.
- **A measure:** the probe-transfer test. Run the same probe on the trained model's activations and check that *ordering*, not just sign, is preserved. This is a concrete, cheap calibration of how stable a read-out is across a training intervention, and it is exactly the quantity a predictive white-box model needs to be checked on.
- **A framing:** treating a probe score as a posterior belief with a calibration requirement, rather than as a classifier output. Calibration is what licenses using the scalar as a reward at all, so any read-out we want to plug into a loop should be reported with a reliability diagram, not just an AUC.
- **A warning:** the decomposition. Most of the improvement came from inserting text into the context, which an untrained model also exploits. A white-box claim that "the probe taught the model X" needs the inline/no-inline and best-of-n ablations side by side, or it will credit the representation for a prompt effect.
- **A shortcut:** cost. Reading activations instead of generating and judging text is roughly two orders of magnitude cheaper at the same reward quality, which is the practical argument for spending more compute on the read-out side of our pipeline than on the scoring side.
