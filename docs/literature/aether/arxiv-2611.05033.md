# Whose Effect Is It? How Objectives Inflate Learned Subspace Interventions

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.05033
- **Authors / organisation:** Toygar Tanyel; Serkan Ayvaz (Centre for Industrial Software, University of Southern Denmark)
- **Date:** 2026-11-03
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05033
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** abstract and sections
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

A standard result in this literature is that a *learned* subspace intervention changes the model's output more than copying the whole state at the same place does — on CausalGym, the learned method's score is a median two to three times the whole-state baseline, and one published sentiment direction scores about 110% of the theoretical maximum. The usual reading is that the learned part isolates the concept more cleanly. The authors point out the obvious problem: the part is contained in the whole, and copying the whole state learns nothing while copying a learned part learns something. So where did the extra effect come from — the network, or the training objective? Their goal is to split a learned effect into the part the network itself produces at that site and an excess above it, then say when the excess exists, how large it can be, and which kind of objective puts a direction where in that range. The larger goal is a reporting standard: a reference that involves no training, so that "this direction has a bigger effect" can never again be read as "this direction is better evidence".

## What they studied

Ninety tasks from six published sources (29 CausalGym tasks plus re-paired number-only variants, negation constructions, sentiment templates, SST, RAVEL, IOI in the original templates and a custom frame); fourteen base checkpoints from 124M to 32B across six families (GPT-2, Pythia, SmolLM, Qwen2.5, Llama, Gemma-2), with Pythia, Qwen2.5 and Llama each at three or more sizes so size can be compared within a family. The unit of analysis is a **subspace of the residual stream at a (layer, position) site** — rank one everywhere inside the network, ranks 2 to 64 at the readout — chosen either by distributed alignment search (DAS, trained) or by an untrained rule (difference of means, probe, PCA, k-means, LDA). The measurement is a scalar log-odds difference between the source and base answer tokens, normalised by the *total effect* T, which is the change when the whole input is swapped.

## How they did it

Everything is built on one decomposition. Write the observable as a function of the state at a site with the rest of the model recomputed after it; expand around the midpoint between base and source states; then to first order a rank-one interchange along a unit direction has mean effect `d^T M d` where `M` is the symmetric part of `E[w Δ^T]`, `w` is the readout gradient at the midpoint and `Δ` is the state change. By the Rayleigh quotient this effect sweeps exactly the interval between the smallest and largest eigenvalues of `M`, while whole-state patching — the patch with the full `Δ` — has first-order effect equal to the **trace** of `M`. Since an orthonormal basis sums to the trace, a part can beat the whole exactly when the remaining parts sum to something negative, i.e. when they pull the output back; no cleaner isolation of the variable is required. Rank-k follows the same way (the Ky Fan bound gives the sum of the k largest eigenvalues as the cap), and the *effect-seeking* / *target-aimed* / *representation-only* taxonomy of objectives then says where a trained direction lands: an objective strictly rewarded for a larger effect picks the top eigenvector and reports the maximum, while an objective minimised at a target level set picks the level set. The whole first-order argument is machine-checked in Lean 4. Empirically: at the **readout** (a complete cut, where whole-state patching provably reproduces T exactly) they train rank-one DAS with four objectives — cross-entropy, per-pair squared error to T, the CDAS divergence, and the mean effect itself — from three initialisations, keep the directions after one pass and after ten, and score exact effects on test pairs resampled per cluster with the mirror included. They search for the true maximum effect by gradient ascent on the unit sphere over training pairs and evaluate it out of sample; they also compute a predicted maximum from `M` before any training. Inside the **network**, every region at every second layer in the six small models is mapped, comparing cross-entropy DAS against whole-state patching at the same cell *and* against a stronger reference `G*` — whole-state patching of the cell alone or together with one other region of the same layer, whichever is larger on training pairs. A cell where the learned effect is significant but `G*` is not is called **off-route**. They separate the share of any effect that runs through attention by freezing every downstream attention pattern to the base run's. Finally they re-run two published results — CausalGym's ranking of seven methods, and the sentiment direction reported at 110% — with the reference measured at the same site.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation of the model, but a **decomposition with a reference**: a learned effect is split into `G` (what the network itself does at that site, measured by a training-free copy of the whole state) and an excess, with a first-order theory saying the excess lives in a computable range whose upper end is known before training. It predicts, concretely and before any fitting: the maximum effect of any single direction at a site is the top eigenvalue of `M`; whole-state patching gives the trace; under a shared readout direction the ratio of maximum to trace is `½(1 + 1/cos θ)` where θ is the angle between the readout gradient and the mean state change, maximised at the bisector. This is the most directly falsifiable theory in this folder's recent entries — and its empirical claims are checked against exact forward-pass effects, not against its own proxy.

## How they judged success

Exact forward-pass effects on held-out test pairs as the ground truth throughout; the first-order theory is scored by how close its trace lands to T (median 0.05–3.8% error per model) and by a sharp signed prediction: on mirrored test sets the midpoint-gradient trace error should be minus one-half the base-point error, observed in 880 of 882 cases. Falsification targets are the predicted maximum vs a gradient-ascent search over directions (out-of-sample), and the angle-prediction of the maximum within a median of 2.1–5.6% per model with rank correlation 0.71–0.98. Controls are 95% bootstrap intervals that resample test clusters with their mirrors, a population rule that keeps only cases with ≥20 test clusters and T's interval above zero, a lemma-effect confound test for the number-agreement tasks (pairs rebuilt to change only number, and only lemma), held-out templates for IOI and RAVEL, the same-initialisation comparison across objectives, ten-pass training to rule out underfitting, and a FDR-controlled count over cells for the maps. Gemma-2 (soft logit cap) is separated out of all first-order counts, and the authors say they made that split after seeing its results.

## What they found

The excess is real, large, and mostly the objective's. At the readout, cross-entropy DAS lands above T in 1096 of 1124 cases with a computable margin (1103 of 1168 overall), at a median of 1.64–3.00 T per model and 0.76–0.97 of the maximum effect. Changing *only* the loss — same initialisations, same site, same rank — moves it to a median 3.0% from T with per-pair squared error aimed at T, and to 0.91–1.06 T with the CDAS divergence. Inside the network, cross-entropy DAS lies significantly above `G` at 16,489 of 18,221 live cells (median excess 0.14 T), above the harder `G*` at 15,998, and 0.51–0.64 of its summed effect lies beyond `G*`; 4300 cells are off-route and their effects are mostly small (median 0.04 T). Freezing attention removes 0.58 of the summed excess over `G` and only 0.10 of it survives — of the excess that runs through attention, most runs through the readout position's own attention — while freezing removes only 0.10 of whole-state patching's summed effect. A squared error aimed at the *cell's* `G` instead of at T lands on `G` (slope 0.91, intercept 0.00) where the same loss aimed at T sits a median 0.09 T above it, so the objective does not merely bias a method toward "more effect" — it aims it at whatever target you name. The confounds are closed: a second feature that changes with the target would lower `G` but the number-only re-paired tasks reproduce the excess in all 84 cases; held-out templates reproduce it in 13/13 IOI cases and 223/224 RAVEL cases. Two published claims are re-examined: CausalGym's DAS/vanilla ratio (median 2.1–3.1) is 1.02–1.08 times DAS's excess over `G*` at the same regions, i.e. the lead is an excess rather than a better region; and the 109.8% sentiment direction re-runs at 114.7% with a layer-accounting band of [85.0, 137.4] that contains 100%, so it is not reliably above the complete-cut ceiling. Before training, one angle does predict the ceiling: the median angle between readout direction and mean state change is 79.7°, and since most of the state change is nearly orthogonal to what the readout reads, the maximum is far above the trace. Size matters: from smallest to largest within a family the maximum effect grows 24–41% per task and DAS 16–29%, and the predicted maximum tracks it, so the growth is a property of the network rather than of the search. Higher ranks do not rescue the story: outside Gemma-2 no cross-entropy run exceeds the rank-k predicted maximum, and on the 20-name IOI task rank one *understates* it — the ceiling stays below T at rank one but every case is above it from rank 16 on.

## Limits and open problems they name

First order throughout; the remaining exact deviations are explained but not modelled (the Gemma-2 logit cap, and inside the network where the excess above the predicted maximum is mostly attention and freezing removes 0.85 of it). Measurements are base checkpoints only, not instruction-tuned models; inside the network only rank one is used and the 7–9B models are described on three syntactic tasks only. The reference itself is bounded: `G*` patches the last token of a region with at most one other region of the layer, so an effect the network produces only through earlier tokens of a region, or through three or more regions jointly, is outside it. They also note that their account is about objectives, not about DAS, and that whether inputs (not just learned edits) can open the off-route attention routes is open.

## What we take from it

- **The reporting control we should adopt immediately.** A learned effect means nothing without a training-free reference measured at the same site; and at a complete cut that reference has a known exact value. This is cheaper and sharper than any control task or random-network baseline, because it is local. For us: for every read-out, probe or direction we credit with an effect, report the whole-state (or whole-slice) copy at the same site, the ratio, and the share the effect loses when attention is frozen.
- **A distinction between "predicts" and "produces".** A linear proxy for whole-state patching (gradient × displacement) estimates the *reference*; a learned subspace direction with an effect-seeking objective reports the *ceiling*. Two different quantities, routinely compared to each other in the literature. Any time we compare our surrogate's prediction to a trained method's effect, we are comparing trace to top eigenvalue.
- **A computable prior for any direction.** The Rayleigh picture says the maximum single-direction effect at a site is computable from one pass of gradients, before any training, and the empirically measured max lands close to it. That is a rare case of a genuinely out-of-sample prediction of an intervention effect — exactly the shape our own predictive model should imitate, including the "search the sphere and score out of sample" evaluation.
- **The geometric core, which is the sharpest warning in the recent batch.** Under one shared readout direction, any direction strictly between the mean state change and the readout gradient beats the whole-state copy, maximised at the bisector; with a 79.7-degree median angle the gap is large. The bisector is *not* a better handle on the variable — it moves the state along a coordinate the input does not change and the rest pulls back. So "our direction has a bigger effect than the copy" is not evidence of anything except that our objective was rewarded for it.
- **A discipline for ablations and edits too.** The same question transfers even though the numbers do not: an edit trained to remove a behaviour may suppress it through a route the network does not use, leaving the network's own route intact. Worth naming as a risk in any safety-motivated edit we evaluate.
