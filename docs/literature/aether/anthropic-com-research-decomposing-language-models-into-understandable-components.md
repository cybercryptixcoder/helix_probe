## What they wanted, and why

The post is the public-facing statement of the argument that the neuron is the wrong unit of analysis. The opening move is an analogy to neuroscience — recording every unit, silencing or stimulating any of them, replaying any input — followed immediately by the point that makes the analogy turn sour: in artificial networks we do have that access, and it still does not give understanding, because a single unit's activity does not correspond to one thing. The example given is a neuron in a small language model that fires across academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests and Korean text; the vision version is a unit for cat faces and car fronts. If the parts are polysemantic then the decomposition into parts is the first thing that has to be fixed, before any circuit can be traced. Their stated target is therefore a *unit* that is interpretable, and their stated end use is monitoring and steering model behaviour from the inside, which is why this is filed under safety rather than under science.

## What they studied

The unit of analysis is the **feature**: a direction in MLP activation space, defined as a linear combination of neurons rather than a single neuron, and deliberately more numerous than the neurons that host them. Models: a one-layer transformer with a 512-neuron ReLU MLP, trained on the Pile, reproduced in a second seed for universality, plus a weight-shuffled copy of the first as a control for "is this just the data distribution". Dictionaries range from 512 features (1×, i.e. not overcomplete) to 131,072 (256×); the detailed analyses concentrate on a 4,096-feature run called A/1, with A/0 at 512 and A/2 at 16,384 used for the resolution comparison.

## How they did it

Train a sparse autoencoder on MLP activations — encoder is a linear map plus ReLU, decoder is linear, the pre-encoder and post-decoder biases are tied so the whole thing amounts to subtracting one fixed bias and then reconstructing — with MSE on the activations and an L1 penalty on the hidden layer, Adam, 8 billion activation samples. Two training practices are singled out as load-bearing: more data makes features visibly sharper, and "dead" units that stop firing are periodically re-seeded onto inputs the current dictionary reconstructs badly (neuron resampling). The post's own framing of why this is dictionary learning at all is the compressed-sensing point: inverting a very rectangular matrix is only possible because the answer is sought in a sparse high-dimensional vector.

Validation runs on five tracks. (a) Four cherry-picked features — Arabic script, DNA, base64, Hebrew — each get a computational proxy for their context (a log-likelihood-ratio score built from Unicode blocks or the alphabet of the format), and are checked for specificity (when the feature is on, is the context there?), sensitivity (when the context is on, is the feature there?), a downstream effect that agrees with the story (logit weights computed by the path expansion through the down-projection and unembedding, plus causal ablation and "pinned" sampling with the feature forced high), non-alignment with any single neuron (correlation with the best-matching neuron, and the feature's coefficients in the neuron basis), and replication in the other seed. (b) A blinded human rubric over 412 activation intervals across 162 features and neurons, sampled uniformly across the activation spectrum rather than only at the maximum — explicitly to avoid the trap that a polysemantic unit looks clean at its top examples. (c) Automated interpretability: Claude writes an explanation from activations, then predicts activations on held-out text (Spearman correlation, 540 predictions per feature), and separately predicts whether an unseen token is one the feature promotes (scored against a 50/50 mix of top positive logit tokens and random tokens). (d) A global question — how much of the MLP is explained — measured as the fraction of the MLP's log-likelihood loss reduction that survives when the autoencoder reconstruction is spliced in. (e) Phenomenology: feature families, splitting across dictionary sizes, universality across seeds, and chains of features that hand off through the token stream.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **feature dictionary**: an overcomplete set of directions, each with a name, the contexts that activate it, and the token-level effects it has. It is explicitly partial and explicitly a foothold rather than an understanding of the model — the authors' own words are that decomposing is only the beginning, and the enumerated payoffs are concrete: attribute a layer's output on one example to named parts, monitor for a feature's presence, change behaviour by changing feature values, demonstrate that a property of the data was learned, demonstrate that it was *used* on a given input, and design inputs that elicit it. The post is careful about what kind of object a feature is: it declines to say whether the model is "really" made of features or whether they are a convenient post-hoc description, and offers universality as the only evidence that they exist beyond one model. Notably the post also claims a predictive role for the decomposition — the knob over dictionary size is presented as control over the resolution at which the model is seen, coarse where coarse is legible and fine where fine is informative.

## How they judged success

Four independent lines, with the quantitative backbone being human rubric scores versus neurons and two automated-interpretability correlations versus neurons; specificity and sensitivity expressed as proxy-feature Pearson correlations over ~40 million tokens; downstream effect measured by logit weights, by ablation (feature zeroed across a context, per-token loss change measured) and by pinned sampling; neuron alignment by searching each neuron's top-20 dataset examples for the context and by the feature's neuron-basis coefficients; a baseline for "is the feature just the data" via the shuffled-weight transformer; and the loss-recovered number as the only global coverage measure. The post itself names no metric and reports no numbers except the feature counts — the whole argument in the post is qualitative, and this is worth noticing as a genre pattern.

## What they found

A 512-neuron layer decomposes into more than 4,000 features that separately track things like DNA sequences, legal language, HTTP requests, Hebrew and nutrition labels, most of which are invisible in the neuron basis. Concretely from the paper: 81% of the tokens where the Arabic feature A/1/3450 fires are Arabic script even though Arabic is 0.13% of training tokens, and its correlation with the proxy is 0.74; Hebrew A/1/416 sits at 0.55, DNA A/1/2937 at 0.8 (binarised proxy); base64 A/1/2357 is only 0.38 because the proxy is too broad — it also catches hex strings, which have their own feature. The Arabic feature has no neuron twin: only one neuron has Arabic in its top-20 examples (a single example), the three largest coefficients of the feature in the neuron basis are all *negative*, 27 neurons have coefficients above 0.1 in magnitude, and the most-correlated neuron turns out to be a generic non-English-language unit, which is read as a superposition strategy — mutually exclusive languages are natural to stack on one neuron. Cross-seed replication is strong: Arabic 0.91, DNA 0.92, Hebrew 0.92, base64 0.85, and median activation correlation to the closest feature in the other model is 0.72 across all of A/1 while the closest *neuron* pair only reaches 0.46. Human rubric: median neuron scores 0, median feature interval scores 12 (a score above 8 was taken as clearly interpretable, so the median neuron is "the annotator could not form a hypothesis at all"). Automated interpretability: logit-token prediction 74% for features against 58% for neurons, chance 50%. Coverage: A/1 recovers 79% of the MLP's log-likelihood loss reduction, and the 131,072-feature run recovers 94.5%, which the authors immediately discount as a measure because a long tail of features means the residual gets ever more expensive to explain.

Steering works in the direction the feature says: activating the base64 feature makes the model emit base64, activating the Arabic feature produces Arabic. Universality is claimed in a stronger sense than seed-replication because equivalent features were reported elsewhere — base64, hex and all-caps features match neurons from the separately-trained SoLU models, and a "pgen"/"pone" feature shows that roughly one token in ten thousand in the Pile is a PLoS citation abbreviation, which is presented as the decomposition revealing a property of the *corpus*. Dictionary size behaves as a resolution knob: base64 goes from 1 feature at 512 to 3 at 4,096, splitting into letters-versus-digits and then into base64 that encodes ASCII text (the ASCII one discovered only because a token decoding to six spaces was its top example), with the digit sub-feature predicting non-digits because the tokenizer would otherwise have merged them. A shuffle-weights control yields only single-token features and arbitrary-looking subsets, which the authors read as evidence that the structure is the model's, not the corpus's. Finally, chains of features hand off through generated tokens — a four-node HTML cycle from open-tag to tag-name to close to whitespace — and the authors are explicit that these are not circuits: the model never learned them to cooperate, they interact only because the data has that structure.

## Limits and open problems they name

The post's headline admission is the strongest thing in it: individual neurons being uninterpretable had been the roadblock, and the next primary obstacle is now *engineering* rather than science. The paper is blunter about the gaps. There is no trusted metric for whether a dictionary is good — an information-based objective they tried did not track interpretability, so they fell back on eyeballing features, feature density, reconstruction loss and toy models with known ground truth. Architectural fixes are declared dead on argument, not just on engineering: they show with a four-mutually-exclusive-feature one-neuron loss calculation (0.8 for a monosemantic neuron versus 0.7 for a polysemantic one) that under cross-entropy a model prefers ambiguity even with no superposition possible, so 1-hot-activation training removes superposition without producing clean neurons. Interpretability degrades in the low-activation tail and they cannot rule out that their features sit at a slight angle to the features that exist. Coverage is unmeasurable in principle as posed — fraction-of-loss is misleading. They cannot say whether token-in-context features (over a hundred distinct "the"s in the 16k dictionary) are the model's local code or an artefact of their L1 pushing toward local codes, though they lean toward the former because sharper predictions require it. "Are these the real features?" stays open, with feature splitting and proposed feature manifolds offered as reasons to think the question of a correct number of features may be ill-posed. Scaling is the stated worry: a 100× dictionary on a 10,000-wide MLP layer would be ~20 billion parameters and may cost more than the original model, and there is no scaling law for expansion factor or data. Attentional superposition is named as unresolved.

## What we take from it

**A warning, and the sharpest one in this entry:** neuron-level probing of a small layer will show almost nothing where a feature-level readout shows a clean signal — the Arabic unit was effectively invisible neuron-wise even though it is 0.74-correlated with a proxy and its ablation moves exactly the right tokens. If our probe is linear on the neuron basis, the natural conclusion that a property is "not represented" is unwarranted. **A measure:** cross-model activation correlation (Spearman/Pearson over the same token set) as the cheap, model-independent test of whether a probe direction is a property of the model rather than of the data; the paper's own use of it — 0.72 median across implementations against 0.46 for neurons — is our best available baseline for "did we find something real", and a shuffled-weight model is the right null. **A control with teeth:** shuffle the weights of the trained network rather than retraining; a decomposition that still finds structure under shuffling was measuring the corpus. **A design constraint:** interpretability that only holds at the top of the activation spectrum is not interpretability — score across intervals, because the polysemanticity hides underneath. **A framing we should adopt and then improve on:** the paper validates features by their *downstream* effects, which is precisely the slot a predictive white-box model is supposed to fill from the inside without running the model; the logit-weight path expansion and per-token ablation effect are the two quantities we should be able to reproduce analytically. **A caution for our own claims:** features that recur across models are the only evidence the paper offers that features exist beyond one model, so any claim about a "true" decomposition in our work inherits exactly the same weakness until we show replication.
