## What they wanted, and why

Reverse-engineering a network bottom-up (circuits, induction heads) only works on toy models, and reading samples top-down cannot distinguish "this output came from memorising a passage" from "this output came from a world model". They want a third source of evidence that is still top-down but counterfactual: **which training sequences, if added or removed, would change this particular behaviour?** They frame this as the question a lab must answer when a model says something false, solves a maths problem, or pleads not to be shut down — regurgitation or composition. The stated larger goal is to connect small-model mechanism to large-model behaviour from both ends and "meet in the middle".

## What they studied

Four decoder-only GPT-style language models from an earlier Anthropic scaling study: roughly 810M, 6.4B, 22B and 52B parameters. Unit of analysis is the **training sequence** (512 tokens drawn from the pretraining distribution) and, through a block-diagonal Hessian approximation, the **MLP layer** and the **token** inside a sequence. Queries are prompt+completion pairs: factual completions (`first_president`, `inflation`, `water`), memorised passages (`tolstoy`, `gettysburg_address`), code and maths (`math_clips`, `binary_search`, `quick_sort`), translation (`english_to_mandarin`), and Assistant-derived role-play prompts (`shutdown`, `paperclips`, `superintelligent`, `trade`, `objective`). Only MLP weights enter the computation; attention, embeddings and LayerNorms are treated as fixed.

## How they did it

The measured quantity is the change in completion log-likelihood `log p(completion | prompt)` when a candidate sequence is added to the training set, estimated to first order as the query gradient dotted with the inverse-Hessian-vector product applied to the candidate's training gradient. Two bottlenecks are attacked. **(1) The IHVP.** Instead of running an iterative solver (LiSSA) for every measurement, they fit a parametric approximation to the Gauss-Newton Hessian once per model — K-FAC represents each MLP layer's block as a Kronecker product of an activation covariance and a pre-activation-pseudo-gradient covariance, and EK-FAC additionally fits the eigenvalues of that Kronecker product individually, which is cheap because eigendecompositions distribute over Kronecker products. Pseudo-gradients (labels sampled from the model's own output distribution, not the true next token) are required so that the Fisher matrix equals the Gauss-Newton Hessian; the loss is summed over tokens, not meaned, for the same reason. **(2) The training gradients.** They either TF-IDF filter (a BM25 variant, top 10,000 sequences per query) or — their preferred route — search unfiltered data and share the gradient cost across many queries ("query batching") by storing each preconditioned query gradient at rank 32, which they verify is nearly lossless. Because the Hessian is block-diagonal there is a clean layerwise and tokenwise decomposition of every influence score, so each number can be split into per-layer, per-token contributions. Validation is against the proximal Bregman response function, optimised with Adam on small datasets where it can be solved accurately, plus the 810M model. Finally they run synthetic-sequence experiments: they write training sequences that never existed and measure their influence, which turns a correlational ranking into a controlled intervention on the *data*.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **ranked list of training sequences with scalar influence scores**, plus a layerwise/tokenwise heat map over each sequence, and — as the explanatory payload — human-readable narratives about which sequences rise to the top for which kind of query ("this role-play trace is supported by science-fiction passages about self-preservation"). It is explicitly partial and non-mechanistic: it says which data a behaviour leans on and roughly where in depth that lean happens, not through what computation. The authors are careful that influence is not even a faithful approximation of retraining (see Limits); what it approximates is a local, damped response function. The strongest predict-then-test loop in the paper is the synthetic-sequence intervention, where a predicted near-zero is confirmed by construction.

## How they judged success

- **Agreement with a ground-truth surrogate**: Pearson correlation between the method's influence scores and the PBRF, over 500 random training points, averaged over 10 seeds with different test points (Concrete and Energy regression, MNIST, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10 with two-hidden-layer MLPs and a ResNet-20; language model with completion log-likelihood as the measurement).
- **Baselines**: LiSSA (the standard iterative IHVP) and the plain gradient dot product (i.e. replacing the Gauss-Newton Hessian with the identity). Wall-clock time to reach a given correlation, with EK-FAC factor-fitting cost included.
- **Tail fit**: maximum-likelihood fits of several distributions to the top 0.01% of influences over 5 million unfiltered samples, with a Kolmogorov–Smirnov goodness-of-fit test.
- **Coverage**: fraction of total positive influence held by the top k percent of sequences.
- **Recall against a filtered candidate set**: how many unfiltered sequences must be scanned to find as many highly influential ones as TF-IDF delivers.
- **Memorisation positive control**: six famous-quote queries, checking that the scan returns the exact passage.
- **Human check**: a Surge AI crowdworker study — annotators shown only query/sequence pairs, asked to summarise and judge relevance.

## What they found

- **EK-FAC matches LiSSA on PBRF correlation across every small-scale task and the 810M language model, and beats the gradient dot product**, which implies the curvature term is doing real work; because LiSSA must re-solve the IHVP per measurement while EK-FAC only multiplies matrices, the wall-clock gap is orders of magnitude. Rank-32 query-gradient compression is essentially lossless (shutdown correlation 0.995).
- **Influence is heavy-tailed but diffuse.** The tail fits a power law for most queries (exponents around 2–4), and for three queries the fitted exponent is low enough that even the variance diverges. But the absolute scale is tiny: scores above 0.1 are rare and no sequence in the eight plotted queries exceeds 1, i.e. no single sequence would raise the completion's probability by a factor of e — far less than the completion's information content. On the 22B model the top 1% of sequences carry 12–52% of total positive influence. Reading: outputs are assembled from very many examples, not recited from a few.
- **About 5 million unfiltered sequences are enough** to recover as many highly influential sequences as the TF-IDF filtered set, so they scan at least 10 million for the rest of the work.
- **Abstraction grows with scale.** For an 810M model the top sequences share short token strings with the query and are otherwise unrelated (for `paperclips`, a page of the word "paper" repeated). For 52B the top sequences for `shutdown` are science-fiction passages about a machine or person facing termination, for `trade` a passage on designing an AGI agent's objective function, for `paperclips` articles about catastrophic AI risk.
- **Layers specialise**: averaged over 50 queries × top 500 sequences (25,000 scores) influence is spread roughly uniformly across lower/middle/upper thirds, but per query it localises — memorised quotes and simple factual completions concentrate in the upper layers, maths, code and translation in the middle. Restricting the scan to the middle third is what yields the most abstract neighbours (for `inflation` the top middle-layer sequence never mentions the consumer price index but discusses consumer confidence, the trade deficit and personal income). A single-layer (final-layer) influence estimate, the standard efficiency shortcut, is therefore not enough.
- **Memorisation is detectable when it exists**: all six famous-passage queries return the exact passage at the top under an unfiltered scan, so the pipeline can find copying; the absence of such sequences for ordinary Assistant responses is evidence, not a blind spot.
- **The ordering result**: influence collapses when the two halves of the relation are swapped. Synthetic sequences with a fictional entity (`Zorald Pfaff` as first President of Astrobia; `Gleem` made of `hydrogenium` and `oxium`) keep high influence under arbitrary rewording and paraphrase as long as the prompt-side content comes first and the completion-side content second; reversed, influence drops sharply, and in the reversed case deleting the prompt-side half leaves the influence unchanged — meaning what remains is the bare string, not the relation. For `english_to_mandarin`, reversing English/Mandarin order cuts influence by at least an order of magnitude despite identical content. Their proposed mechanism is the layer split from the previous bullet: seen tokens are processed bottom-up, predicted tokens top-down, and with no weight sharing an update to one side does not reach the other.
- **Role-play looks like imitation, not planning**: the influential sequences are descriptions and fiction of similar behaviour, never near-identical sentences, i.e. generalisation at a level of abstraction above token copying; they say this is weak evidence against a sophisticated-agent-representation account, which they cannot rule out because influences from a genuine planner could be spread thinly across many examples.
- **Crowdworkers** judged a majority of the top sequences relevant to their query, which is offered as a sanity check on the qualitative reading.

## Limits and open problems they name

The big one, stated upfront: influence functions have been shown **not to match the counterfactual that motivated them** and are better read as approximating the proximal Bregman response function — a local quantity around the final weights. They therefore expect the method to miss genuinely nonlinear training phenomena such as circuit formation or representational reorganisation, and they validate against the PBRF but do **not** verify that the PBRF itself captures what they care about. Pretrained models only, not RLHF/fine-tuning (fine-tuning is harder because the outcome depends on the optimiser's implicit bias over a body of pretraining knowledge that influence functions do not model); MLP weights only, so influence routed through attention is invisible; 52B is far from frontier scale; only a fraction of the corpus was scanned, so even more influential sequences may exist. Tokenwise attribution is semantically shaky in both directions — the highlighted token is the one *before* the predicted token, per-token signs are not interpretable, and highly sparse tokenwise profiles usually mean the sequence is spurious (they use an L2/L1 ratio above 2/3 as a filter). The "clipping to positive influence" trick for the coverage statistic may overstate concentration; they know of no good correction. Approximate-nearest-neighbour indexing of gradients is suggested but judged hard because the IHVP whitens the parameter space.

## What we take from it

- **A framing to borrow wholesale (method)**: the counterfactual "which inputs, if added or removed, change this behaviour" is the same question as "predict this behaviour under intervention", and it gives a way to test a white-box model against a non-mechanistic baseline. Our probe-based predictor should be able to answer influence-style questions, and this is the published yardstick.
- **A validation skeleton (measure)**: correlate against a solvable surrogate (here PBRF via an optimiser on small models), against two baselines that isolate the ingredient being claimed (gradient dot product = no curvature; LiSSA = accurate but slow), average over seeds and measurements, and report wall-clock alongside accuracy. Apply the same shape to our predictors: correlate against an expensive ground truth *and* show what the cheap alternative loses.
- **Synthetic-input interventions as the cheapest true test (method)**: inventing data that was never in the training set and measuring its effect converts a narrative into a controlled experiment — the direct analogue of our intervention experiments, but on the data side instead of the activation side. Worth copying whenever we want to separate "the model learned the relation" from "the model learned the string".
- **Order-sensitivity as a discriminative signature (warning)**: a model can look like it has generalised a relation when it has only stored the co-occurrence of two phrases in one order. Any of our probes that claims a relational or compositional feature should be tested on order-swapped and half-deleted inputs, with the diagnostic that influence surviving the deletion of the contextual half means the pure-string account is not excluded.
- **Depth localisation is coarse but real (method)**: influence spreads uniformly on average yet specialises per query (upper layers for recall, middle for abstraction). That matches the probe literature's preference for mid-stack abstraction and is a cheap prior for where to plant probes; it also warns that any all-layer-pooled readout will blur exactly this distinction.
- **Concentration statistic (measure)**: "top 1% carries 12–52% of the total" and "no sequence above 1 nat" are two numbers that together say heavy-tailed-but-diffuse. That pair is a good template for describing how concentrated any attribution we compute is — including whether a probe's evidence is concentrated in a few examples or smeared.
- **A warning about the tool itself**: the guarantee is local and linearised. If we use gradient-based or curvature-based attribution anywhere, we inherit the same limitation and should not claim it explains nonlinear structure.
