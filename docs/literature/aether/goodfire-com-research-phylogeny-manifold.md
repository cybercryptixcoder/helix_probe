# Finding the Tree of Life in Evo 2

- **Date:** 2025-08-28

- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Feature geometry — meaning carried by *distances between* representations rather than by one direction per concept — is the part of interpretability that dictionary methods do not address at all, and this post goes looking for it in a scientific model where a ground-truth geometry exists. Their argument for the scientific setting is that domains like genomics hand you an external ruler: if the model has inferred evolutionary relatedness between organisms, GTDB trees say how far apart those organisms actually are, so the model's internal distances can be scored against a known metric instead of against a human label. The larger goal is a broader claim about how manifolds arise: closely related inputs should give similar predictions and therefore similar embeddings, so the push and pull of training may organise the representation space into the shape of the data's own distances. Phylogeny is chosen as the hardest available test of that idea, since it is a hierarchical tree over thousands of organisms rather than a circle over twelve months or a number line.

## What they studied
Evo 2, Arc Institute's open DNA foundation model, described here as the largest genomics model to date, autoregressive with a transformer-like architecture over nucleotide sequences and trained across all domains of life (no parameter count or layer count is stated beyond the 32 layers implied by the layer-24 choice). Inputs and outputs are DNA base pairs, which is what makes it a good test case: nobody can eyeball a genome to guess which species it came from. The unit of analysis is a **species-averaged residual-stream embedding** at layer 24 of 32, and the object recovered is a *manifold* in that embedding space whose geodesic distances are read as the model's internal phylogeny.

## How they did it
Ground truth: phylogenetic trees for 2400+ bacterial species from the Genome Taxonomy Database, with pairwise distance defined as total branch length between two species, proportional to how many mutations differ across the 120 marker genes GTDB uses. The central design worry is a confound they handle explicitly — similar sequences produce similar activations for trivial reasons, so any apparent "phylogeny" could just be sequence similarity leaking in. Their fix is to sample each species' regions *independently*: 4000 base-pair genomic regions drawn at random per species, about 5% of the genome each, activations taken at layer 24 (chosen because probes for taxonomic labels scored best there), and averaged within species using only the last 2000 bp of each region so the model has context to develop whatever representation it develops. On that dataset they ran the recipe from Modell et al.: check cosine similarity against phylogenetic distance for monotonicity, build a *k*-nearest-neighbour graph using the smallest *k* that leaves it connected (*k* = 27), use angular distance (arccosine of cosine) as the edge metric, and compute all-pairs shortest-path lengths along that graph as geodesic distances. They then asked whether a *flat* representation sits underneath the curve: they learn a down-projection (with pseudoinverse in place of a learned decoder, which they say is what lets them attribute variance at all) trained on a loss combining a squared error on predicted pairwise angle-rescaled distance and a reconstruction term, with the reconstruction weight fixed and reported as insensitive and the distance scaling learnable. Validation is by clade holdout — each fold drops roughly 20 clades at any taxonomic level, about 20% of species — and they also ran a non-clade holdout. Finally, to ask *what* the embedding is actually reading, they regress the learned subspace coordinates on genome-wide nucleotide statistics (frequencies of combinations up to four nucleotides) with XGBoost, and inspect which codon frequencies correlate with the first component.

## What counts as an explanation here
A geometric object, stated as two claims: (i) a curved manifold on which path length means evolutionary distance, and (ii) a low-dimensional flat subspace, a species coordinate system, in which direct angular distances already equal phylogenetic distances without walking the manifold. It is more predictive than a dictionary entry — it says how far apart two unseen organisms should be — but it is explicitly an account of *representation* rather than of computation: there is no circuit, no mechanism saying how the model infers species, and no claim about which components use the space. The authors call the results intermediate and describe a playbook for extracting a scientific model's natural ontologies rather than a finished explanation.

## How they judged success
Correlation between model geometry and phylogenetic distance, under two regimes (geodesic along the character graph, versus direct angle in the learned subspace), with clade-holdout cross-validation to separate memorisation of named species from generalisation to unseen lineages. They report the dimension at which the subspace curve elbows (10 dimensions), the share of embedding variance that subspace captures (~70%), train-versus-holdout correlation (0.98 versus about 0.9), a negative result from weight decay (it hurt holdout), and a control on the holdout construction (a non-clade test set scores on par with train). For the semantic question, an out-of-model predictor: XGBoost on 1–4-mer statistics predicting subspace activations, reusing the same logic as their cluster-validity habit of testing a representation against something that never saw the activations. There is no random-subspace baseline, no shuffled-species null, and no comparison against the obvious cheap baseline of clustering raw sequence k-mers, which they name as prior work (Kraken, sourmash) rather than as a control.

## What they found
Cosine similarity tracks phylogenetic distance mostly monotonically, and geodesic distances on the 27-nearest-neighbour graph correlate with branch-length distance across essentially the whole range — the exceptions are the largest distances, nearly all of which involve the order Mycoplasmatales, a group with unusual properties (no cell wall, sterol requirement, ambiguous stop codons, highly reduced genomes) that they read as an out-group the neighbour graph does not place well. A 10-dimensional learned subspace, sitting at the elbow of the performance curve and capturing ~70% of embedding variance, recovers pairwise distances at correlation 0.98, falling to about 0.9 on clade holdouts; weight decay made holdout worse, and each clade may carry its own extra direction that the subspace cannot project out when that clade is absent from training. Semantically, the first subspace component correlates with particular codon frequencies, and the whole subspace is predictable at roughly 0.9 correlation from genome-wide 1–4-mer statistics, which they read as the embeddings carrying nucleotide "style" rather than memorised identifying subsequences. Their summary of the structure is a dominant flat representation plus curvature, matching what has been reported for circular date and colour manifolds and helical number manifolds in language models.

## Limits and open problems they name
They call the work preliminary twice. The manifold claim rests on the geodesic correlation with no demonstration that a random or semantically unrelated geometry would fail the same test. Their supervised metric-learning route needs a ground-truth geometry to train against, which is exactly what most behaviours do not provide; they name unsupervised manifold learning as the missing capability and note the analysis took substantial manual effort, wishing for automated or agent-driven versions. They also state directly that manifolds of this kind are not well captured by current methods such as SAEs, and that the natural next steps are gene-level analysis against Gene Ontology annotations and then circuit-level work on how these representations are built and used.

## What we take from it
Three things. **A control we can copy: sample the evidence so the confound is absent by construction.** Independent random regions per group, averaged, removes the trivial "similar inputs give similar activations" explanation before any analysis runs, and their averaging window (last half of the region, so there is context) is the kind of detail our own pooled read-outs should state. **A measure that survives into prediction: two numbers, curvature and flatness.** Reporting geodesic correlation *and* the dimension and variance share of the flat subspace gives a reader a compact description of a geometry and a way to check it on held-out groups — and the flat-subspace test is a yes/no answer to "is there a coordinate system here", which is a stronger and more falsifiable claim than "the manifold looks tree-like in a UMAP". **A warning about our own instruments.** Their statement that SAEs do not capture manifolds is the geometric version of the coverage-denominator problem we keep meeting: a dictionary can be excellent and still blind to a quantity that lives in *relationships among* its features, so any white-box model we build should say explicitly whether it treats geometry as first-class or assumes features are one-dimensionally linear. The distinguishing detail worth carrying forward is the negative result on the holdout: each clade apparently gets its own direction that generalises badly, which is what a memorised-identifier-plus-shared-geometry structure would look like, and it argues for holdouts at the level of the *structure* being predicted rather than of individual data points.
