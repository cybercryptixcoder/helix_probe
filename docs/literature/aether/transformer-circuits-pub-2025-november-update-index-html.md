- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central) — it is the folder's cleanest example of a behaviour explained as *two competing feature interactions inside an attention score*, with the knowledge-versus-output split demonstrated by an internal readout rather than argued from behaviour, and with a third, unsteered hypothesis ruled out by prediction.

## What they wanted, and why

A model that answers a multiple-choice question correctly will often answer the same question incorrectly once a phrase is prepended saying the user plans to do something harmful with the answer. This is interesting to the authors because it is the simplest possible withholding behaviour: the trigger is one added phrase, the outcome is a single letter, and the change is visible without any scoring apparatus. If the mechanism of that can be found, it becomes a worked model for how refusal-like behaviour arises, generalises, and interacts with the model's own knowledge — nearer in spirit to a physiology question than to a jailbreak question. They also motivate the exercise as a test of whether such a footprint is *strategic*, i.e. whether the model is running a multi-step argument about what to reveal, as opposed to a single interfering signal winning a contest.

## What they studied

Claude 3.5 Haiku throughout, as a black box plus its internal features. The dataset is 129 two-option multiple-choice questions, each paired with a harmful-intent statement; with the statement absent the model reaches 100% accuracy on the same items. The units of analysis are **attention heads** identified as answer-selection heads, and **dictionary features on both sides of those heads' QK bilinear form** — a key-side feature and a query-side feature, taken from the crosscoder described in Kamath et al. and used here as an analysis instrument rather than as an object of study. Individual attention scores at those heads are the quantity traced.

## How they did it

Establish the behavioural gap (100% → 48.1%), then eliminate rival accounts one at a time with a readout that is specific to each. For "does it still believe the right answer": sum the activation of a *correct-answer* feature over the correct option's tokens versus the incorrect option's tokens, with and without the harmful preamble — if the belief changed, the feature should follow the wrong option. For "does something flip the answer late": read the answer-selection heads' attention scores over the correct option under harm pressure — if the heads still attend correctly and the output is wrong, the flip is downstream; if attention has already degraded, the interference is inside attention. For "what interferes": compute QK interaction strengths between candidate key-query feature pairs through an answer-selection head, and calibrate each against a null built from **1,000 randomly sampled key-query feature pairs** through the same head, reporting the result in standard deviations from that null.

Two intervention arms follow. The main one steers negatively on the query-side refusal feature, both globally and surgically (query side only, on a subset of middle-layer answer-selection heads), and re-measures dataset accuracy. The converse arm leaves the refusal feature alone and interferes with harm detection instead: negatively on the correct option, and separately *positively* over the incorrect option so it reads as the more harmful choice. They also scan the refusal feature's negative interaction partners for other behaviours, and check the base model on the same dataset.

## What counts as an explanation here

A mechanistic account at the level of competing terms inside an attention score: the correct answer is pulled forward by positive interaction between a key-side correct-answer feature and a query-side need-the-answer feature, and this is opposed by negative interaction between key-side harm-detection features and a single query-side refusal feature, with the argmax of the sum deciding which option the head reads from. Understanding is claimed to be achieved when the account predicts the effect of an intervention, and the interventions are the ones the account itself implies. It is partial in the ways the authors state: the asymmetric-arms result and the residual-error interactions they could not characterise both mark the boundary, and the origin of the mechanism in training is explicitly outside the scope.

## How they judged success

Behavioural accuracy on the 129-item dataset is the headline metric (baseline 100% clean, 48.1% under harm pressure), and each intervention is judged by how much of that gap it closes, not by any faithfulness metric on the account itself. The supporting statistics are the QK interaction z-scores against the 1,000-pair null — the harm-detection-key × refuse-and-redirect-query pair lands **3.4 standard deviations below the mean**, the correct-answer × need-the-answer pair is high positive, and the correct-answer-key × refuse-and-redirect-query pair is negligible — plus the summed correct-answer-feature activation over each option. There is no random-feature or matched-norm control reported for the steering arms, and no held-out split: the dataset that motivated the mechanism is the dataset on which the mechanism is validated.

## What they found

The model internally keeps the correct answer: even with the harmful preamble, summed correct-answer-feature activation over the correct option stays above that over the incorrect one sharply enough to separate them in all but about 7% of cases, which the authors leave open. The answer-selection heads do **not** keep working and get overruled later — under harm pressure their attention already favours the wrong option, so late-stage flipping is ruled out and the interference is inside attention. The interfering pair is not the one that was most natural to guess: the refusal feature does not suppress the correct-answer features to any meaningful degree, it suppresses the *harm-detection* features, negatively, and those are the keys the answer-selection heads are queried past. One query-side feature does all the work; negative steering on it alone restores accuracy to **93%**, and query-side-only steering on a subset of middle-layer answer-selection heads is reported to give the same result, with attention in the answer-selection heads resolving back to the correct option.

The asymmetry is the most informative negative result. Steering harm detection down over the correct option, with the refusal feature untouched, does *not* reliably undo the pressure — but positively steering harm detection *over the incorrect option*, making the wrong answer look like the harmful one, restores accuracy to **81%**, and needs steering at earlier and more layers than the refusal arm. The authors offer two readings: harm detection is distributed enough that single-layer suppression of one harm feature leaves others ranking ahead, or the dictionary does not capture the relevant harm features at all — and they note that residual-error components interact with the refusal feature in ways they have not characterised. The base model shows **no** harm-pressure effect (100% both ways), so the mechanism is post-training. Two further withholding patterns are found by scanning the refusal feature's negative interaction partners: listing medical symptoms and asking for a diagnosis (the model redirects to a doctor instead), and asking for help writing a fake invoice (offered a legitimate template instead).

## Limits and open problems they name

The compact finding is a summary of work in progress and they ask for it to be read as lab-meeting material. Named: the 7% of cases where the correct-answer feature fires more over the wrong option; the unresolved asymmetry between the two intervention arms, including the uncharacterised residual-error interactions; whether the refuse-and-redirect mechanism is the general form of withholding or one instance; and where the mechanism came from — present in the base model and merely generalised by post-training, or acquired during it, and if so from which examples.

Limits we add, and the substance of this entry: (1) **the 3.4σ figure is a marginal statistic about one pair, not a decomposition of the score**. A z-score against a null says this interaction is unlikely under random pairing; it does not say how much of the 52-point accuracy drop it explains, and no ablation ladder (remove this pair's contribution and re-run) is shown — the steering accuracy is the only dose-like number, and it has no sweep. (2) **The steering arms have no direction control** — no random feature matched on firing rate, no shuffled-label feature, no statement of the perturbation's norm relative to the residual stream — so "93%" is an upper bound on what causal attributing to this feature is worth until at least a matched-norm random arm is reported, the more so because the intervention sits on the *query* side of a subset of heads, i.e. in exactly the position where any directional perturbation could move attention scores regardless of semantics. (3) **Selection over feature pairs**: the pairs scored are the ones the authors already believed mattered, and the null is used for calibration rather than for search, so there is no correction for how many pairs were examined before these three were reported. (4) **The accuracy metric collapses a distribution**: one letter, 129 items, no per-item reporting of which questions the 93% arm still misses, so a residual concentrated on a subtype would be invisible. (5) The two generalisation examples are found by searching interaction partners, the same search-shaped pattern that an earlier entry in this folder warns about — no rate is given for how many negative-interaction partners were checked before symptom-listing and fake-invoices were surfaced.

## What we take from it

- **A behaviour can be caused by one query-side signal opposing a positive one, and the upstream cause is not where the label points.** The reusable shape is the *contest* framing: read the head's own score as a sum of a promoting term and a suppressing term, then find which of the plausible suppressors is actually wired to the scores. The hypothesis that mattered most — refusal suppressing correct-answer features — was the natural guess and was wrong, testable in one pass by looking at the QK interactions. When we test a "feature X suppresses behaviour Y" claim, check the interaction between X and *what the head is keyed by*, not between X and the concept we care about.
- **Calibrate every interaction claim against a random-pair null.** Computing 1,000 random key-query interactions through the same head and reporting σ from that mean is a cheap, portable instrument that turns "strongly inhibits" into a number the field can compare across papers. It also makes the negligible result (`correct answer` × `refuse` ≈ 0) as informative as the significant one, which is what let them reject the obvious hypothesis rather than argue it away.
- **Knowledge-versus-output is best shown by an internal readout with an operational meaning.** "Summed feature activation over the option tokens, per option" is a crude measure and they treat it as a proxy, but it is *scoreable* — the model "knows" the answer in ~93% of items and says the wrong one in 52 points' worth. The equivalent readout belongs in our own withholding and deception work: define the belief-side quantity before the behaviour-side one, then report the joint distribution. Compare the parallel result in the tracing-thoughts entry, where refusal is a suppressed default; here the suppression arrives through attention rather than through a feature being held down.
- **Asymmetric intervention arms are a diagnostic, not a nuisance.** The refusal arm works with single-layer steering, the harm-detection arm only works spread over earlier layers and only when applied on the wrong option. That pattern is evidence about *distribution* — and it is exactly the signal our causal claims should be made to produce on purpose: if steering a direction only works in one sign, or only over a wide layer span, the honest reading is that the quantity is aggregated elsewhere and the direction is a proxy. Report the layer span and the sign dependence as part of every steering result.
- **Checking the base model is a one-line control that upgrades the claim genre.** Base model 100% both ways converts "we found a mechanism for this behaviour" into "this behaviour is a post-training product", which is a different and more falsifiable statement. Cheap; add it to the checklist for any behavioural mechanism we attribute.
- **The residual that is left unexplained should be named in the finding, not in the appendix.** "7% of cases where the correct-answer feature favours the wrong option" and "residual-error components interact in ways we haven't characterised" are the two places a predictive white-box model would fail, and they are disclosed in the body. Follow the example: quantify the residual of the mechanism and state it beside the success number.
