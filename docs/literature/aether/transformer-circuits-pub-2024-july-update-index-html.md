## What they wanted, and why

Olah opens by declaring the grand challenge of the previous year — superposition — substantially unblocked, and asking what the remaining obstacles are between dictionary learning and an actual mechanistic understanding of a network. His stated path is: find the interpretable features that act as the variables of the computation, understand the circuits that compute them, then lift that microscopic picture into a macroscopic one. The five hurdles he names along that path are the programmatic content of the post: features we cannot yet extract, cross-layer superposition, attention superposition, interference weights, and the final zoom-out problem. The two shorter Olah pieces are definitional housekeeping forced by outside work — a multidimensional-feature result showed that the team's "linear representation" phrase had been quietly conflating two different claims — and a scaling argument about how many features may remain permanently invisible. The two empirical notes are methodological: one is a reproducibility case study for pivot tables, the other attacks the half of feature evaluation that dictionary learning has never measured, namely sensitivity (does the feature fire when the concept is present) as opposed to specificity (is the concept present when it fires).

## What they studied

At least four different objects, none of them a single model. The essays are about the field and about Claude-sized networks abstractly; the dark-matter argument is anchored on the 13-million-feature Sonnet SAE of *Scaling Monosemanticity* and on the model's knowledge of San Francisco geography. The pivot-table note studies small attention-only and layered transformers of 1–4 layers across several architectures (with and without layer norm, different positional embeddings), analysing a single attention head's QK and OV circuits as full vocabulary-by-vocabulary matrices. The sensitivity note studies the four highlighted features from the *Scaling Monosemanticity* feature tour, in Claude 3 Sonnet, against a corpus of 160,000 snippets of 128 tokens each, with Claude 3.5 Sonnet acting as the concept-relevance rater. Unit of analysis: directions in residual-stream activation space (features), heads and their circuit matrices, and — for the essays — the whole model.

## How they did it

The three essays are argument, not experiment, and should be read as such. The pivot-table note is a replication guide: reconstruct C_QK and C_OV for one head as embedding-matrix products through the head's weights and both layer norms, subtracting the pre-unembed layer-norm bias so a global logit bias does not masquerade as an effect, then rank key tokens by a heuristic that multiplies the largest attention score they receive, the largest output-logit effect they produce, and their training-set token frequency — with the query and output sides scored similarly — and display the top entries as a table. They report which training variables moved the result: poorly-trained runs, corpora that are hard to read, and deeper models all degrade it, and single-layer models are clearest. The sensitivity note replaces the computational proxies used in *Towards Monosemanticity* (DNA strings, base64) — which only work for concepts with a formal generator — with an LLM judge: prompt Claude to reason about a concept's relevance to a snippet and then emit a 0–10 relevance score, validate the judge against snippets where the feature already fires (using rank correlation across judge versions plus manual spot checks), filter a broad corpus down to snippets rated highly relevant, then count how often each of the dictionary's features is active anywhere in a filtered snippet at all. Because false negatives cost more than false positives here, they set the activation criterion at nonzero rather than thresholded, take the maximum over each snippet's tokens, and give each snippet a wider 2048-token context during inference. They ran this separately for each of the four tour concepts and then, for snippets the highlighted feature missed, inspected which other features did fire and how the fifty most-responding features partition the high-relevance snippets.

## What counts as an explanation here

Three distinct forms, and the interesting thing is that they are explicitly ranked. What counts as an explanation for the essays is a *research agenda*: a named obstacle that is not itself a result, offered so that it can be attacked in parallel — and Olah is careful to note that the hurdles no longer block one another the way basic superposition did. The pivot-table note's explanation is a *circuit*: a head's behaviour expressed as two matrices over the vocabulary, legible as "this key token, when present earlier in the context, promotes these output tokens". The sensitivity note's explanation is a *measurement on a dictionary*: a per-feature sensitivity/specificity pair, which together are offered as the honest way to say what a feature means. The essays also redefine the word "linear" itself: a linear representation now means composition-by-addition plus intensity-as-scaling, deliberately decoupled from one-dimensionality, and the authors say plainly that multidimensional features are compatible with all their prior work while a failure of mathematical linearity would send the field back to the drawing board. That is a *framing* claim, not a model, and nothing in the post predicts behaviour under intervention.

## How they judged success

For the essays: internal coherence and plausibility of the agenda, plus the sketching of a path — no metrics, and none claimed. For the pivot tables: the fraction of top-ranked table entries that a reader judges to be genuine skip-trigrams, with an explicit floor — the team says to distrust tables showing under about 10% interpretable entries as possibly "interpreting mirages", and reports 25–75% on their better runs, alongside a large mass of plain copying entries. Model quality is thus inferred from a legibility statistic, which is a deliberate inversion: interpretability used as a probe of the base model rather than the reverse. For feature sensitivity: agreement between Claude judge versions (rank correlation, share of ratings differing by more than two points) and against human spot checks; then absolute sensitivity on the filtered set (does the feature fire at all), reported with the highlighted feature's *rank* among all dictionary features by sensitivity rather than a single number, and paired with the *specificity* measure from *Scaling Monosemanticity* so the two axes can be read together. The Golden Gate Bridge feature had too few filtered snippets to assess and was dropped, which is itself reported.

## What they found

The pivot-table reconstruction is unexpectedly brittle — small training details break it — and the fix is documented rather than theorised. Interpretable skip-trigrams appear across architectures, including with different positional embeddings and with or without layer norm, and later layers of multilayer models also show structure, but single layers are clearest. The sensitivity result is the sharp empirical content of the month: the four features highlighted in *Scaling Monosemanticity* sit within roughly the top 0.1–10% of the dictionary by sensitivity, yet are far from the most sensitive, and **the highlighted features mostly do not fire at all on snippets where the concept is clearly present** — the post shows worked snippets for brain sciences (axon regeneration, myelin inhibitors, rated 10 by the judge), transit infrastructure (ring roads, tunnel boring versus drilling-and-blasting, rated 9) and popular tourist attractions (Angkor Wat, rated 9) on which the associated feature never activates. The most sensitive feature for brain sciences appears to track human-subjects experimental methods rather than brains; the most sensitive transit feature was uninterpretable and labelled unknown; the tourist-attraction winner responds to histories of attractions and covers every snippet rated 9, but loses that coverage as the rating threshold is lowered. Complementary features often do fire where the highlighted ones miss — e.g. a feature favouring proximity to the token "brain" — and the fifty most-responding features show vertical banding across high-relevance snippets, which the authors read as distinct subtypes of the concept being represented differently, with finer dictionaries giving finer splits. The conclusion they draw is that one feature's sensitivity is not evidence about how well a dictionary captures a concept, and that a faithful dictionary should be able to account for every clear instance through cooperation among features; since they cannot, they raise the possibility that this is dark matter or another fundamental gap. On multidimensional features: composition-as-addition forces a manifold to be orthogonal or nearly so to other features, intensity-as-scaling forces it to contract to a bias point along rays with semantic change as angular movement, and the "rays" are one-dimensional features locked to a point on the manifold — so a probe on a predefined property still reads a direction, but the rays are not privileged and a multidimensional feature can be seen as an uncountably large family of one-dimensional ones. They argue continuity (midnight on Tuesday sitting between Tuesday and Wednesday) is the distinguishing evidence, and that a manifold may be embedded in a deliberately more complex way than its topology needs purely to achieve the right distance metric — in which case the residual principal components that look like noise are load-bearing. The dark-matter argument: Sonnet's 13M-feature dictionary resolves some San Francisco neighbourhoods but the model knows individual coffee shops, street intersections and parks, at least two orders of magnitude more objects and correspondingly rarer in the corpus; the same holds for named people and for memorisation-type features, so barring an order-of-magnitude-efficiency breakthrough in dictionary learning the bulk of features may be unobservable, and the recommended agenda is to study them indirectly.

## Limits and open problems they name

All five hurdles are unsolved by construction, and the post adds a longer list: whether dictionary learning can be made orders of magnitude more efficient (explicitly called uncertain, and the answer determines whether the rarest features are *ever* resolvable); how to do dictionary learning across layers at all, since their basic approach offers no handle on cross-layer superposition; whether attentional features exist as objects comparable to induction heads, including the suspicion that familiar heads are themselves split across heads or obscured; whether interference weights — virtual weights that exist only as a compromise between circuits, and that should be irrelevant on distribution while possibly explaining adversarial examples — can be disentangled; and how to produce a macroscopic picture at all. On the definitional side Olah says outright that he does not expect his definitions to survive, and offers a third possible criterion (almost-linear readability, up to noise) as something he is not confident belongs in the definition. The sensitivity method's own limits: the corpus must be a representative distribution of the concept for the firing rate to be a sensitivity estimate; the judge's blind spots are unpredictable, which is exactly why they prefer recognition to generation; an all-or-nothing rater (Claude 3 Haiku) distorts the scale; a rare concept yields too few snippets to assess; and the interaction between complementary features — which features should be grouped as jointly representing a concept — is left as future work along with cosine-similarity neighbours of the highlighted features. For pivot tables the limits are that the sample is small models of 1–4 layers and that the proportions may not transfer, that the whole procedure is uninformative exactly when the model has learned structure too subtle to read, and that the heuristic ranking is a heuristic.

## What we take from it

This is the best available statement of the *shape* of our problem, and two of its parts change how we should report results.

- **A measure, and a warning, from the sensitivity result.** Sensitivity/specificity as a pair, with sensitivity estimated by LLM-judged filtering of a representative corpus rather than by proxies or generated adversarial examples. The warning is directly about our claims: a direction that fires on a concept's most obvious instances is not thereby *the* representation of that concept — here the field's own showcase features failed to fire on clearly on-topic text. Concretely, every "we recovered direction d for concept c" statement we make needs a firing rate on a filtered positive set and a rank among the dictionary's other candidates, not a handful of top-activating examples. The complementary-feature finding is the constructive half: coverage of a concept is distributed across a cooperating family, so our read-out should be a *set* of directions plus a rule for combining them, and the failure to cover all clear instances is the correct thing to report as incompleteness rather than hide.
- **A checklist of what "explanation" has to survive, for free.** Olah's five hurdles double as the objections a reviewer will raise at us: rare features we have not resolved, features that are not layer-localised, units of computation that are combinations of heads rather than single heads, virtual weights that are partly circuit and partly compromise, and the absence of any macroscopic story. Our white-box model should state, per claim, which of these it is vulnerable to.
- **A precise definition to test rather than assume.** Composition-as-addition and intensity-as-scaling are falsifiable properties of an activation space, and the two named counterexamples from earlier monthlies (magnitude-as-symbol, ring-shaped weekday features read by angle) are exactly failures of the second. Any linear read-out we build should be validated against both properties separately — a direction can satisfy addition and fail scaling.
- **A prediction-under-intervention caution on interference weights.** If a virtual weight between feature i and feature j exists partly as a compromise for other circuits, then multiplying out circuit weights will produce nonsense edges that are nevertheless real parameters of the network. Any circuit we extract must therefore be tested behaviourally, and a spurious edge should be reported as interference rather than as a bug.
- **A measurement protocol worth stealing wholesale.** The pivot-table score — interpretable-entry fraction with a distrust floor, plus the observation that poor training and unreadable corpora make tables look like mirages — is a cheap legibility statistic that could be applied to any structure we claim to have found, and its inversion (legibility of the readable part as a proxy for how well-trained and how legible the model is) is an evaluation trick we could reuse.
