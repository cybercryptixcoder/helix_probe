## What they wanted, and why
Generative models for materials are supposed to do *inverse design*: name the property you need, and the model proposes chemistries and structures that have it. In practice the conditioning is weak — asking a fine-tuned diffusion model for a band gap in a chosen window yields a small fraction of samples actually in range, and a much smaller fraction once you also require the candidate to be stable, unique and new. The existing dial, the conditioning weight γ, trades targeting against diversity, so turning it up collapses the output onto a few known materials. Their ambition is to get better targeting *without* paying that diversity cost, by giving the sampler a feedback signal it does not currently have. The larger frame is their company-wide claim that a model's internals encode more usable information than its outputs, and this post is the generative-modelling instance of it.

## What they studied
MatterGen, a diffusion model over crystal structures that also chooses the constituent elements, used as a band-gap-conditional generator with a target window of roughly 4–6 eV. The unit of analysis is a *single denoising step* of the diffusion trajectory: at each step the model's internal activations are read by a trained probe whose scalar output is an estimate of the property value the finished material would have. The object being steered is therefore not a direction in activation space but the sampler's accept/reject decision, and the things being compared are two samplers — the fine-tuned conditional model alone versus the same checkpoint with probe-guided correction — over a γ grid.

## How they did it
They start from a MatterGen checkpoint fine-tuned for conditioned generation, so the baseline already has the conditional input pathway and the γ dial available. A probe is trained on model activations to predict the final material's property, so that at sample time a *partial* denoising trajectory can be scored before the structure exists. Sampling is then run as a Metropolis-style accept/reject walk over proposals — a denoising step is taken as a proposal, accepted when the predicted property improves, and accepted with probability decreasing in how much worse it is otherwise (they name the algorithm MALA, i.e. Langevin ascent with a Metropolis correction). Evaluation filters every generated candidate through the standard SUN screen: stability computed by a learned approximation to density-functional energies, uniqueness within the batch, and novelty as absence from the training data — and then scores targeting as the share of candidates falling inside the requested band-gap window. The headline comparison holds sampling parameters fixed and sweeps the conditioning strength across an evenly spaced grid between 1.0 and 3.0, so the claim is about a curve rather than one operating point. The prior work they position against used an *exact* search metric in the same feedback slot; their substitution is an approximate metric read out of internals.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description of the model and not a dictionary. The probe's predicted property is an explanation only in the operational sense: it is a scalar that is used, inside the sampler, to make a decision that changes the outcome. This is the folder's clearest case of the predictive read-out outrunning the descriptive one — nothing here says what a "band gap" looks like as a feature or which internal structure carries it, only that a read-out of internals can rank partial trajectories well enough to act on. Coverage is explicitly partial: the probe stands in for a property calculator, not for the generative process.

## How they judged success
Three-way SUN viability (stable / unique / novel) plus in-window targeting, combined as the share of samples passing everything, with the baseline stated for the fine-tuned model alone. The comparison is done as a Pareto argument over the γ sweep — probe-guided sampling claimed to dominate plain guidance at every conditioning strength tested — and sampling parameters are held fixed so the two arms are matched on everything except the correction step. Cost is asserted to be minimal, not measured in the post. There is no random-probe control, no matched-cost control that spends the same compute on more samples or a longer γ sweep, and no reported uncertainty on the percentage improvements.

## What they found
Probe-guided sampling raises targeting while leaving the SUN screen roughly intact, for a combined gain in viable in-window candidates of about a quarter to a third depending on which sentence you read (~27% in the results paragraph, ~30% in the introduction and in the SUN-materials sentence) — the two figures are not reconciled in the text. They also report that the fine-tuned baseline is weak in absolute terms: on the order of 15% of samples land inside the target band gap and about 6.5% once the SUN filter is applied (prose, not read off the chart), and that fewer than one in ten fine-tuned candidates survive all the filtering stages. Against the γ grid, the probe-guided sampler is presented as dominant at every conditioning strength, which is the claim that matters: the gain is not a re-pointing along the same trade-off curve but a shift of the curve.

## Limits and open problems they name
They present the method as general to diffusion models and flag protein design, small molecules and de novo antibodies as the obvious extensions without testing any of them. They do not discuss the failure mode that matters most for a probe-based search — what happens when the probe is systematically wrong about the region of trajectory the sampler is exploring, since the accept/reject rule then steers the model toward whatever the probe *likes* rather than toward what is actually wanted — and they report no probe accuracy, no probe-vs-true-property correlation, and no held-out evaluation of the probe on partial trajectories. My additions, marked as mine: the improvement is reported without error bars over sampling seeds; the SUN numbers depend on learned approximations (a learned energy proxy rather than converged DFT, novelty as training-set membership), so the "good" side of the frontier is itself instrumented; there is no cheap alternative baseline in the same slot (e.g. rejection sampling on the same probe without the Metropolis term, or spending the equivalent compute on more baseline samples), so "self-correcting" is not separated from "any property-scored rejection at test time"; and the two inconsistent percentages (~27% vs ~30%) suggest the summary numbers were written at different points in the analysis.

## What we take from it
Two reusable ideas, and one warning. **The reusable idea is that a read-out earns its keep by entering a decision loop, not by being interpretable**: the probe here is an unexplained scalar, and its value comes from being plugged into a sampler as the acceptance criterion — which is the same shape as the value-transplant and closed-loop results elsewhere in this folder, arrived at in a completely different domain. For us that is further evidence that the deliverable is "a state reading that changes what the system does", and that a read-out can be worth its weight while remaining completely undescribed. **The reusable measure is the curve, not the point**: because they sweep the conditioning strength, the claim becomes dominance over a trade-off frontier rather than a single win at one setting, and that is cheap for us to copy whenever our intervention competes with an existing dial. **The warning is the missing probe audit**: an accept/reject rule built on a predictor is a control system, and a control system with an unvalidated sensor is a known failure mode — before we ever steer by a read-out we should publish its accuracy *on the distribution it will be queried on* (here: partial trajectories, not finished materials), and report what the loop does when the sensor is deliberately degraded. Finally, the domain matters less than it looks: it is a diffusion model over crystal lattices, so nothing here transfers to autoregressive language models directly, but the test-time-selection framing does, and that framing is where our own predictor could plausibly act before it is good enough to describe anything.
