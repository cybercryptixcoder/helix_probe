## What they wanted, and why

A grab-bag update from the team after *Towards Monosemanticity*: eight short notes, a set of open problems offered to outsiders, and a digest of what related work by other groups showed. The organising interest is what to do next now that sparse-autoencoder features exist — specifically whether the approach survives contact with attention layers, with multi-layer models, with models at billions of parameters, and with the awkward question of whether the features are real or just a convenient account of the data. Two of the notes are warnings rather than results: a pair of counterexamples showing that forcing a model to be sparse can produce representational forms *worse* than superposition. The stated plan is to work on dictionary-learning scaling, on attention superposition, and on circuits built from the features.

## What they studied

Several separate targets. **Attention superposition:** a toy where five sparse "attentional features" — each a sparse attention pattern paired with a sparse OV circuit — are compressed into two attention heads by learning a single mixing matrix used for both the patterns and the OV weights, with random fixed OV weights, random residual-stream contents and ReLU after the mix, trained by MSE. **MNIST:** fully connected ReLU classifiers, 16–64 units per layer, 1–3 layers, dictionary learning over the activations with a hyperparameter scan across several algorithms. **An 8-layer transformer:** MLP activations, features searched for safety relevance, so explicitly not a random sample. **Large models:** billions of parameters, preliminary feature inspection only. **Counterexamples:** one-layer models regularised for sparse activations, including one variant where the MLP output *replaces* the residual stream instead of being added to it. **Predicting future activations:** variations where an SAE predicts a *later* activation instead of reconstructing its own input.

## How they did it

Attention superposition: generate the target as the ReLU of five sparse heads applied to the residual stream, then fit a two-head model whose mixing matrix is shared between attention patterns and OV matrices, and read the learned mixing matrix as a scatter plot to see how the five true features arrange themselves in the two-head basis — directly inspecting geometry the way the earlier toy superposition work did. MNIST: train dictionaries on the hidden activations, look for reconstruction "elbows" as a function of sparsity or dictionary size, and test whether feature directions, pushed through the output weights, up-weight the correct class logits. 8-layer model: train dictionaries on each layer's MLP activations and label features by inspection, with layer position recorded. Ghost Grads: an auxiliary loss term — take the dead units (defined as not having fired in 2e6 tokens), run a second forward pass using only those units but with an exponential activation in place of ReLU, scale their output to half the L2 norm of the reconstruction residual (scale treated as constant for the backward pass), compute MSE between residual and that output, rescale to match the ordinary reconstruction loss in magnitude (again constant for gradients), and add it to the loss. The design forces gradients that can only be reduced by making the dead units' pre-activations less negative, i.e. by reviving them. Counterexamples: hand-construct sparse-regularised one-layer models and inspect a single neuron's behaviour, holding the residual stream fixed to check whether the residual disambiguates it. Predicting future activations: three pairings — pre- to post-nonlinearity MLP, pre-MLP residual to MLP output, pre-attention residual to attention output (the last with a wide, sparsity-regularised attention block).

## What counts as an explanation here

Still a feature dictionary, but the interesting move is in the "predicting future activations" note: the authors consider replacing a *slice of computation* with a sparse one, so the sparse model's input weights compose with residual-stream elements and its output weights compose with later layers, with the nonlinearity in between. The stated payoff is that the linear path-expansion analysis from the transformer-circuits framework then extends to cover MLP features as well as heads and logits — i.e. the explanation becomes composable, which is what a predictive account needs. Elsewhere understanding is field notes ("biologist's field notes" is their own phrase) and, for MNIST, a template-matching reading of digit features.

## How they judged success

Attention superposition: geometric resemblance to the toy-model pattern, judged from a mixing-matrix scatter plot; the crucial ablation is OV sparsity — dense OV circuits produce no superposition. MNIST: interpretability by inspection, whether each feature's logit weights point at the correct class, presence of multiple templates per digit, and the planned but absent elbow in reconstruction error versus sparsity or feature count; the negative result is that low reconstruction error required either a dictionary comparable in size to the dataset or many simultaneously active features. 8-layer: inspection with confidence explicitly rated below the detailed features in the paper. Ghost Grads: number of dead features, and performance at equal live-feature count versus neuron resampling, plus the cost accounting (roughly 2× compute for the autoencoder). Counterexamples: argument plus a worked example, checked by making sure no other unit disambiguates the case and by looking at what the output layer then predicts. Other-groups section: no independent replication attempted, though one item is singled out as the first case of a reverse-engineered algorithm later proved optimal.

## What they found

Attention superposition forms in the toy model but only when the OV circuit is sparse; the mixing-matrix geometry is described as strongly reminiscent of the toy superposition work. On MNIST the dictionaries did yield interpretable features that mapped cleanly to individual digits — many features per digit, each a slightly different way of drawing it, which is read as template matching — with matching class logits downstream, and notably *not* the composable edge/line/loop features a convolutional model would have in early layers. But there was no evidence these were "true" features: no elbow appeared, and good reconstruction needed either a dictionary the size of the dataset or very dense activation. On the 8-layer model, last-layer features resemble the one-layer ones (simple, often single-token, predicting plausible next tokens), earlier layers hold more abstract content such as strongly valenced text, and layers 4–6 are believed to carry the most high-level features — flagged as an impression without a careful census, and consistent with what the SoLU work found about middle layers. Safety-relevant examples are listed per layer and read convincingly as a local-code zoo: single-token units for "I" in romance prose, "was" describing a person's state, a "." marking a sharp contextual transition, a comma between an if-clause and its result, "?" in simple social-reasoning questions, a violence detector, body language, and in layer 7 LaTeX, Hebrew, Swedish, repeated letters, spam and base62. At billions of parameters the preliminary features are strikingly *multilingual* — one unit firing on the notion of uniqueness across English, Spanish, Portuguese, Russian and German phrasing, others for "two", "sad", time quantifications, whole/entire, why-questions, future needs, and court documents.

Ghost Grads produces very few (often zero) dead features and matches or beats neuron resampling at equal live-feature count. Its justification is a scaling argument: the dead fraction grows with autoencoder size and very large SAEs can lose more than half their units even with resampling, so a smaller dictionary with Ghost Grads is often cheaper than a larger one without. **A correction the page itself carries at the top:** their view on Ghost Grads changed in the March 2024 update, so this is not their final position. The counterexamples: in a sparse-regularised one-layer model, a very sparsely active neuron responds to three unrelated input facets — a contraction split, an "and /" split, and a quantifier — with no other unit disambiguating, yet the model still predicts correctly because the residual stream running alongside supplies the missing context; the authors call this joint superposition between an MLP neuron and the residual stream and note that the residual has no privileged basis, so making it the target is a worse problem than the one it solves. In the "cut" residual variant where one neuron alone fires, a single unit encodes personal pronouns at low magnitude and the "m" of "diplomacy" at high magnitude, and the output layer decodes the facet from magnitude — with a worked softmax construction showing binary features can be stacked as distinct values on one axis. This contradicts the linear-representation hypothesis and is offered as the reason to stop trying to build monosemantic models by architecture.

## Limits and open problems they name

No metric for dictionary quality anywhere; the MNIST negative result is presented as unresolved (is it superposition, or are small models pathological and lacking crisp abstractions?). The 8-layer feature labels are unverified inspection, from a non-random, safety-biased sample. The Ghost Grads recipe is superseded by a later update on the same page, and costs 2× compute. The attention-superposition result is a toy with hand-designed sparsity, not a demonstration in a real model — the open problem of whether it is even the right frame for real attention layers is left open. Whether features are "true" features or a "pragmatically useful abstraction" — the strong versus weak superposition hypothesis — is left as the central open question. The residual-stream-target alternative is dismissed on structural grounds rather than measured. The attention variant of "predicting future activations" is early-stage and they do not know the right sparsity regulariser for it.

## What we take from it

**A mechanism, not just a fix: Ghost Grads.** The idea worth stealing is the general trick of reviving a dead component by adding a loss term that pits it against a residual the rest of the system fails to explain, with the magnitude of its output deliberately held below the residual so that the only downhill direction is toward life. That transfers directly to any sparse readout we train, and their scaling argument (dead fraction rises with width) says it matters more the more ambitious our dictionary is. **A warning that should shape our whole agenda:** the two counterexamples say that suppressing superposition can push a model toward representations that are harder to read — cross-layer superposition, where a unit's meaning depends on a residual stream with no privileged basis, and magnitude-as-symbol, where one axis carries several discrete features. Any interpretability intervention of ours that makes units "cleaner" must be checked against both failure modes rather than assuming the gain. **A negative result we should not skip:** MNIST classifiers are small, ancient and still have no mechanistic account, and dictionary learning there gave interpretable-but-unverified templates with no elbow and no evidence of completeness. That is a useful floor for how much "interpretable" is worth on its own. **A composability target:** the "predicting future activations" variants point at the thing we actually want — a sparse surrogate for a slice of computation whose weights compose with the residual stream the way real weights do, so the path-expansion analysis applies. That is a concrete design requirement for a predictive white-box model. **A dataset of confirmed phenomenon:** the layer-by-layer table is one of the best available lists of what actually recurs in mid-stack MLPs at frontier scale — multilingual concept units rather than language-specific ones — which is a constraint on any claim that abstraction is organised by language or by surface form.
