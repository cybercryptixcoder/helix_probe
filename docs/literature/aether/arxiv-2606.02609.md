## What they wanted, and why
Activation Oracles (AOs) are fine-tuned models given a residual-stream activation plus a free-text question, asked to describe in natural language what the target model is computing there. They are the main entry in the "scalable end-to-end interpretability" family: instead of hand-built dictionaries, train a model whose job is to map internals to English. Existing AOs hallucinate, answer vaguely and in unfalsifiable generalities, and are hard to evaluate because the oracle can succeed by guessing the surrounding text instead of reading the activation (the text-inversion confound). This paper reworks the training recipe to reduce hallucination and vagueness, and introduces a benchmark that tries to measure what a *good* oracle should do while resisting text inversion.

## What they studied
AOs trained on chain-of-thought-generating target models (Qwen3-8B is used for the injection comparison), interpreting activation batches from a single or several layers. Unit of analysis: an activation vector (a slice of the residual stream at one token position over 1-5 contiguous layers), summarised as text. The benchmark AObench reuses "model organisms" from the earlier Activation Oracles paper.

## How they did it
Four changes are each added one at a time on top of the Karvonen et al. recipe, trained for 50M tokens with matched learning rates, over several seeds:
1. A new conversational (question, answer) dataset. LatentQA was judged low quality — complex prompts, questions not answerable from the activations, generated by an outdated model — which incentivises vagueness and guessing. Instead a separate LLM (Sonnet 4.6) is shown the target model's own chain-of-thought and asked to split it into a prefix and a suffix, then write a question about the suffix that is *hard to answer from the prefix text* (anti-text-inversion) but *plausibly answerable from the prefix activations* (solvability).
2. Layer choice: instead of randomly sampling 25/50/75% depth, sweep layers and also feed 5 contiguous layers.
3. Training corpus for the unsupervised past/future-lens objective: swap pre-training web text (FineWeb) for on-policy chain-of-thought rollouts generated by the target model.
4. Injection formula: keep additive, norm-matched injection after the second layer, and raise injection strength.
Evaluation is chance-adjusted AObench score plus separate hallucination and vagueness scores, and the ablation ladder is the main evidence.

## What counts as an explanation here
A natural-language description of the activation, produced by a trained model, intended to be specific and falsifiable. It is partial and unverifiable by construction — the oracle's output is a hypothesis about the computation that a human must check. The key methodological move is that "good explanation" is operationalised as a benchmark score over tasks designed so that the answer cannot be read off the surrounding text.

## How they judged success
AObench: a suite of tasks targeting what an ideal oracle should do (describe reasoning, answer specific questions about the model's planned continuation, support model diffing), reporting a chance-adjusted score relative to a chance baseline, plus hallucination rate (specific but unsupported claims) and vagueness (generic, unfalsifiable claims). Controlled ablation ladder with 95% CIs over seeds; LLM-judge scoring with prompt-sensitivity analysis in the appendix; qualitative testing. Comparison point: narrow post-training on the hard CoT-interpretation tasks of Ivanova et al. never beat a simple linear probe.

## What they found
Full recipe raised chance-adjusted AObench score from +0.244 to +0.435. Largest single step: the conversational dataset swap, +0.244 to +0.310. On-policy lens data added about +0.03; layer sweep layer 22 gave +0.025 over layer 18 and five contiguous layers +0.05; a 2x injection-strength tweak added a final small gain but changed hallucination noticeably (79% to 85% on their measure). Hallucination went from 68.8% to 84.6% on the task in their decomposition, with an initial apparent rise attributed to the model making more specific claims (so more opportunities to count as hallucination). Vagueness improved from 0.076 to 0.205 chance-adjusted. Feeding more layers help most on model-diffing tasks. Narrow post-training on hard CoT tasks did not exceed linear probes.

## Limits and open problems they name
Checkpoints are slightly undertrained (50M vs 65M tokens) though recipe ordering is stable; the on-policy ablation is confounded because the conversational dataset is also built from policy rollouts; AOs remain hard to evaluate — LLM judges are noisy on open-ended output and several metrics move with prompt phrasing. AOs still hallucinate often, though supplying more layers helps and resampling gives an uncertainty estimate. In many settings reading the chain-of-thought directly yields the same insight. Uncertainty quantification is preliminary.

## What we take from it
Measure/framing: the *text-inversion control is the central design idea* — any "model explains itself from internals" test must ensure the answer cannot be derived from the visible context, otherwise the metric measures reconstruction. This applies directly to how we evaluate any predictive read of hidden state. Measure: separate hallucination and vagueness axes; a chance-adjusted score rather than raw accuracy; an ablation ladder with seed CIs as the reporting format. Method: on-policy data and mid-depth (about 62-65%) layers as the default place to look for the most informative activations, and multi-layer slices for tasks about change over depth. Warning: recursive-judge evaluation is soft; any end-to-end reader we build needs a task-grounded benchmark (behaviour prediction) rather than prose quality. Opportunity: the "solvable but not inferable from context" property is a template for constructing our own evaluation sets.
