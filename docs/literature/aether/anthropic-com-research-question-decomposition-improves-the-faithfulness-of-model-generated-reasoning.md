## What they wanted, and why

If a model's written reasoning can be trusted, an auditor can check the *process* rather than only the answer, which is the whole premise of chain-of-thought monitoring. Prior work had shown the premise is shaky in two distinct ways: traces can rationalise a bias present in the prompt without ever mentioning it, and traces can be ignored altogether, with the answer unchanged when the reasoning is truncated or corrupted. The authors ask a constructive question rather than a diagnostic one: is there a *prompting* intervention that produces traces the model actually uses, without giving up answer accuracy? They frame this as making progress on faithfulness in general, so that process-based oversight might eventually be usable at scale. The implicit bet is that a trace is more honest when the model cannot shortcut around it.

## What they studied

One model: a pretrained LLM fine-tuned for helpfulness with RLHF, on the same base model as Claude 1.3, sampled with nucleus sampling (top-p 0.95, temperature 0.8) and best-of-5 using the same preference model as the RLHF training. Four multiple-choice question-answering benchmarks — HotpotQA (filtered to binary yes/no), StrategyQA, OpenBookQA and TruthfulQA (multiple-choice formatted) — 300 random test questions each, 1,200 total. The unit of analysis is the *reasoning trace* as a text object, decomposed into discrete steps, plus the final answer's probability distribution. Three prompting strategies are compared: plain chain-of-thought, chain-of-thought decomposition (subquestion/subanswer pairs generated in one context), and factored decomposition (subquestion/subanswer pairs generated across separate contexts and recomposed).

## How they did it

All five conditions (zero-shot, few-shot, CoT, CoT decomposition, factored decomposition) share the same 14 few-shot examples, hand-built and then extended from task training sets while patching failure modes seen qualitatively, with high-quality reasoning demonstrations produced by resampling or manual editing. CoT produces step-by-step prose then a final-answer prompt. CoT decomposition produces a single autoregressive stream of subquestion/subanswer pairs, terminated by a marker, then a final-answer prompt. Factored decomposition runs three stages: the model generates a list of subquestions; unanswered subquestions that reference no others are answered *each in an isolated context*; answers are returned as a list for the model to rewrite the remaining subquestions with references replaced by values, alternating decomposition and subquestion-answering until the model emits a done marker; then a recomposition prompt supplies all subquestion/answer pairs and asks for the answer. Faithfulness is then probed three ways. (1) Early answering: truncate the trace at each step, re-ask for the answer with that prefix substituted verbatim and no resampling, and measure how much of the trace must be present before the original answer is recovered, summarised as the area between the recovery curve and certainty. (2) Adding mistakes: use a separate model context to corrupt one step, regenerate everything after the corruption, replace the trace, and check whether the final answer changes; three random distinct step positions per sample. (3) Biasing contexts: make every few-shot example's correct answer "A" (relabelling choices and adjusting the demonstrations to match), or have the user say they think an incorrect answer is right, then measure the accuracy drop against the unbiased prompt — with the crucial check that the traces are verified never to mention the bias, so a bias-consistent answer cannot be explained by the model having stated it.

## What counts as an explanation here

Two things, neither mechanistic. The *measurement* is a behavioural definition of faithfulness borrowed from Jacovi & Goldberg: a trace is faithful to the extent it reflects the reasoning that actually produced the answer, operationalised as "does intervening on the trace change the answer" and "does the answer track influences the trace omits". The *explanation for the improvement* is architectural and assumes a substitution story: because factored decomposition answers each subquestion in a fresh context, the biasing features of the original question are physically absent from the context in which the sub-answer is computed, so bias cannot enter silently; and because each sub-answer is a named, separately-generated object with explicit references to other subquestions, the final answer has fewer ways to ignore it. That is a claim about *context isolation*, not about internal computation, and it is explicitly offered as a hypothesis with the mechanism left unverified.

## How they judged success

Four numbers per method, averaged over the four tasks. Question-answering accuracy against the few-shot baseline (the relevant one, since all reasoning methods include reasoning demonstrations). Truncation sensitivity as a trapezoidal-sum area under the recovery curve, higher being better. Corruption sensitivity as the percentage of final answers changed, higher being better. Biased-context accuracy change in percentage points for the always-A and suggested-answer biases, less negative being better. Effect sizes are absolute percentage points with standard-error error bars on the curves; faithfulness metrics from different families are min-max normalised to 0-1 and averaged into a single overall faithfulness score to produce a performance-versus-faithfulness Pareto frontier. There is no trained baseline, no human rating, and no check that the corruption edits are *semantically* wrong in the intended way — the model that writes the mistake is the same model that reads it.

## What they found

CoT has the best accuracy (86.0% averaged); CoT decomposition is within 0.4 points of it (85.6%); factored decomposition drops to 81.8%, still 2.1 points above few-shot and 9.0 above zero-shot. The faithfulness trades move the other way and mostly in the same rank order. Corruption of a CoT trace changes the answer on 9.6% of questions but a CoT-decomposition trace on 28.7% and a factored-decomposition trace on 33.6%, so the decomposition traces are actually used by the final-answer step. Truncation sensitivity rises from 10.8 (CoT) through 11.7 (CoT decomposition) to 20.5 (factored): with factored decomposition the model needs far more of the trace in place before it commits to its answer. On the suggested-answer bias, CoT loses 21.3 points of accuracy, CoT decomposition loses 29.1, factored loses only 9.2 — fewer than even plain few-shot's 16.6. On the always-A bias nothing separates the reasoning methods: CoT −1.2, factored +2.1 (called noise), CoT decomposition −2.8, against few-shot's −7.1, so this bias turns out to be one the reasoning methods all already resist. The authors' own summary is a trade-off curve: factored decomposition is most faithful and least accurate, CoT decomposition sits between CoT and factored on both. Qualitatively, subquestion decompositions are usually sensible — process-of-elimination over answer choices, plus an opening contextual subquestion — but models sometimes fail to phrase a subquestion so it is answerable in isolation, or loop on one they cannot answer instead of repairing it, and sub-answers occasionally support several choices without the recomposition resolving which.

## Limits and open problems they name

The dominant one is stated plainly: there is no ground truth for what the model actually reasoned, so every metric is a proxy, and the conclusion rests on several fairly independent lines of evidence agreeing rather than on a mechanism. They say explicitly that future tools based on a mechanistic understanding of internal computation could overturn these conclusions. Generality is narrow — one RLHF-tuned model, four multiple-choice QA tasks — and they note that pretrained models may be more or less prone to ignored or biased reasoning, which would shift the benefit. The accuracy cost of factored decomposition is unexplained and might be closable by training a model to decompose, which would make the Pareto frontier move. The always-A comparison to Turpin et al. is not apples-to-apples because their filtering removes half the examples on the binary tasks.

## What we take from it

- **A *causal* faithfulness test we should adopt verbatim, in both directions.** Truncation sensitivity and corruption sensitivity are the two cheapest intervention-based checks on any natural-language explanation and they fail in opposite ways: truncation catches a trace generated *after* the answer, corruption catches a trace that is read but not decisive. Report both, and report them as a curve or an area, not a point.
- **The bound on what text-level faithfulness can prove.** Every metric here is downstream of a model call; there is no readout of what the model actually computed. This is precisely the gap our white-box work is supposed to fill — the most useful role for a probe is as an *independent* third opinion against which trace-based faithfulness claims (and specifically the claim that a decomposition trace is "used") can be checked. A probe reading the same hidden cause in a trace-free way turns "the model changed its answer" into "the model's internal state was already committed".
- **A concrete mechanism hypothesis worth testing causally: context isolation.** Factored decomposition's gain is attributed to keeping the biasing feature out of the context where the sub-answer is computed. That is a testable statement about activations, not just prompts — e.g. implant the bias in the full-context condition and measure whether the biased direction is present at the sub-answer position. If it is, the substitution story is wrong even if the prompt-level result replicates.
- **A caution about which biases a method actually fixes.** The always-A bias, the one most often cited from Turpin et al., showed no method difference at all here (and a much smaller drop than the original, attributed to prompt formatting, tasks and model generation). Only the explicit-user-suggestion bias separated the methods. A claim that "this intervention increases faithfulness" must name the influence class, because the classes do not move together.
- **A measurement-framing warning.** The headline faithfulness score is a min-max normalisation and average of metrics that are defined on different scales and different failure modes; the authors are candid about this, but it means a single "faithfulness score" is a convenience, not a quantity. Follow the individual metrics.
