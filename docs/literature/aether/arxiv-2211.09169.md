# Engineering Monosemanticity in Toy Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2211.09169
- **Authors / organisation:** Adam S. Jermyn, Nicholas Schiefer, Evan Hubinger; Anthropic
- **Date:** 2022-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.09169
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, body + Appendix A training table; the v2 HTML route returned no document, so v1 was used)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

If single neurons map onto single human-named features, circuits become readable; if a neuron answers to several unrelated inputs, everything built on top of that neuron is guesswork. The authors' motivation is that monosemanticity may be partly an *engineering* choice rather than a fact about networks — an earlier pair of posts had shown that architecture moves the number (softmax linear units there, capacity there) — so the question is whether we can deliberately select for it in a controlled setting. The toy setting lets them know the ground truth feature set, which is what makes "is this neuron about one feature" measurable rather than a judgement call. A step toward models whose internals can be read off a neuron list, and a probe into how much of legibility is a property of the training path rather than of the task.

## What they studied

A deliberately minimal two-layer model: a linear map from a d-dimensional input to k nonlinear units with a per-unit bias, a pointwise nonlinearity (normally ReLU, also GeLU), then a linear read-out with no bias, over N = 512 possible features with average per-feature firing rate ε (default 1/64 — about 8 features per sample), input dimension d = 64 by default, and k = 1024 units unless varied. Features are sampled independently, each zero with probability 1 − εᵢ and otherwise uniform on [0,1]; frequency distributions are either uniform or a power law with exponent −1.1, standing in for Zipf. Three tasks: recover the full feature vector from a lossy random projection into d dimensions (feature decoder); recover a *different* fixed random projection of the same features (re-projector); and recover the element-wise absolute difference of two feature vectors (absolute-value calculator). Unit of analysis: individual neurons, plus their biases and the factored linear maps into and out of the positive-bias subpopulation.

## How they did it

The measurement is a per-neuron "monosemanticity" ratio: activation on the feature it responds to most, divided by the sum of non-negative activations over *all* single-feature unit-strength inputs — i.e. one feature's share of the neuron's total response — with a tiny constant to stop unreachable neurons blowing up. A neuron counts as monosemantic above 0.999. They first swept learning rate at zero initial bias and found the striking result that final loss is essentially identical across models whose monosemantic-neuron counts differ wildly, then inspected the neuron biases of those equal-loss models and found monosemantic neurons sitting at moderate negative bias and polysemantic ones at small positive bias. From that correlation they built an intervention on the training path rather than on the objective: offset all initial biases so the mean starts near −1 (comfortably larger than the typical pre-activation scale, ≈ε^½, so nothing fires and gradients vanish), then use weight decay **on the biases only** to let the negative bias relax until units begin to activate, and switch weight decay off for the final 50% of steps so the found model is a true minimum. They compared this with L1 regularisation on activations, swept the initial bias against feature frequency, swept k, swept ε across the compressed-sensing threshold Nε ≈ d, repeated on power-law frequencies, GeLU and the other two tasks, and finished with a hand mechanistic dissection of the most-legible decoder models by splitting the network into positive-bias and negative-bias halves and tracing each half's input→output map.

## What counts as an explanation here

A fully explicit algorithm for a *known* weight set: per-neuron assignment of features, plus the piecewise-linear transfer function each neuron implements, plus a factored linear map (projection × first-layer weights × second-layer weights) describing what the remaining polysemantic neurons collectively do. It is meant to be complete for the highly-monosemantic decoder models and explicitly not attempted for the polysemantic ones, where the authors say outright they do not understand the algorithm. Prediction: yes in principle — the account is a composition of explicit maps, though the paper reports no held-out prediction of outputs from the description.

## How they judged success

Two axes reported together: task loss (MSE to the ground-truth output), and the count/fraction of monosemantic neurons and monosemantic-neurons-per-feature from the ratio measure. Comparisons are always between families of models — learning-rate sweeps, bias-offset sweeps, L1-strength sweeps, k sweeps, sparsity sweeps — with the same loss axis, so "more legible at equal loss" is directly checkable. Sanity check for the measure is the full single-feature activation matrix (all k neurons × all N features, at unit strength), sorted by the ratio, in which monosemantic neurons appear as single clean cells and polysemantic ones as near-full rows. Because batches are regenerated per step, train and test loss have the same expectation, so no train/test gap correction is involved. Controls: L1 regularisation as the standard alternative; a bias-offset sweep showing the threshold behaviour at −ε^½; GeLU as a second nonlinearity; power-law as a second frequency law; and the authors' own disclaimer that some trends may reflect limited training time.

## What they found

Equal-loss, very different algorithms: at fixed loss, models across learning rates range from mostly polysemantic to mostly monosemantic, with the highest learning rates finding the legible basins; the authors weakly read these as separate basins rather than connected equal-loss paths, on the argument that post-plateau training would otherwise keep shifting legibility. Bias is the marker: monosemantic units carry moderate negative bias, polysemantic ones small positive bias, matching the earlier toy-model post. Engineering works: initialising mean bias at −1 with bias-only weight decay produced 30-50% monosemantic neurons and 0.6-1 monosemantic neurons per feature across learning rates, with the effect vanishing once the initial bias is more positive than ≈ −ε^½; the scheme matched the best L1-regularised model on both loss and legibility, and in the best run 503/512 features got exactly one dedicated neuron, 5 got two, and 4 got near-misses (0.9 < r < 0.999). Width helps: more units means more legible units and lower loss until k passes N, after which extra units mostly just buy legibility; when k > N the legible models are also *lower* loss, while for small k (256/384/512) they are slightly worse — the authors' reading is that polysemanticity is how a model copes with too few units. Sparsity as a compute saving failed: Erdős–Rényi-sparse layers at matched compute lost badly to dense ones. There is a residual positive-bias band whose width tracks d almost exactly — 65 units at d = 64, 33 at d = 32, 130 at d = 128 (using bias > 0.05 as the proxy) — one more than d, which they explain in rank terms but cannot explain the +1. Feature density sets the regime: legibility peaks around ε ≈ 0.03 (Nε ≈ d/4) and is essentially gone by Nε ≈ 2d, with no sharp transition visible in the activation matrices — a smooth slide from a monosemantic algorithm to a polysemantic one, argued against the compressed-sensing recovery threshold d = Ω(Nε log(1/ε)). The algorithm itself: a single-neuron feature spends its one ReLU kink suppressing low-amplitude inputs that are probably interference, so the high-amplitude end is stuck on a straight line; a two-neuron feature uses the second kink for a three-regime response with a better high-amplitude fit; the polysemantic band collectively contributes a tiny near-linear tilt (intercepts < 0.005, slopes 0.09-0.16, varying by < 0.01 across [0,1]) because the optimal estimate is never exactly zero under interference, and the factored map of the positive-bias population is close to a low-rank identity with singular values either ~0 or 0.99-1.03 — which is exactly why it needs about d units, and why their positive biases are pushed through the ReLU while their combined contribution is held near zero (< 0.005). Denser features shift the crossover points down (≈0.4 → ≈0.3 onset, second kink ≈0.8 → ≈0.6). On the absolute-value task the basins differ in loss (legible is better) and a negative initial bias alone is not enough — slow weight decay is also required, which they attribute to the task taking longer to learn, i.e. time scale of learning versus time scale of decay.

## Limits and open problems they name

The most polysemantic models are not understood at all, which is the majority of units in the regimes a real model would be in when units are scarce. The engineering recipe is fragile: it needs a bias-decay rate slow relative to how fast the task is learned, which the authors call the main weakness, and they sketch per-neuron decay, decay tied to weight-change magnitude, or a regulariser toward negative bias as fixes (preliminary runs of the last one worked). Sparse weights as a way to afford width did not work. They did not get clear results with softmax linear units — behaviour differed enough that they stopped. Whether these toy trends transfer to models with real data is asserted as plausible rather than shown; the tasks are all feature-recovery style, which is precisely where a negative bias is a natural denoiser.

## What we take from it

- **Method, and the single most useful idea for us:** legibility is a *loss-basin* property, so equal-loss checkpoints can implement materially different algorithms. Before claiming a readout failure means "the model has no such feature", test whether a different training path reaches a legible model at the same loss — and conversely, any white-box claim from one checkpoint needs a same-loss sibling as its control.
- **Bias sign as a cheap coordinate**: sorting units by bias recovered the legible/polysemantic partition with no probe and no labels. For our reader, per-unit bias plus weight norm is a free first-pass salience/locality feature — and it is a warning that a "polysemantic" unit may be a *deliberate* linear correction, not noise.
- **Measure, cheap to copy:** one feature's share of a unit's total response, over a systematically enumerated set of single-feature inputs. Enumerating the input basis is what makes "monosemantic" a number; we should prefer enumerable synthetic probes over sampled data for legibility metrics.
- **Framing to steal:** separate the output into a sparse-assignment part and a distributed-linear-correction part, and factor the correction's linear map (projection × W_in × W_out) to see it is a low-rank identity. A rank bound explained a structural fact (the residual polysemantic band's width ~ d) — exactly the kind of prediction a weight-side account should deliver.
- **Warning on interpretation of "distributed"**: in these models the polysemantic units are mostly a calibrator for the sparse units' confidence, so distributed activity does not imply distributed *content*. And the +1 anomaly in that band is a reminder that even a clean rank argument leaves residue.
- **Warning on regime:** the polysemantic regime (Nε ≳ d, i.e. many features active per input) is where real models live and is where this paper has nothing to say — so the corresponding insight for us is a hypothesis to test, not a result to rely on.
