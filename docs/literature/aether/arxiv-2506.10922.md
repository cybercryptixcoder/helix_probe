# Robustly Improving LLM Fairness in Realistic Settings via Interpretability

- **Key:** arxiv-2506.10922
- **Authors / organisation:** Adam Karvonen (independent); Samuel Marks (Anthropic)
- **Date:** 2025-06-12
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10922
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, main body plus Appendices A–I, including the exploratory white-box appendix and the prompt templates)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Hiring is one of the first places LLMs make decisions that change people's lives, and the published audits of that decision had a comforting shape: make two resumes that differ only in the name, ask the model to interview or not, and the measured gap between groups is near zero once you paste in an anti-discrimination instruction. The authors suspected the comfort was an artefact of how thin the audit is. Real screening happens inside a company, a location, a culture document and a competitive bar; if prompt-level mitigation only holds in the simplified setting, then everything the field measures about deployment-time fairness is measuring the wrong prompt. Their goal is therefore two-part: show that realistic context revives demographic bias in models that look clean, then show that an intervention on internal representations is more robust than anything you can say in the prompt. The larger stake is methodological — that evaluation realism, not just mitigation strength, determines which conclusions about a model survive.

## What they studied

Eight models across two access regimes: five commercial models judged behaviourally (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Flash, plus Claude 3.5 Sonnet substituted in one cell where Claude 4 Sonnet refused nearly all candidates) and four open-weights models whose activations they can edit (Gemma-2 27B, Gemma-3 12B, Gemma-3 27B, Mistral Small 24B). The task is binary resume screening — interview or not — over two prompt corpora: 116 LLM-generated scenario templates from prior work, and 111 real IT resumes — the 120 in that category minus nine longer than 12,000 characters. The unit of analysis differs by half of the paper. Behaviourally it is the prompt: which components (response format, anti-bias instruction, company context, selectivity constraint) are present. Mechanistically it is a **difference-of-means direction per layer**, one for race and one for gender, edited at every token position and every layer — i.e. a direction-level account, not a feature or circuit account.

## How they did it

A paired counterfactual design throughout: the same resume is presented with demographic signals swapped (names/pronouns for the simple setting; for the "inference" setting, a plausible alumni-network affiliation — Howard or Morehouse against Georgetown or Emory, matched by city and rough standing) and the interview rate is differenced, with McNemar's test for paired categorical data and 95% intervals pooled over the four anti-bias prompts. Four anti-bias instructions of increasing specificity (a bare legal reminder, a full legal-ramifications warning, an instruction to check hidden assumptions, and an equity framework) are each run separately and then averaged, so "prompting works" is never a single-prompt claim. Realism is added on top: company name, location and culture text scraped from the companies' own careers pages (Meta, General Motors, Palantir), plus a high-selectivity instruction requiring candidates to be roughly top-10% to get an interview. To separate "company culture contains diversity language" from "any realistic employer context does it", they re-ran the Meta condition with every diversity-related phrase deleted.

The mitigation is affine concept editing. For each attribute and each layer they take the mean activation over all token positions for the two groups, divide the difference by the element-wise standard deviation of activations (a whitening step, with an epsilon of 1e-4), and normalise to a unit direction; the target value is the midpoint between the two group centroids' projections on that direction. At inference, every activation's projection on each of the two directions is shifted to that midpoint, at all layers and all token positions. Two choices carry the argument: the directions are extracted from the **synthetic** template corpus while evaluation happens on **real resumes and realistic context** (a generalisation test borrowed from earlier work that found bias subspaces do not transfer across templates), and the edit is affine rather than a zero-ablation because plain zero-ablation destroyed Gemma-3's ability to produce valid outputs at all. Capability is tracked with MMLU, and side-effects on non-biased decisions with mean acceptance rate.

## What counts as an explanation here

The explanation is a **direction plus a target value** — a linear representation claim of the same family as refusal directions and steering vectors, with an explicit "and here is what removing its projection does" attached. It is deliberately partial and behaviour-specific: the authors say outright that they are neutralising the influence of demographic attributes on decisions, not erasing the concepts, and that models may still know stereotypical associations (their example is a nurse/caregiving link). Its predictive content is narrow but real: if the direction is the channel through which the demographic signal reaches the decision, then editing it should reduce bias in contexts the direction was never fitted on — including contexts where the attribute was never stated, only inferable. That last prediction is the paper's strongest evidence and the reason the entry is relevance 3: an internal artefact is credited because it generalises beyond the surface form used to build it.

## How they judged success

Outcome bias in interview rates per model × prompt condition, averaged over the four anti-bias statements, with McNemar significance and pooled 95% intervals; the *direction* of bias and its *stability across contexts* as a secondary pattern; MMLU before/after as the capability floor; change in mean acceptance rate in the unbiased setting as the "did we perturb unrelated decisions" check; and, qualitatively, a chain-of-thought audit. The CoT audit is unusually strong evidence because it is run on counterfactuals: for the 63 resumes where Claude 4 Sonnet's decision flipped on the demographic signal, the model's stated reasoning was searched — by hand and by GPT-4o — for any mention of race or gender. A method that suppresses bias *and* leaves the trace silent is doing something a prompt cannot, and the paper reads the silence both ways: as a mitigation success and as an admission that CoT monitoring will not catch this class of behaviour.

## What they found

- **Prompting works in the thin setting and fails in the realistic one.** In the simple context every model sits under 2% bias with anti-bias instructions. Adding company name, location and culture text pushes the open-weights models into a substantial bias despite identical instructions; Mistral Small 24B moves from 2% to 11% in favour of Black candidates. Across all model/context/instruction combinations, interview-rate differences reach 15%.
- **The frontier models break too, and the break depends on format.** With Metacontext and plain yes/no answers, GPT-4o, Claude and Gemini all show 6–9% racial bias. Switching to chain-of-thought removes it — until the top-10% selectivity constraint is added, at which point it returns. The paper's framing is that bias emergence is an interaction of prompt components nobody can predict from the pieces, which is why prompt engineering cannot be the fix.
- **Bias has a consistent sign.** Wherever it appeared, it favoured Black over White and female over male candidates, across three companies and both evaluation formats, and it survived deletion of the diversity language from Meta's culture text (a filtered-Meta run and a Palantir/Texas run both reproduce it). So the "company values push the model left" explanation is tested and rejected; the driver is the realism itself, or something correlated with it the authors did not isolate.
- **The CoT is empty of the cause.** Zero instances of the justification mentioning race or gender were found, in any model, including in the 63 direct counterfactuals; the authors read this as domain-dependent unfaithfulness (screening invites post-hoc rationalisation where a hard reasoning task would not) rather than as evidence of fairness.
- **Affine concept editing holds where prompting does not.** Across the realistic conditions bias falls to typically under 1% and always under 2.5%, including the college-affiliation setting where race was never mentioned and the GM-plus-selectivity condition where induced bias reached 7.6%. MMLU cost: pragmatic — Mistral Small 24B −0.10%, Gemma-2 27B −0.48%, Gemma-3 27B −1.11%, Gemma-3 12B −3.71% — and mean acceptance rate in the unbiased setting moves by at most 1.8%.
- **Model-side fragility of the edit.** Zero-ablation was adequate for Gemma-2 27B and Mistral Small 24B but wrecked Gemma-3, which the authors link (citing others) to Gemma-3's unusually high activation norms; whitening the directions was needed to keep MMLU intact there. The same intervention family costs 3.7 points on the smaller Gemma-3 and 0.1 on Mistral, which by their own account means technique transfer is model-dependent.
- **The exploratory white-box appendix (B) is the part most relevant to us.** Attribution patching from a yes/no logit difference onto SAE features at roughly 25% depth produced a suggestive ordering: models whose top features by indirect effect were more race- or gender-related in the *clean* evaluation went on to show larger bias once realism was added. In chain-of-thought conditions, patching backwards from a **logit-lens** readout at roughly 70% depth surfaced demographic features better than patching from the output logits. The authors refuse to claim it — small model sample, enough analytic freedom to cherry-pick, and attempts to predict *which* anti-bias instruction would fail did not replicate — but the conjecture is exactly ours: latent susceptibility readable in activations before it is visible in behaviour.

## Limits and open problems they name

Binary race (Black/White) and gender (male/female) only; no intersectional, age, disability, pregnancy or other protected axes, no non-binary identities. The evaluations are adaptations of two existing frameworks and lack job descriptions, full pipelines, and emerging surfaces such as social-media screening. The college-affiliation manipulation confounds race with prestige, region and academic focus (their defence is that race-derived directions built from a different dataset reduce the effect there). The intervention neutralises decision-relevant influence but does not erase concepts, so success is not evidence of a concept-free model. The white-box exploratory analysis is explicitly unfinished. And the central positive result has no matched-random-direction control of the kind their own citations use: there is no reported arm where an equally large edit along a random or unrelated direction is applied, which is what separates "we removed the bias channel" from "we perturbed the model enough that its decision surface flattened".

## What we take from it

- **A method we can lift almost unchanged.** Whitened difference-of-means direction **plus** centroid-midpoint clamping is a template for a five-line, training-free intervention on any behaviour we can build two groups for, and the whitening is the non-obvious ingredient — it is what let them keep MMLU while zero-ablation broke Gemma-3. Compare with the plain unit-norm difference-in-means recipe in the rest of this folder: same extraction, different edit, materially different damage profile.
- **A measure worth stealing: prediction of a bias we never conditioned on.** The college-affiliation test is a clean instance of a read-out built on one surface form being scored on a different one, and the internal artefact beating the prompted model there is a real prediction-under-shift result rather than a fit.
- **A warning about single-prompt evaluations, aimed directly at our own protocol.** Sixteen cells averaged or discarded a single anti-bias instruction would have produced different conclusions, and the CoT-vs-binary flip says the response format is as large an experimental variable as the intervention. Any probe-versus-baseline comparison we run on prompt-conditional behaviour has to cross format and instruction, or it is reporting one cell of a 4×2 grid.
- **A prediction-shaped null we should chase.** Their appendix is the closest published thing to "the model's susceptibility is visible before the behaviour": a label-free ranking of features by indirect effect, read at two depths with two attribution targets, weakly predicting downstream bias magnitude, plus the observation that mid-to-late-depth logit-lens patching exposes suppressed demographic computation that output-side patching misses. It is under-powered and they say so, but it is precisely the shape of the claim our white-box model is supposed to make — and it is a claim about internal state predicting behaviour under an intervention the read-out never saw.
- **A supporting data point for the pooling/format lesson already in this folder.** Same models, same resumes, same fairness question: yes/no and chain-of-thought give opposite answers. This belongs next to the feature-versus-raw classifier result where max-pooling and last-token pooling flipped the winner.
