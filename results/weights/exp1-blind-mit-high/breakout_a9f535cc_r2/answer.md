Observations: I probed the network by flipping individual input bits from a baseline empty grid and measuring the change in action logits. The most striking structure is in channel 0 (agent): its influence is negligible for rows 1–7, but enormous on row 8 (bottom playable row). For example, the sum of absolute logit changes for agent cells on row 8 are 39.76, 17.40, 7.36, 3.07, 14.63, 7.55, 14.81, 46.26 across columns 1–8, whereas every cell in rows 1–7 has influence below 3.5. This means the network only expects the agent to appear on the bottom row. Channel 1 (moving objects) has large influence in rows 2–6 (e.g., row 3: 145.07, 176.05, 174.74, 147.38, 85.67, 146.63, 133.45, 118.76), moderate influence in row 7, and near-zero influence in row 8 (0.46, 1.05, 0.81, 1.35, 0.13, 2.70, 0.57, 2.12). Thus moving objects are confined to the middle rows and never reach the bottom row. Channel 2 (reward targets) has weak influence overall, with some structure in the top rows (e.g., row 3 has 13.15, 12.17, 19.47, 8.16, 18.63, 4.07, 10.47, 15.23). Channel 3 (obstacles) is nearly flat except for the border walls. Channel 4 (previous frame) is highly influential across rows 3–7 (e.g., row 5: 106.11, 16.91, 47.00, 4.23, 34.50, 45.09, 46.68, 139.48), indicating the network relies on motion history. The output layer biases, inferred from the baseline (agent at (8,4), no moving objects, no previous frame), strongly favour no-op (logit -10.28) over up (-13.83), down (-12.22), left (-14.49), right (-23.58). When a moving object is placed in the middle, the network often outputs left regardless of the agent’s column, suggesting a learned default to move the bottom paddle leftward when a ball is present.

Reasoning: The agent’s confinement to the bottom row rules out games where the player moves vertically through the grid, such as Frogger, Freeway, Snake, and Pac-Man. The moving objects being restricted to the middle rows and never reaching the bottom row rules out a falling-object catcher (where objects would reach the bottom) and is consistent with a ball bouncing above a paddle. The weak reward-channel influence fits a game where the reward targets (bricks) are static and the policy mainly needs to track the moving ball. The strong reliance on the previous frame is exactly what is needed to estimate the ball’s velocity and predict its trajectory. The action set includes up/down, which are not useful for a bottom paddle; however, in a custom grid-world implementation the action space may be fixed to five actions even if some are invalid. The baseline preference for no-op when no ball is present is appropriate for Breakout (wait for the ball), whereas in Freeway the agent would want to move up to cross. The strong left bias when a ball is present may be a training artifact (e.g., the ball often approached from the right) or a local optimum. Overall, the weight structure—agent only on the bottom row, ball in the middle, bricks at the top, heavy use of previous frame—points most strongly to Breakout.
